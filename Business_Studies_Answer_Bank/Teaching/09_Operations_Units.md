# Business operations — checked teaching units

**Scope:** clauses 5.1.1–5.4.1 (7 clauses). Authority: Pearson, *International GCSE Business Specification*, Issue 2 (February 2026), printed p.19. Each practice task below is constructed and unscored unless an official item is specifically identified; none of these constructed examples is presented as a Pearson question.

## 5.1.1 — Internal and external economies of scale

**Requirement (§5.1.1, p.19):** internal economies (average total cost falls as output increases) and external economies (average total cost falls because of external factors).

**Teach it.** Economies of scale are cost advantages that lower average total cost as a business/industry’s scale grows. Internal economies arise within the firm: purchasing discounts, specialist management, finance access, technical efficiencies or spreading fixed costs can lower cost per unit, if savings exceed added costs. External economies arise from outside the firm as an industry/region develops, such as shared infrastructure, a deeper skilled-labour pool or nearby specialist suppliers; several firms may benefit. Growth alone does not guarantee either.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A furniture factory raises output from 10,000 to 15,000 units while rent stays £120,000 and material cost per unit falls after a bulk contract. Separately, a regional furniture cluster gains a specialist training college. Identify one internal and one external economy and explain an average-cost effect.

**Answer / rationale.** Bulk purchasing and spreading unchanged rent over more units are internal; rent per unit falls from £12 to £8 before other costs. A specialist regional college is an external economy if it improves worker availability/skills and reduces recruitment/training cost for firms in the cluster. Calculate: £120,000/10,000=£12; £120,000/15,000=£8 per unit. Do not call all lower costs economies of scale without linking them to scale. Spec p.19; constructed, recalculated.

## 5.1.2 — Diseconomies of scale and limits to growth

**Requirement (§5.1.2, p.19):** diseconomies of scale; limits of growth.

**Teach it.** Diseconomies occur when expansion raises average total cost, for example through slower communication, coordination failures, managerial overload, duplicated work or reduced motivation. They can offset economies and limit efficient growth. The issue is not that “large is always bad”: systems, delegation and technology may manage complexity, but such changes also cost money and may not work. Compare cost/quality/productivity before and after expansion.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A manufacturer doubles output by adding distant sites. Defect rates and late deliveries rise; supervisors spend more time resolving inconsistent instructions. Explain why this may be diseconomies and give two possible responses.

**Answer / rationale.** Coordination/communication costs and defects may raise cost per acceptable unit despite higher volume—evidence of diseconomies. Standardised systems/training, local decision authority with clear controls, improved logistics or slower staged expansion could help. Evaluate cost and effectiveness; simply adding more managers may increase overhead without fixing root causes. Spec p.19; constructed, unscored.

## 5.2.1 — Production types, capital/labour intensity and productivity

**Requirement (§5.2.1, p.19):** job, batch and flow production; labour-/capital-intensive production; impacts of production types; calculate productivity and impacts of improvement.

**Teach it.** Job production makes a one-off/custom item; batch production makes a group of identical products before switching; flow production moves standardised goods continuously through stages. Job offers customisation but can be slow/costly; batch balances variety/scale but needs changeovers/stock; flow can produce high volume consistently but requires investment and may be inflexible. Labour-intensive production uses relatively more workers; capital-intensive uses relatively more machinery/equipment. Productivity = output ÷ an input over a stated period, e.g. units per labour hour; higher measured productivity does not automatically mean higher quality or profit.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A bakery chooses production for custom wedding cakes, 200 identical loaves for a school, and 10,000 standard rolls daily. State a likely method for each. A shift produces 480 acceptable packs in 24 labour-hours; after training it produces 540 acceptable packs in the same labour-hours. Calculate productivity before/after and percentage improvement; interpret one possible benefit and caveat.

**Answer / recalculation.** Wedding cakes: job; school loaves: batch; high-volume standard rolls: flow may fit if demand/capacity justify it. Before: 480/24 = **20 packs per labour-hour**. After: 540/24 = **22.5 packs per labour-hour**. Increase = 2.5/20×100 = **12.5%**. This may reduce labour cost per pack or raise throughput; check quality, safety, demand and whether the 24 hours include comparable labour. Do not report “12.5 packs” or omit the time unit. Spec p.19; all methods and calculation constructed/unscored.

## 5.2.2 — Lean production, JIT, Kaizen and effective resource use

**Requirement (§5.2.2, p.19):** JIT; Kaizen; importance of effective resource use.

**Teach it.** Lean production seeks to remove activities/resources that do not add customer value while protecting quality and reliable delivery. Just-in-time (JIT) receives materials close to when needed, reducing inventory/holding costs; it depends on accurate schedules and reliable suppliers and can make disruption more damaging. Kaizen is continuous, incremental improvement involving workers identifying and testing better methods. Effective resource use can reduce waste of materials, time, energy, labour and capital, but “minimum stock” or “faster” is not efficient if it causes stoppages, defects or unsafe work.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A phone assembler holds six weeks of components and has frequent obsolete stock; deliveries from its single overseas supplier are sometimes delayed. Recommend how JIT and Kaizen could help and identify a safeguard.

**Answer / rationale.** More frequent smaller deliveries may reduce holding/obsolescence costs; Kaizen teams can map delays, reduce changeover/waste and improve scheduling. Safeguards: qualify backup suppliers, monitor lead times, keep risk-based buffer stock for critical parts and test changes before rollout. JIT is not “no inventory” regardless of risk. Spec p.19; constructed, unscored.

## 5.2.3 — Robotics and trade-offs in production

**Requirement (§5.2.3, p.19):** robotics; balance cost, productivity, quality and flexibility.

**Teach it.** Robots can repeat precise tasks quickly, work continuously and reduce exposure to hazardous work; they require capital, integration, maintenance, programming and trained workers. They may improve productivity/consistency but be less adaptable to customised products, demand changes or frequent design changes. Assess total life-cycle cost, capacity utilisation, defect rates, safety and flexibility rather than equating automation with lower costs.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A factory makes 100,000 identical metal brackets annually and is considering a robot cell. It may also need to make short custom runs. State one potential effect on each of cost, productivity, quality and flexibility, then recommend a pilot or immediate purchase.

**Answer / rationale.** High initial/integration cost; output per labour-hour could rise if utilisation is sufficient; consistent welding may reduce defects after correct programming; custom runs may be harder/costly to reprogram than manual work. A pilot on the stable high-volume product, with measured total cost, downtime, defect rate and changeover performance, is defensible before full commitment. A full purchase may be justified by reliable demand and payback evidence. Spec p.19; constructed, unscored.

## 5.3.1 — Factors of production and changing relationships

**Requirement (§5.3.1, p.19):** enterprise, capital, land and labour relationships; difference between capital- and labour-intensive activity.

**Teach it.** Land includes natural resources/site; labour is human effort/skill; capital is man-made productive assets such as machinery/buildings; enterprise organises the other factors and bears business uncertainty. They are interdependent: a machine requires a site, workers to operate/maintain it and enterprise to coordinate investment and output. Capital-intensive activity uses relatively more equipment/capital per worker; labour-intensive activity uses relatively more labour. A firm can substitute some capital for labour but may need more skilled labour/maintenance; the appropriate mix depends on wages, finance, output, flexibility and quality.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A farm replaces hand sorting with an optical sorter on leased land. Identify all four factors and explain how this changes the capital/labour relationship and one new dependency.

**Answer / rationale.** Land: farm/site; labour: growers and machine operators/technicians; capital: optical sorter and other equipment; enterprise: owner/manager coordinating the change and risk. Sorting becomes more capital-intensive and may reduce manual sorting hours, but depends more on finance, electricity, calibration and skilled maintenance. Factors complement as well as substitute. Spec p.19; constructed, unscored.

## 5.4.1 — Quality control, TQM and competitive advantage

**Requirement (§5.4.1, p.19):** quality in goods and services; quality control; total quality management; competitive advantage.

**Teach it.** Quality means consistently meeting customer/required standards, not simply “expensive” or “luxury.” For goods it may include performance, durability and safety; services can be judged by reliability, response and customer experience. Quality control checks outputs/processes against standards to detect defects, often through inspection/testing. Total quality management (TQM) is a whole-organisation approach to preventing defects and improving quality continuously, with staff involvement and customer focus. Quality can create advantage through reputation, loyalty, lower rework/returns and differentiation, but prevention, training and testing cost money; a defect-free offer still needs demand and suitable price.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A hotel has repeated room-cleaning complaints and a food factory finds defective seals only after packing. Propose one quality-control action and one TQM action for each; explain how better quality could improve competitiveness and one cost/limitation.

**Answer / rationale.** Hotel QC: supervisor checklist/spot inspection after cleaning; TQM: train all teams, log root causes and use guest feedback in continuous process redesign. Factory QC: sample seal tests during/after packing; TQM: involve operators, maintenance and suppliers in preventing seal defects at source. Fewer failures may reduce refunds/rework and improve loyalty/reputation, differentiating the business; inspection/training/equipment changes cost money, and competitors can copy improvements. Spec p.19; constructed, unscored.
