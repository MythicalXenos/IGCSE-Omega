## SKILL-CALC — quantitative skills and calculation method

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** Northstar Appliances is a large manufacturer. Use these figures: annual revenue £12,000,000; cost of sales £8,400,000; operating expenses £2,700,000; stated net profit after all expenses £420,000; fixed costs for one product £15,000; selling price £50/unit; variable cost £30/unit. It makes 540 acceptable units in 24 labour-hours. It expects £28,000 cash inflows and £35,500 cash outflows next month; opening cash is £12,000. The exchange rate is £1=US$1.25. Calculate (a) gross profit, gross profit margin and operating profit margin, (b) net profit margin using the stated net-profit figure, (c) break-even output, (d) total revenue, total costs and profit at output of 2,000 units, (e) productivity per labour-hour, (f) next-month net cash flow and closing balance, (g) the average of monthly revenues £9,000, £12,000 and £15,000, and (h) convert a US$2,500 import bill into pounds.

**Answer / recalculation.** (a) Gross profit = £12.0m−£8.4m=**£3.6m**; GPM=£3.6m/£12m×100=**30%**. Operating profit=£3.6m−£2.7m=**£0.9m**; OPM=£0.9m/£12m×100=**7.5%**. (b) On the constructed question’s expressly supplied definition, NPM=£420,000/£12m×100=**3.5%**. (c) Contribution=£50−£30=£20/unit; BE=£15,000/£20=**750 units**. (d) At 2,000 units, total revenue=2,000×£50=**£100,000**; total costs=£15,000+(2,000×£30)=**£75,000**; profit=**£25,000**. (e) 540/24=**22.5 acceptable units per labour-hour**. (f) Net cash flow=£28,000−£35,500=**−£7,500**; closing=£12,000−£7,500=**£4,500**. (g) Mean=(£9,000+£12,000+£15,000)/3=**£12,000 per month**. (h) US$2,500÷(US$1.25/£)=**£2,000**. Method checks: distinguish GPM/OPM/NPM (profit/revenue) from markup (profit/cost); show units; retain the negative sign for a net outflow; closing balance = opening + net flow; divide, not multiply, when converting USD back to pounds. NPM’s terminology/formula omission in the specification is documented below; here net profit is given explicitly, so no hidden accounting definition is assumed.

**Checked record:** all results independently recalculated; formula/units and conversion direction checked. These constructed numbers are not Pearson figures.

### Average rate of return (ARR) — source reconciliation and worked practice

**Specification check.** Appendix 3 p.29 explicitly requires calculation and interpretation of average rate of return. Appendix 4 p.30 lists the supplied formulae but does **not** provide an ARR formula. Its formula table does provide ROCE = operating profit ÷ capital employed ×100. A text search of the locally held 4BS1 papers did not find an ARR item; that bounded search is not proof Pearson has never assessed it. Accordingly, the following is a transparent constructed example using the common simple-project convention, **average annual accounting profit ÷ original investment ×100**, with no residual value. It is not represented as a Pearson-provided formula. If a question or matching mark scheme states a different investment base or method, use that specified method.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A project requires an initial investment of £50,000 and has no residual value. Forecast accounting profits after project operating costs and depreciation are £8,000, £11,000, £12,000, £10,000 and £9,000 over five years. Using the simple method stated above, calculate ARR and assess it against the business’s 15% target.

**Answer / interpretation.** Total forecast profit = **£50,000**; average annual profit = £50,000÷5 = **£10,000 per year**. ARR = £10,000÷£50,000×100 = **20%**. On this criterion it exceeds the 15% target by **5 percentage points**, so it meets the stated return hurdle. It is an average accounting-profit ratio relative to the project’s original investment; it does not show cash-flow timing, risk or the year-by-year pattern (profits range from £8,000 to £12,000), and is not a guaranteed outcome.

**Do not confuse ARR with ROCE.** This constructed ARR uses average annual project profit and initial project outlay. Pearson Appendix 4’s ROCE formula instead uses **operating profit ÷ capital employed ×100**. ROCE analyses return on capital employed for the business; it is not the same calculation or denominator as this simple project ARR. The qualification specification requires ARR but omits its formula from Appendix 4; preserve that omission and follow item-specific instructions rather than silently calling ARR “ROCE.”

**Terminology inconsistency to preserve.** Appendix 3 p.29 names **gross profit margin and net profit margin** for calculation/interpretation. Appendix 4 p.30 supplies gross profit margin and **operating profit margin** formulae, but no net profit margin formula. This unit does not silently treat the terms as interchangeable or exclude NPM: the constructed calculation above supplies net profit explicitly and divides it by revenue; use an item’s defined net-profit figure and any item-specific MS guidance.

## SKILL-INTERPRET — read data/documents and make evidence-based implications

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** Northstar’s year-on-year results: revenue £10m→£12m; cost of sales £6.4m→£8.4m; operating expenses £2.4m→£2.7m; customer complaints 400→620; employee turnover 18%→12%. Explain two changes and make a cautious overall judgement about performance.

**Answer / rationale.** Revenue rose 20% (£2m/£10m), but gross profit stayed at £3.6m (10−6.4; 12−8.4); GPM fell from 36% to 30%. Operating profit fell from £1.2m to £0.9m, a 25% decrease, and OPM fell from 12% to 7.5%. Complaints rose 55% (220/400), while turnover fell 6 percentage points. This is mixed: revenue increased in value and employee turnover fell, but margins/profit and complaints worsened. Revenue growth alone does not show increased unit demand; price, mix or exchange-rate effects could contribute. The data do not prove why; investigate price/cost mix, product quality and complaint rate per sale, and consider the period/market before deciding. Rationale: compare like with like, calculate from the correct base, distinguish percentage-point changes from relative percentages, and avoid claiming causation from correlation.

### Appendix 3 — full interpretation practice (constructed, unscored)

**Practice.** Use the break-even chart in `04_Business_Finance_Units.md` §3.3.2 and these additional fictional data. A business reports revenue £700,000, gross profit £210,000 and net profit after all expenses £42,000. A project ARR is 20% against a 15% target (calculation above). Its next-month forecast shows total cash inflows £280,000 and total cash outflows £265,000, with opening balance £10,000. In a 200-person target-customer survey, 124 say they are interested in a new product. The firm sells £12m in a £120m market. Its price rises from £100 to £105 while unit input cost rises from £60 to £66. Interpret one result from each of: graph/chart, profitability ratios, financial/ARR/cash-flow data, market-research data, market share, and price/cost changes. Give one evidence limitation.

**Answer / interpretation.** From the chart, break-even is 1,000 units; at 1,500 units the chart shows £45,000 revenue and £39,000 total costs, hence £6,000 profit under the chart assumptions. GPM=£210,000/£700,000×100=**30%**; stated NPM=£42,000/£700,000×100=**6%** (the Appendix 3/4 terminology issue above remains). ARR at 20% clears the 15% hurdle by 5 percentage points but averages accounting profit and says nothing about timing. Forecast net cash flow=£280,000−£265,000=**£15,000**, so closing cash=£10,000+£15,000=**£25,000**. Survey interest=124/200×100=**62% of this sample**, not guaranteed purchases or population-wide demand. Market share=£12m/£120m×100=**10%** on the same sales basis and period. Price change=£5/£100=**5%**; unit cost change=£6/£60=**10%**. Unit contribution falls from £40 to £39, and gross margin falls from 40% to £39/£105×100=**37.14%** (2 d.p.). One limitation: survey responses are stated intention and may not convert to sales; likewise forecast and ARR are estimates. This practice covers the Appendix 3 interpretation categories with checked calculations and qualified uses; it does not make claims about an actual company.

## SKILL-AO1 — recall, select and communicate business knowledge

**Practice — constructed, unscored (short-answer rehearsal).** (a) Define market share. (b) Give/name one non-financial method of motivation. (c) Give/name one internal economy of scale.

**Answer / rationale.** (a) The firm’s sales as a proportion of total market sales over the same period/measure (a formula may be given if asked). (b) Job enrichment, job rotation or autonomy. (c) Purchasing, technical, managerial, financial or risk-bearing economies arising within a growing business (one valid example). Use the relevant term accurately; do not substitute sales revenue for profit or “weak competition” for the specification’s reason “the business is not competitive.” These are constructed generic recall checks; they are not `State` questions because `State` requires contextual information.

## SKILL-AO2 — apply knowledge/calculations to a case

**Practice — constructed, unscored (application rehearsal).** A 300-employee regional distributor has a 90-day customer-credit period, pays suppliers in 30 days and is considering an overdraft. Explain why the timing is relevant to this business and name one risk of relying on an overdraft.

**Answer / rationale.** The distributor may need to pay suppliers about 60 days before collecting the related customer cash, creating a working-capital timing gap despite sales/profit. A short-term overdraft may bridge it, but it can be costly, variable-rate or repayable on demand and is not a safe permanent substitute for long-term finance. Application comes from using the 300-employee context only where relevant and tying the 90/30-day terms to the business’s cash timing; merely repeating a case name is not reasoning.

## SKILL-AO3 — analyse with developed causal links

**Practice — constructed 6-mark-style analysis, unscored.** Northstar’s factory is experiencing rising packaging defects. Analyse two ways a TQM programme could affect the business.

**Answer / rationale.** First, training and worker-led root-cause checks could identify a poorly calibrated sealing machine; correcting it may reduce defective packs, which lowers rework/returns and protects Northstar’s operating margin. Second, involving suppliers in material standards could reduce inconsistent packaging inputs; fewer line stoppages may raise output per labour-hour and improve delivery reliability. These outcomes depend on training/time/cost and management follow-through; a TQM label alone does not guarantee improvement. The answer builds cause → operational effect → business outcome in Northstar’s case. This is not a Pearson-marked response; the tariff-style prompt only rehearses extended analysis.

## SKILL-AO4 — evaluate, justify and reach a supported judgement

**9-mark-style practice — constructed, unscored.** Northstar must reduce complaints. It can either (A) train customer-service staff to resolve issues or (B) introduce a discount voucher for every complaint. Justify which option it should choose, using a condition that could change the decision.

**Answer / rationale.** Prefer training first because it addresses the handling process and may solve problems at the first contact, rebuilding trust without giving every complainant a price reduction. A voucher could provide immediate goodwill, but repeated compensation raises cost and does not fix the product/service cause; it may also reward avoidable complaints. The decision depends on complaint analysis: if a verified product defect is widespread, fix production/quality before either service-only action; if complaints concern one-off delays, a limited voucher may help. A supported choice compares alternatives and makes the condition explicit. Constructed; no score/mark prediction.

**12-mark-style practice — constructed, unscored.** Northstar is deciding whether to launch a premium energy-efficient appliance nationally now or test it in two regions first. Evaluate which approach is better, using the case evidence: market research finds 62% stated interest among 500 surveyed customers; the existing model has a 30% gross profit margin; the new product requires £2m development and has no actual sales data. Reach a justified conclusion and state what evidence could change it.

**Answer / rationale.** A national launch could capture potential sales if stated interest converts into purchases, and a 30% gross profit margin on the existing model shows gross profit relative to revenue before operating expenses. It does not establish operating profit or cash available for the £2m investment. However, stated interest is not purchases; sample representativeness and willingness to pay are unknown, and national stock/marketing creates exposure before demand is demonstrated. A two-region test costs time and may allow competitors to move, but gives sales, price, reliability and service evidence and limits initial inventory risk. On these facts, test first with clear success measures (conversion, contribution after launch costs, returns and repeat demand), then scale if thresholds are met. A rapid full launch becomes more persuasive if reliable pre-orders, representative willingness-to-pay data and production capacity demonstrate demand and cash can cover the £2m. This judgement weighs short-term opportunity against uncertainty and links a condition to the decision; it is constructed, not an official indicative answer or candidate score.

**Checked record.** The 9-mark rehearsal compares options and gives a contextual condition; the 12-mark rehearsal develops both sides, interprets evidence limits and concludes with decision criteria. Neither imposes a universal paragraph count/AO quota. Official calibration remains bounded to the actual May 2023 scripts/items described in the source review (P2 Q3(e): 5/9; Q4(c): 10/12), not these constructed answers.

## SKILL-EXAM — command words, calculator, context and mixed demands

**Appendix 2 source check — full taxonomy (p.28).** The current specification lists: multiple-choice (select one or more correct answers; recall or calculation); **Define** (term from specification); **Give** (recall); **State** (one-sentence answer selected from contextual information); **Identify** (read a graph/table); **Calculate** (use given data, calculator permitted, show workings); **Complete the table** (work out missing values); **Outline** (two linked contextual points); **Explain** (one fact plus two expansions, with no context in these questions); **Analyse** (extended exploration, in context); **Justify** (use provided information to recommend one of two options); **Evaluate** (extended answer using specification knowledge to reach a supported conclusion about a business situation). `Name` is used here as a generic recall rehearsal alongside Give, not as a replacement for contextual State.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** Harborline is a 12-store retailer with 480 employees. (1) MCQ rehearsal: select all valid internal finance sources: retained profit, personal savings of an owner, or a bank overdraft. (2) Define delegation. (3) Give one internal economy of scale and name one non-financial motivation method (generic recall). (4) State one method Harborline could use to alert its 12 store managers to an urgent product recall. (5) Identify the break-even output from the labelled chart in `04_Business_Finance_Units.md` §3.3.2. (6) Calculate the percentage change in an import bill that rises from £120 to £135. (7) Complete the cash-flow table: cash inflows £18,000; £2,000; total inflows blank. Cash outflows £12,000; £3,000; total outflows blank. Net cash flow blank. (8) Outline one advantage to Harborline of a shared stock system for its 12 stores. (9) Explain one benefit of training (no business context). (10) Analyse two effects on Harborline of inaccurate stock communication. (11) Justify whether Harborline should use email or an urgent phone cascade for the recall, using the case information. (12) Evaluate whether Harborline should open two more stores next year; conclude and state evidence that could change the decision.

**Answer / rationale.** (1) Retained profit and owner’s personal savings; overdraft is external. Follow the instruction to select all correct options. (2) Delegation is assigning a task and authority to another person while the manager retains overall accountability. (3) Internal economy: bulk-purchasing/technical/specialist-management savings within a growing firm; non-financial method: job enrichment, rotation or autonomy. Give/Name are generic recall. (4) Example: “Harborline can phone its 12 store managers immediately and require confirmation,” selecting a method in Harborline’s context. A generic “phone” without reference to the contextual prompt would not meet the stated State demand. (5) **1,000 units**, read at the total-revenue/total-cost intersection. (6) Change=£15; £15/£120×100=**12.5% increase** (original value is denominator). (7) Total inflows=**£20,000**; total outflows=**£15,000**; net cash flow=**+£5,000**. (8) The stores can see common stock information; therefore managers can transfer goods before a store runs out. These are two linked points and both refer to Harborline. (9) Training improves a worker’s skills (fact); the worker can complete tasks more accurately (expansion 1); fewer errors may reduce rework/cost (expansion 2). No context is added, as specified for Explain. (10) Incorrect stock figures may cause Harborline to reorder items it already has; this ties up cash in excess stock. At the same time, stores may show items as available when shelves are empty; customers may leave without buying and the retailer may lose revenue/reputation. (11) Prefer a phone cascade first because the product recall is urgent and managers can confirm receipt; follow it with a written instruction so details are recorded. Email alone may be missed, but phone-only communication risks errors in technical detail. (12) Opening stores could extend Harborline’s reach, but existing store-level stock-communication problems may be multiplied and leases/staffing commit cash. A staged opening is defensible after local demand, cash-flow and operational-control evidence is checked; proceed sooner if those data show profitable demand and reliable systems. The answer must conclude and weigh the specified situation; it is not a fixed-script model.
