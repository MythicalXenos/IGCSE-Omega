# Marketing — checked teaching units

**Scope:** clauses 4.1.1–4.3.4 (10 clauses). Authority: Pearson, *International GCSE Business Specification*, Issue 2 (February 2026), printed pp.16–18. All practice below is constructed and unscored unless explicitly named as an official source; no candidate score is claimed.

## 4.1.1 — Purpose of market research

**Requirement (§4.1.1, p.16):** identify/understand customer needs; identify market gaps; reduce risk; inform decisions.

**Teach it.** Research gathers evidence about customers, competitors and markets. It can reveal what customers value, uncover an unmet need or under-served segment, test assumptions before a launch and support decisions on product, price, promotion or place. It reduces uncertainty, not risk to zero: samples may be biased, stale or unrepresentative, and conditions can change.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** Before launching a low-sugar snack, a producer wants to know which flavours commuters prefer and whether a no-added-sugar option is under-served. Explain how research could inform one product decision and why it cannot guarantee success.

**Answer / rationale.** A survey/test of target commuters can compare flavour preferences; competitor/market evidence can reveal whether the no-added-sugar segment is underserved. The company can refine product and estimate demand, but sample quality, taste, price and competitor response still affect success. All four purposes are covered; spec p.16.

## 4.1.2 — Primary and secondary research methods

**Requirement (§4.1.2, p.16):** primary survey, questionnaire, focus group, observation, test marketing; secondary internet, market reports, government reports.

**Teach it.** Primary research is collected first-hand for the current question. A survey is a systematic sample inquiry; a questionnaire is the instrument (online, paper or interviewer-led); a focus group explores attitudes through guided discussion; observation records behaviour; test marketing sells/trials an offer in a limited market. Secondary research already exists: internet information, commercial market reports and government reports. Compare cost, speed, depth, relevance, coverage and reliability. A large sample is not automatically representative; secondary information may be outdated or collected for another purpose.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** For a new parcel-delivery locker, match each question to a method: (a) count how many passers-by use lockers at three locations; (b) explore why users dislike a design; (c) ask 1,000 target users how often they would use one; (d) trial lockers in two districts; (e) find national population and commuting patterns.

**Answer / rationale.** (a) Observation; (b) focus group; (c) survey using a questionnaire; (d) test marketing; (e) secondary government report (or relevant credible internet data). Each method has limitations: observed behaviour does not reveal motives; focus group is small; questionnaire responses may be biased; trial areas may not represent all districts; secondary data may not match the target segment. Spec p.16; constructed, unscored.

## 4.1.3 — Qualitative/quantitative data, social media and reliability

**Requirement (§4.1.3, p.16):** qualitative and quantitative data; social media collection; reliability.

**Teach it.** Qualitative data describe reasons/opinions in words; quantitative data are numerical and can be compared/statistically analysed. Social media can gather comments, polls, reactions and behavioural data quickly, but users are self-selecting, platform algorithms shape exposure, bots/fake accounts may distort results and the platform audience may not represent all customers. Reliability concerns whether data are consistent and dependable: examine method, sample selection/size, question wording, date, source, repetitions and bias. Reliable collection does not itself ensure a correct business decision.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A brand uses 300 social-media comments (mostly from followers aged 18–24) and a random telephone sample of 600 customers. Identify qualitative and quantitative possibilities, discuss one strength/limitation of each and decide what additional evidence is needed before changing a product for all customers.

**Answer / rationale.** Comments can provide qualitative explanations; coded poll counts/ratings provide quantitative data. Social posts are fast and detailed but self-selected and age-skewed; random telephone sampling may represent customers better but can exclude people who do not answer or lack access. Check sampling and wording, compare sales/returns across customer groups and perhaps conduct a representative survey/test. Do not treat comment volume as reliable population demand. Spec p.16; constructed, unscored.

## 4.2.1 — Why marketing matters; orientation, share and niche/mass markets

**Requirement (§4.2.1, p.16):** satisfying customer needs, relationships, loyalty, market/product orientation, market share and analysis, niche and mass marketing.

**Teach it.** Marketing identifies and meets customer needs and manages the offer/relationship beyond promotion alone. Good service/consistent value can build loyalty and repeat purchase, though loyalty is not guaranteed. A market-oriented business starts with research/customer needs; a product-oriented business focuses more on its product/technical strengths and may then seek buyers. Market share = the firm’s sales ÷ total market sales ×100 for a consistent period/measure. Market analysis considers size, segments, growth, rivals and customer behaviour. Niche marketing serves a narrow segment; mass marketing targets a broad market. Niche can support tailored differentiation but limits volume; mass can reach more buyers but faces wider competition and may fit diverse needs less closely.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A firm sells one specialist hiking shoe to a small group of long-distance walkers and surveys them before each design. A rival sells a basic shoe nationally to a broad audience, using large-scale advertising. Compare orientation/targeting and calculate share if the first firm sells 18,000 pairs in a market of 240,000 pairs.

**Answer / rationale.** The specialist firm is market-oriented if research drives design and targets a niche; rival is closer to mass marketing (its orientation depends on whether it begins from customer research or its product). Share = 18,000/240,000×100 = **7.5%**. Same product units and market/time denominator required. Tailoring may build loyalty but limits market size; mass reach can increase volume but broad messages may fit customer needs less precisely. Spec p.16; constructed, unscored.

## 4.2.2 — Responding to market change

**Requirement (§4.2.2, p.16):** changing customer needs; changing consumer spending patterns; increased competition.

**Teach it.** Businesses monitor what customers value, how much they are willing/able to spend and what alternatives rivals offer. Needs may shift by lifestyle, technology or social trends; spending patterns may shift to essentials, discounts or online channels when budgets tighten; more competition can pressure price, quality, service and differentiation. Responses might adjust product, price, promotion or distribution, but require evidence and cost/benefit assessment. Lowering price may defend volume but reduce margin.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A mid-price homeware brand faces demand for repairable goods, households trading down and a new low-price online rival. Recommend two distinct responses and explain one risk of each.

**Answer / rationale.** It might offer durable/repairable products or repair service to meet a need; risk: redesign and service costs. It might create a clearly specified value range or targeted promotion to retain price-sensitive buyers; risk: lower margins/brand dilution. Improve online service or differentiate quality against the rival; risk: investment may not recover if customers remain price-led. The answer links all three market changes to choices rather than assuming one response solves all. Spec p.16; constructed, unscored.

## 4.2.3 — Segmenting and targeting customers

**Requirement (§4.2.3, p.17):** identify segments using location, demographics, lifestyle, income and age; use segments to target.

**Teach it.** Segmentation divides a market into groups with shared characteristics that may respond differently. Location includes geography/climate/urban-rural; demographics can include household/family characteristics; lifestyle includes activities, interests and values; income concerns spending power; age can relate to needs/life stage. Categories overlap, and age/income do not determine an individual’s preferences. Target a segment only when it is identifiable, reachable and large/profitable enough for the offer; use evidence and avoid stereotypes.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A bank designs a low-fee mobile account for (1) students aged 18–24 with limited monthly income in university towns, and (2) a premium branch service for affluent rural retirees who prefer in-person support. Identify at least two segmentation bases for each and one targeted feature.

**Answer / rationale.** Segment 1: age, income and location; app-first budgeting/low fees may fit, subject to research/accessibility. Segment 2: income, age, location and lifestyle/service preference; local appointment support could fit. Bases overlap; verify needs rather than assuming all people in a group behave alike.

**Official practice cross-check (identity-matched, bounded).** May 2023 4BS1/02 Q4(c), QP printed p.19, evaluates demographic segmentation; matching MS p.23 and ER pp.56–58 were checked. Pearson’s sampled candidate received 10/12, not full marks; this item samples demographic segmentation only and does not test every basis in §4.2.3 or score the constructed practice above.

## 4.3.1 — Product, service, packaging, life cycle and portfolio

**Requirement (§4.3.1, p.17):** product/service development; goods/services difference; packaging; product life-cycle phases/extension; product-portfolio review using Boston matrix.

**Teach it.** Goods are tangible items; services are activities/experiences, often produced and consumed together and less storable. New product/service development moves from idea/research through design/testing/launch; it is costly and uncertain. Packaging protects, informs, facilitates transport/use and can differentiate, but affects cost and waste. Product life cycle is commonly described as introduction, growth, maturity and decline; extension strategies (refresh, new use/market, promotion/packaging changes) seek to prolong sales but may fail. A Boston matrix uses market growth and relative market share (the product’s share compared with its leading competitor): stars (high/high), cash cows (low growth/high share), question marks (high growth/low share), dogs (low/low). It is a portfolio discussion aid, not a guaranteed investment rule; data and cash requirements matter.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A firm launches a reusable bottle and a subscription water-filter service. State one goods/service difference, two packaging roles, a plausible life-cycle extension for a mature bottle, then classify: product A is high-growth/high-share; B low-growth/high-share; C high-growth/low-share; D low-growth/low-share.

**Answer / rationale.** Bottle is a tangible good; subscription is a service. Packaging can protect/inform and improve shelf/transport appeal; also consider cost/waste. A new colour/functional cap, new user segment or revised promotion may extend mature sales if customers value it. A=star, B=cash cow, C=question mark, D=dog by the supplied axes. Matrix labels do not automatically determine “invest/close”; examine cash, strategy and evidence. Spec p.17; constructed, unscored.

## 4.3.2 — Pricing strategies and when to use them

**Requirement (§4.3.2, p.17):** cost-plus, penetration, competition, skimming and promotional pricing, and when each may apply.

**Teach it.** Cost-plus pricing adds a chosen **markup on cost** to unit cost; it is not the same as adding a profit-margin percentage to cost. Example: at unit cost £60, a 25% markup gives price £60×1.25=£75 and profit £15; the resulting profit margin is £15/£75×100=20%, not 25%. Appendix 4 (p.30) defines markup as profit per item ÷ cost per item ×100; margins use revenue as denominator. Cost-plus is simple but can ignore demand and rivals. Penetration sets a low initial price to encourage trial/market entry, often aiming to build volume; it needs finance/capacity and later price rises may be resisted. Competition pricing sets price with reference to rivals; differentiation and cost still matter. Skimming starts high for a novel/differentiated offer with willing early buyers, then may reduce; it can deter price-sensitive buyers/attract competitors. Promotional pricing temporarily lowers price/offers a deal to stimulate trial or clear stock; frequent discounts may train customers to wait and reduce margin/brand value. Select based on objectives, demand, costs, competition and product life cycle.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** Match a plausible strategy to: (a) established cereal with known unit cost and stable demand; (b) new streaming service seeking rapid trial in a crowded market; (c) patented premium device with early enthusiasts; (d) standard building material in a market with transparent rival prices; (e) seasonal stock that must be cleared next month. State one limitation for two.

**Answer / rationale.** (a) Cost-plus may provide a cost-based floor; (b) penetration; (c) skimming; (d) competition pricing; (e) temporary promotional pricing. Limitations: cost-plus may price above willingness to pay; penetration can lose money/trigger response; skimming narrows initial volume; competitor prices may not reflect cost/value differences; promotion erodes margin. Alternatives are acceptable with context. Spec p.17; constructed, unscored.

## 4.3.3 — Place and distribution channels

**Requirement (§4.3.3, p.17):** distribution methods including retailers and e-tailers/e-commerce.

**Teach it.** Place is how the product reaches the customer. A producer may sell directly (store, website/app) or through intermediaries such as wholesalers and retailers; e-tailers sell online and may hold stock or use fulfilment partners. Channels affect reach, convenience, control, data, delivery time, returns and margin. A retailer offers physical access/advice but charges margin and controls some presentation; e-commerce broadens geographic reach and provides transaction data but requires digital marketing, fulfilment, payment/security and returns capability. Choose a channel suited to product, customer and service expectations.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A specialist lamp maker sells fragile custom products to architects and household consumers. Compare direct e-commerce with selling through lighting retailers; recommend a channel mix.

**Answer / rationale.** Direct online sales increase control/customer data and can serve distant buyers, but packaging, delivery damage, installation advice and returns must be managed. Retailers provide display/advice and may reassure customers, but take margin and reduce direct customer control. A mix—direct for custom orders plus selected retailers for demonstration—can fit both segments if margins and service capacity support it. Spec p.17; constructed, unscored.

## 4.3.4 — Promotion, technology, PR and brand

**Requirement (§4.3.4, pp.17–18):** advertising, sponsorship, trials, special offers and branding for segments; above-/below-the-line techniques; PR/image; targeted online advertising, viral social media and e-newsletters; brand importance.

**Teach it.** Promotion communicates value and encourages response. Advertising is paid media; sponsorship associates a brand with an event/person; trials reduce uncertainty; special offers create a temporary incentive; branding creates recognisable identity/associations. Above-the-line commonly means broad paid mass media; below-the-line commonly means more targeted/direct promotion (e.g. samples, direct mail, events), though classifications can depend on the teaching framework. PR manages relationships/publicity and can improve image, but the business does not control independent coverage as it controls an advert. Targeted online ads use audience/data criteria; viral campaigns rely on users sharing and are unpredictable; e-newsletters reach opted-in subscribers but require consent, relevance and unsubscribe controls. A strong brand can differentiate, support loyalty and pricing, but takes consistent investment and may be damaged by poor quality or misconduct.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A premium running-shoe brand wants to reach (i) elite runners and (ii) new casual runners. Propose one promotion method for each, include an above- or below-the-line example, one technology method and one PR action; explain how brand matters and one risk.

**Answer / rationale.** Sponsor a respected race/athlete or use targeted performance content for elite runners; offer a trial/introductory event or segmented e-newsletter for casual runners. A broad paid TV campaign is above-the-line; direct samples/events are below-the-line in the common distinction. Targeted online ads or permission-based e-newsletters use technology; PR could arrange a transparent product-safety/community event. Brand recognition/trust can differentiate and encourage repeat purchase; an unsubstantiated performance claim or poor product can damage reputation. Methods must fit each segment; constructed, unscored.
