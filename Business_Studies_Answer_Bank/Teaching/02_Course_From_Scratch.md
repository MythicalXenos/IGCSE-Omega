## Lesson 1 — The 32 short marks (tonight, 25 minutes)

These are 40% of the paper and the cheapest marks on it. Your mock scored **26/32** here — the only
losses were **one MCQ wrong, one left blank, one circular definition, and one too-general 1-mark.**

### 1.1 The six MCQs in 1(a)(i)–(vi) — never leave one

- **Answer all six, always.** A blank is a guaranteed zero; a guess is a 25% chance.
- **Two of the six are calculation or definition, and are worth doing slowly.** The other four are
  recall.
- The distractors are usually *the right definition of the wrong term*. If two options both look
  familiar, the question is testing the difference between them — find the difference, not the meaning.

### 1.2 Define — the circularity trap

> ❌ *"Fringe benefits are benefits given to employees."* — **defines the word with itself. 0.**
> ✅ *"Fringe benefits are **extra** payments or rewards **in addition to the normal wage or salary**."*

**Three rules for every definition:**
1. **Never use the word inside the definition.**
2. **Use the scheme's trigger words** — *extra / in addition to* (fringe), *not fully responsible / only
   up to what they invested* (limited liability), *the amount a business expects to have at the start of
   a month* (opening balance).
3. **One sentence, no example.** An example earns nothing and costs time.

**The twelve definitions the paper uses most** (all from mark schemes in `By_Paper/`):

| Term | Say this |
|---|---|
| **Limited liability** | shareholders are only responsible for the amount they invested, not the business's debts |
| **Fringe benefits** | extra rewards **in addition to** the normal wage or salary |
| **Cash flow forecast** | a prediction of the expected receipts and payments over a future period |
| **Net cash flow** | the difference between cash inflow and cash outflow |
| **Opening / closing balance** | the amount of cash the business expects to have at the start / end of a month |
| **Break-even point** | the level of output at which total revenue equals total costs (no profit, no loss) |
| **Margin of safety** | the amount by which current sales exceed the break-even level |
| **Social enterprise** | a business whose main aim is to improve human well-being rather than make a profit |
| **Market segmentation** | dividing a market into groups of customers with similar characteristics |
| **Branding** | creating a name and logo that customers recognise and trust, distinct from rivals |
| **Opportunity cost** | the benefit given up of the next best alternative |
| **Added value** | the difference between the cost of inputs and the price the customer pays |

### 1.3 State — one fact, one context noun

**Rule: the sentence must contain a noun that belongs to this case.** Not the business's name —
Pearson: *"The name of the business does not provide context."* A **product, a person, a place, a
number, a supplier, a customer type.**

> ❌ *"One reason is to increase profit."*
> ✅ *"One reason is to increase profit **on the wedding cakes**."*

Your mock lost a mark on 1(d) by writing *"to evaluate their range of products"* when the scheme wanted
the tool's own category — *"identify the **dog** products and stop making them."* **Name the term's own
vocabulary.**

### 1.4 Calculate — the four rules

1. **Show working.** It earns a mark on its own even when the final figure is wrong.
2. **Do exactly the decimal places asked.** *"to 2 decimal places"* → **1.75**, not 1.8. A correct
   answer at the wrong d.p. **loses the mark** — Pearson says so in as many words.
3. **Units and currency** on the answer line: £, %, units, "people", "tubs".
4. **If the answer looks wrong, re-check which denominator the paper used.** Your mock was nearly
   marked down for using 330 000 when the scheme used **330 606** — the scheme outranks your mental
   arithmetic.

**The formula sheet — memorise all nine.** (Derived from the 2-mark items in all 19 papers.)

| Formula | |
|---|---|
| Revenue | price × quantity sold |
| Total cost | fixed costs + variable costs |
| Variable cost | variable cost per unit × quantity |
| **Break-even (units)** | **fixed costs ÷ (selling price − variable cost per unit)** |
| Margin of safety | actual sales − break-even sales |
| Gross profit | revenue − cost of sales |
| **Gross profit margin** | (gross profit ÷ revenue) × 100 |
| Operating profit | gross profit − other expenses |
| **Operating profit margin** | (operating profit ÷ revenue) × 100 |
| **ROCE** | (operating profit ÷ capital employed) × 100 |
| Mark-up | ((selling price − cost) ÷ cost) × 100 |
| Net cash flow | total inflows − total outflows |
| Closing balance | opening balance + net cash flow |
| **Current ratio** | current assets ÷ current liabilities |
| **Acid test** | (current assets − stock) ÷ current liabilities |
| Retained profit | profit − dividends (or drawings) |

> **Exam habit:** the four calculations are **1(e), 3(b), 3(c), 4(a)** — always. When a table of
> figures is printed (Figure 1, Figure 2), the 2-mark question after it is the calculation and the
> 6-mark is usually *"how useful is this information"*. Read the table's row and column headings first.

## Lesson 2 — The 3-mark Explain (10 minutes)

**Twelve marks — always 1(f), 2(c), 2(d) and 2(e). Three rungs, one sentence each — or one sentence
with three clauses.**

> **Rung 1** what happens (cause) → **Rung 2** what that leads to (effect) → **Rung 3** what it does to
> the business (outcome: money, customers, capacity).

**Worked — "Explain one reason why businesses want to use resources effectively in manufacturing."**

> ❌ *"So that they are more profitable."* — one rung. **1 mark.**
> ✅ *"Using resources effectively means less material is wasted **(1)**, so the business buys fewer raw
> materials for the same output **(1)**, which lowers unit costs and lets it either cut price or keep a
> higher margin **(1)**."*

**The three rungs must use different nouns.** If rung 3 repeats rung 1's noun, you have written one rung
twice.

⚠️ **No definitions here.** *"No marks are awarded for a definition."* Start at the cause.

## Lesson 3 — The 6-mark Analyse (tomorrow, 30 minutes)

**Three questions, 18 marks, and no judgement whatsoever.** You wrote **3/6, 4/6, 4/6** on your mock —
**seven marks** — all from the same cause: **one chain where the question pays for two.**

### The shape

> **Chain 1** (4 sentences): point → because → this means → so the business [money/customers]
> **Chain 2** (4 sentences): *"A second reason is…"* → because → this means → so the business […]
> **Stop.** No conclusion. A 6-marker has **no AO4 marks**, so a conclusion earns nothing.

**Two chains must end in different places.** Money, customers, capacity, staff, reputation — pick two
different ones. If both end at "better reputation", you have written one chain twice.

### Worked — a real **6/6** (June 2024 P2, TUI, Boston Matrix)

> *"TUI could use the Boston Matrix to analyse which of their holidays are 'dog' products. They can then
> choose whether or not to **shut down** that holiday or to try re-charging and re-advertising it. This
> informed decision will **improve the efficiency of the business**.*
>
> *TUI could also use the Boston Matrix to identify 'star' products which are worth significant
> investment due to **high market share in a growing market**. This information could enable TUI to
> prioritise supporting the correct holiday services, **greatly increasing their revenue** through
> effective investment into the right areas."*

**Notice what is absent: the Boston Matrix is never explained.** No axes, no grid, no *"a dog is…"*.
The examiners' own complaint about the weak scripts was exactly that — *"Some candidates gave a very
detailed description of the Boston Matrix but did not apply it to TUI."*

> ### ⭐ THE 6-MARK RULE, in one line
> **Never define the thing. Say what the business does with it, and what that does to its money or its
> customers — twice, in two different directions.**

### The drill (do this once, timed, 7 minutes)

Take any topic from the chain sheet. Set a 7-minute timer. Write **two** chains only, each ending at a
different outcome. Then count: have you written four "this means / so that" links? If not, you have
written a description.

## Lesson 4 — The 9-mark Justify (tomorrow, 40 minutes — the biggest single win)

**Two questions, 18 marks. You scored 14/18 last time. The four missing marks are one clause.**

**First: the stem never names the topic.** Across all 19 papers the 9-markers read only *"Justify which
one of these two options X should choose."* **The two options are printed above it, in the case or a
box.** So you read the options, not the question.

### The four moves, in order

| Move | Write | Earns |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Choose in your first sentence.** *"CCT should hold weekly meetings."* | AO4 — the decision must be visible, not saved for the end |
| **2** | **Give the case-specific reason.** The fact that makes it right *for this business*. | AO2 |
| **3** | **Dismiss the other option with a consequence.** *"However, email means…"* | AO3/AO4 |
| **4** | **Name the condition.** *"This depends on…"* — a circumstance that could only be written about *this* option. | AO4 — the mark most candidates never earn |

### The condition test

> **Could your last clause be pasted onto a different question without changing a word?**
> If yes, it is a **platitude** and scores nothing.

- ❌ *"This depends on whether the business can afford it."* — pasteable. Worth nothing.
- ❌ *"It depends on the objectives."* — pasteable. Worth nothing.
- ✅ *"However, the success of this depends on **how much employees get to voice their own concerns**."*
  — could only be written about weekly meetings. **9/9.**

### The two verified 9/9s — both routes are legal

**Route A — one option, weighed** (June 2019 P1 Q3(e), 9/9). *"Candidates do not have to consider both
options… can achieve the full marks only considering the one option."*

**Route B — both options, then a conclusion** (June 2025 P2R Q2(f), 9/9 — Rabanne). *"This candidate has
chosen to look at both options which is acceptable."*

**The criterion is not one-option vs two. It is evaluate vs describe.** May 2023: *"many candidates
described both methods, which is knowledge"* → Level 2.

### The M&M 9/9 conclusion — three sentences, three jobs

> *"**Personally, I would choose to use Bonuses** as money can be a key motivator to people, it also helps
> increase your sales. **A disadvantage of job rotation is that people may not be qualified for every
> job** in a company/business. **In the long term, job rotation may be better** as it gives employees a
> sense of value."*

**1** decides · **2** names the other option's drawback · **3** concedes the long-run counter-case.

⭐ **The third sentence argues against the candidate's own choice and the script still gets 9.** A
judgement that concedes something is *stronger* than one that does not.

### What to write, in ten minutes, on any option pair

```
[1] "X should choose OPTION 1."                              (choice — 15 words)
[2] "This is because [case fact] → [effect] → [outcome]."   (two chains — 80 words)
[3] "Option 2, however, [drawback] → [consequence]."        (counter — 40 words)
[4] "Overall … This depends on [named condition]."          (condition — 25 words)
```
**≈160 words. The 9/9s are 150–200 words.** More than that is time taken from Question 4.

### The option-pair families — what 2(f) and 3(e) are usually about

| 2(f) — almost always **marketing or finance** | 3(e) — almost always **operations or people** |
|---|---|
| promotion: social media vs coupons, sponsorship vs offers | contracts: full-time vs part-time, permanent vs temporary |
| pricing: skimming vs penetration, cost-plus vs competitive | training: on-the-job vs off-the-job, induction |
| research: primary vs secondary, questionnaires vs focus groups | recruitment: internal vs external, CV vs interview |
| finance: bank loan vs venture capital, crowdfunding, retained profit | production: job vs batch, batch vs flow, Kaizen vs JIT |
| place: physical store vs online | structure: centralised vs decentralised, partnership vs Ltd |

> **So if you are short of time, you already know what 2(f) and 3(e) are about before you read them.**
> The chain sheet has a chain for every pair in that table.

## Lesson 5 — The 12-mark Evaluate (tomorrow, 50 minutes)

**One question, 12 marks, always 4(c), always 3/3/3/3 — the only place AO1 is paid for.**
You scored **6/12**: the judgement was there, but the *financial* methods were listed, not applied.

### The four paragraphs

```
P1  What it is, in your own words, + your answer in the same breath      AO1 + AO2
P2  Applied to THIS business — a chain, not a claim                      AO2 + AO3
P3  "However…" — turn against what you just argued                       AO3 (+AO2)
P4  Decide, and name the condition                                      AO4
```

**Rules that decide the band:**
- **Continuous prose. Bullets cannot reach Level 3**, however good the content.
- **No separate "In conclusion" paragraph.** The 12/12 has none; its last paragraph *decides*.
- **≈330–400 words in 12–14 minutes.**
- **AO2 is woven, not stamped.** *"Crumb is a cake business because it makes cakes"* is not context.

### The 12/12, and what it actually does (Irsi, May 2023 P1 Q4(c))

> *"Irsi Chocolatier can use financial methods of motivation… like **bonuses and promotions and
> commission**. The **older three loyal employees** could be offered a higher position via promotion,
> this would **improve the motivation**… **However** disadvantage of rewarding bonuses based on piece
> rate is that it could **compromise quality**…
> **Furthermore** the **effectiveness of using financial methods of motivation could be limited because
> it depends on factors such as nature of employee**, they may not look for factors of motivation that do
> not involve financial incentives — for example, they may prefer **job rotation, job enrichment** …"*

**Three things to copy:**
1. **A chain per method**, not a list: *piece rate → more output → productivity ↑ → more sold*.
2. **"However" attached to the thing it just praised** — not a new topic.
3. **The condition, then knowledge from outside the case** (*job rotation, job enrichment*). That is
   the AO1 mark: the 12-marker is the only tariff that pays for knowing something the case never said.

### The eight 12-mark families, and the angle each one wants

| Family | The angle that scores |
|---|---|
| **Quality / TQM** | quality → recommendation → premium price; **however** cost of control and price-sensitive customers |
| **Location** | footfall / proximity to customers, suppliers, labour; **however** rent and online delivery |
| **Market research** | reduces risk before spending; **however** cost, bias, and the sample being wrong |
| **Marketing's importance** | builds awareness → sales; **however** product and price may matter more |
| **Responding to change** | adapt to tastes → stay relevant; **however** cost and existing loyal customers |
| **Measuring success** | profit vs customer satisfaction vs growth; **however** profit is short-term and can hide problems |
| **Social objectives / franchise / legal structure / growth** | the benefit, the cost, and **who** it is for |
| **Financial methods of motivation / interest rates / cash** | chain to money; **however** the condition that makes it work |

## Lesson 6 — The clock (tonight, 20 minutes — this cost you 6 marks)

Your mock was timed. Two answers died mid-sentence. **That is not a habit; it is the clock**, and the
fix is a rule about when to leave.

### The timing that is not negotiable

| Question | Marks | Time | Running total |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Q1 | 20 | **22½ min** | 22½ |
| Q2 | 20 | **22½ min** | **45 — if you are past this, you are behind** |
| Q3 | 20 | **22½ min** | 67½ |
| Q4 | 20 | **22½ min** | **90** |

**Item level:** 1 mark ≈ 1 min · 2 ≈ 2¼ · 3 ≈ 3½ · 6 ≈ 6¾ · 9 ≈ 10 · 12 ≈ 13½.

### The three rules that protect the marks you are losing

1. **Write the last sentence first, or not at all.** Before you start a long answer, know the sentence
   that ends it. If you cannot, you do not have the chain yet — plan for 20 seconds.
2. **At the time limit, stop mid-sentence and move.** A finished 3-mark answer is worth more than a
   perfect 6-marker you never start. **Never leave a question blank because you ran out of time on the
   one before it.**
3. **Finish Q4(c) at 85 minutes.** The last five minutes are for the four checks below — they are
   cheaper marks than anything you can write in five minutes.

### The last five minutes, in this order

1. **Every calculation**: d.p., units, working shown.
2. **Every 1-mark "state"**: does the sentence contain a case noun?
3. **The 12-marker**: does the last paragraph decide, and name a condition?
4. **Nothing blank.** *"Try to answer every question"* is printed on the cover.

### The 20-second plan before each long answer

> **6-mark:** two nouns for my two outcomes. → write.
> **9-mark:** my choice (one word) · one case fact · the other option's drawback · my condition.
> **12-mark:** what it is · one chain · the however · my condition.

**Four nouns and a clause. That is the plan. Write it in the margin, not on rough paper.**

## Appendix B — What to do if the case study is unfamiliar

**It always is.** The case is new every series; the machines are not. Three questions to ask of any
case in the first 60 seconds:

1. **What does it sell, and to whom?** (that is your context for every answer)
2. **How big is it — staff, sites, money?** (that decides whether a suggestion is plausible)
3. **What has just changed or is about to?** (that is what the 12-marker will be about)

Write those three in the margin of Question 1 and use them in every answer. **That is what "context
throughout" means:** the same three facts, re-used.
