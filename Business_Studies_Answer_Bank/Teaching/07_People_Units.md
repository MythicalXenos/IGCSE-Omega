# People in Business — checked teaching units

**Scope:** clauses 2.1.1–2.5.3 (12 clauses). Authority: Pearson, *International GCSE Business Specification*, Issue 2 (February 2026), printed pp.11–13. Practice below is constructed and unscored unless a specific official source is named; no candidate marks are invented.

## 2.1.1 — Good communication, methods and IT

**Requirement (§2.1.1, p.11):** importance of good communication; methods used, including IT; each method’s benefits and limitations.

**Teach it.** Communication transfers information/instructions and checks understanding. Good communication can coordinate work, support customer service and safety, clarify targets and reduce errors. Methods include face-to-face conversation/meetings, phone/video calls, letters/reports, email, messaging, intranet and shared digital systems. Compare speed, cost, detail, permanence, confidentiality, accessibility and opportunity for questions. IT can reach dispersed teams quickly and preserve searchable records, but risks overload, misinterpretation, access/cybersecurity issues and exclusion of workers without access or training. Choose a method for the message and audience; sending is not proof that it was understood.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A multinational retailer must (a) warn warehouse staff of an urgent safety hazard, (b) circulate a detailed policy that staff must later consult, and (c) discuss a sensitive performance concern with one manager. Choose a method for each and explain a benefit and limitation.

**Answer / rationale.** (a) Immediate face-to-face/phone or an urgent alert followed by confirmation, because it is quick; verify receipt and understanding. (b) Written policy on a controlled intranet, because it is detailed/searchable; ensure access, version control and training. (c) Private face-to-face/video meeting, because it allows clarification and sensitivity; confidentiality and scheduling are concerns. Alternatives can be justified by context. Spec p.11; all methods are examples, not a mandatory answer list.

## 2.1.2 — Communication barriers and solutions

**Requirement (§2.1.2, p.11):** how barriers arise; problems caused by ineffective communication; how barriers can be removed.

**Teach it.** Barriers include language/literacy differences, jargon, noise, poor technology, time zones, status/fear, information overload, unclear responsibility, inaccessible formats and selective filtering. A barrier may cause wrong production, delay, unsafe work, conflict, missed sales or duplicated effort. Diagnose the specific cause before choosing a remedy: plain language/translation, accessible formats, reliable systems, training, feedback/confirmation, suitable channels, clear reporting lines or protected two-way discussion. More messages can worsen overload.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A factory’s head office emails a long technical instruction only in English to a night shift with limited English access; staff do not acknowledge it and use the previous process. Identify two barriers, one consequence and two targeted fixes.

**Answer / rationale.** Language/technical jargon and inaccessible timing/channel are barriers; outdated process can cause defective or unsafe output. Provide translated/plain-language visual instructions accessible on shift, brief supervisor-led demonstration, and ask workers to explain the process back/confirm receipt. A generic “send another email” does not address the barriers. Spec p.11; constructed, unscored.

## 2.2.1 — Types of employment

**Requirement (§2.2.1, p.11):** full-time; part-time; job share; casual/seasonal/temporary.

**Teach it.** Full-time and part-time describe hours relative to the employer’s/contract’s standard; definitions vary by jurisdiction and contract. Job sharing means two or more people share the duties/hours of one role. Casual work is irregular/on-demand; seasonal work responds to predictable peak periods; temporary work is for a limited period/project/cover. These arrangements can overlap (a seasonal worker may be part-time). Consider labour demand, continuity, flexibility, training, coordination, employment obligations and staff preferences; do not assume non-full-time means no employment rights.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** Match each need to a likely arrangement and give a limitation: year-round department manager; four-hour weekday cashier; two experienced people jointly cover one full role; extra packers for a six-week holiday peak; cover for a three-month parental leave.

**Answer / rationale.** Full-time; part-time; job share; seasonal/casual temporary peak staff; temporary cover, respectively. Limitations include higher committed hours, less coverage, handover/coordination needs, short-notice availability/training and end-of-contract recruitment. Suitability depends on the actual contract and legal framework. Spec p.11; constructed, unscored.

## 2.2.2 — Recruitment documents

**Requirement (§2.2.2, p.11):** job description; person specification; application form; CV/résumé.

**Teach it.** A job description states the role’s duties, responsibilities and reporting arrangements. A person specification states the skills, qualifications, experience or attributes needed to perform it. An application form collects standardised candidate information; a CV/résumé summarises an individual’s education, experience and skills in their own format. Documents help clarify a vacancy and compare evidence but can be incomplete or biased; requirements should be relevant and fair.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A retailer needs a stock-control supervisor. Classify: “reconcile weekly stock records and supervise four staff”; “accurate spreadsheet use and prior team-supervision experience”; a standard form asking every applicant the same work-history questions; an applicant’s two-page career summary.

**Answer / rationale.** Duty statement = job description; required skills/experience = person specification; standard information collection = application form; personal career summary = CV/résumé. The criteria should be job-related; a CV alone does not prove ability. Spec p.11; constructed, unscored.

## 2.2.3 — Internal/external recruitment, advertisements, shortlisting and interviews

**Requirement (§2.2.3, p.11):** internal and external recruitment; job advertisement; shortlisting; interviewing.

**Teach it.** Internal recruitment fills a vacancy with an existing employee; it can reward/develop staff and reduce induction time, but limits the field and may leave another vacancy. External recruitment seeks applicants outside; it can widen the skills pool but costs time/money and requires induction. A good advertisement accurately states role, essential criteria, hours/location and application method. Shortlisting compares applications against relevant pre-set criteria; interviewing explores evidence consistently, with accessible arrangements and questions linked to the role. Neither process guarantees the best future performance.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A hotel needs a bilingual reservations supervisor within one month. Explain one reason to consider an internal applicant and one to advertise externally; list two useful advertisement details and describe fair shortlisting and interviewing.

**Answer / rationale.** Internal applicants may know booking systems and customers; external recruitment could find language skills absent internally. State duties, bilingual requirement, location/hours and application deadline/method. Shortlist against job-related essential criteria, record evidence consistently, then ask candidates comparable job-linked questions and score responses using the same rubric. Fairness and reliable evidence matter; no approach guarantees success.

**Official practice cross-check (identity-matched, bounded).** May 2023 4BS1/02 Q3(d), QP printed p.14, analyses benefits to IKEA of shortlisting factory candidates; matching MS p.18 and ER pp.45–47 were checked. The ER records one candidate at 6/6; this is an actual Pearson-scored script, not a score for the constructed practice above. It samples shortlisting only, not the full recruitment-process clause.

## 2.2.4 — Equal opportunity and minimum-wage laws

**Requirement (§2.2.4, p.11):** legal controls and effects—equal opportunities for gender, race, disability, religion, sexual preference and age; minimum-wage laws.

**Teach it.** Equal-opportunity controls aim to prevent unlawful discrimination across the characteristics specified in the current qualification. Employers should use relevant criteria, accessible recruitment/work arrangements and consistent treatment; exact legal duties and protected categories vary by jurisdiction, so follow applicable local law. Minimum-wage law sets a legal wage floor for covered workers; employers must check rates, hours and eligibility, keep records and budget labour costs. Compliance can raise costs but reduces legal risk and can support fair treatment/retention. Do not present one country’s thresholds as universal.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A chain rejects a qualified applicant because a manager assumes older workers cannot use its system; it also schedules a covered worker for 40 hours but pays below the applicable statutory minimum. Identify issues and propose compliant corrections.

**Answer / rationale.** The age-based assumption is discriminatory; use objective, job-related evidence and offer an accessible skills assessment/training if needed. Recalculate pay against the applicable local minimum wage and actual hours, correct underpayment, maintain records and review payroll. This is general teaching, not jurisdiction-specific legal advice. Spec p.11; constructed, unscored.

## 2.3.1 — Training: purpose, types, safety, benefits and limitations

**Requirement (§2.3.1, p.11):** importance to business/workers; induction, on-the-job and off-the-job purposes; health-and-safety compliance; benefits and limitations.

**Teach it.** Induction introduces a new worker to the organisation, role, procedures and safety arrangements. On-the-job training occurs while doing work under guidance; it is practical and job-specific but can disrupt production or pass on poor habits. Off-the-job training occurs away from normal tasks (course, simulation or external provider); it can offer specialist expertise but costs money/time and may transfer imperfectly to the job. Training can improve skills, quality, productivity, confidence, adaptability and safety; it can be expensive, take staff away from work and risk trained workers leaving. Employers must meet relevant health-and-safety duties, including instruction/training appropriate to the work.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A new machine operator needs company orientation, safe machine operation and advanced robotics knowledge. Select a suitable type for each and explain one business and one worker benefit plus a limitation.

**Answer / rationale.** Induction for company/site/emergency procedures; supervised on-the-job training for safe operation and routine practice; off-the-job specialist course or simulator for advanced robotics (with supervised transfer to the actual equipment). Benefits include fewer injuries/errors, higher output/quality and stronger worker capability; costs and downtime are limitations. Safety instruction cannot be skipped to save time. Spec p.11; constructed, unscored.

## 2.4.1 — Why motivation matters; Herzberg, Maslow and Taylor

**Requirement (§2.4.1, p.12):** attracting/retaining employees, productivity; motivational theories of Herzberg, Maslow and Taylor.

**Teach it.** Motivation can affect willingness to join/stay, effort, attendance, service and productivity, but outcomes also depend on skill, resources and management. Maslow proposed a hierarchy of needs (physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation); it is a framework, not a universal fixed sequence. Herzberg distinguished hygiene factors (e.g. pay/conditions/security, whose absence can cause dissatisfaction) from motivators (e.g. achievement, recognition, responsibility); improving hygiene alone may not create lasting motivation. Taylor’s scientific-management approach emphasised task measurement, standard methods and financial incentives linked to output. Theories are lenses, not guaranteed predictions; employees differ.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A call centre has high turnover, low pay relative to local rivals, strict scripts and few progression opportunities. Apply one idea from each theory and explain how improved motivation might affect retention or productivity.

**Answer / rationale.** Maslow: address security/needs and offer routes to belonging/esteem/development; Herzberg: fair pay/conditions address hygiene while recognition/responsibility add motivators; Taylor: clear standards and a fair performance-linked reward may raise measured output. A balanced package could improve retention and effort, but excessive targets may reduce call quality or increase stress. The theories suggest choices; results should be monitored. Spec p.12; constructed, unscored.

## 2.4.2 — Financial and non-financial motivation methods

**Requirement (§2.4.2, p.12):** financial—remuneration, bonus, commission, promotion, fringe benefits; non-financial—job rotation, job enrichment, autonomy.

**Teach it.** Remuneration is pay for work; a bonus is additional reward for meeting a target; commission links pay to sales; promotion moves to a higher role/responsibility and often pay; fringe benefits are extra rewards such as insurance or a company vehicle. Job rotation moves workers between tasks; job enrichment adds responsibility/variety; autonomy gives discretion over how work is completed. Match method to job, employee and measurable outcome. Incentives can motivate but may encourage unsafe shortcuts, narrow selling, unhealthy competition or costs; non-financial methods may need training and well-designed roles.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A service team must improve customer retention without pushing unsuitable products. Choose one financial and one non-financial method; explain a risk and how to monitor the outcome.

**Answer / rationale.** For example, a team bonus for verified renewal quality (not raw sales alone) plus autonomy/job enrichment to resolve customer issues may align effort and service. Risks include gaming the retention measure or extra decision burden; audit complaints, renewal quality and staff workload, and train staff. Other choices—remuneration, commission, promotion, fringe benefits, rotation—must be correctly identified and justified.

**Official practice cross-check (identity-matched, bounded).** May 2023 4BS1/02 Q3(e), QP printed p.15, asks which motivation method IKEA should use; matching MS p.19 and ER pp.48–50 were checked. The ER’s selected candidate response received 5/9, not full marks; this official example samples bonus/job enrichment and evaluation but not every method in §2.4.2. It does not score the constructed practice above.

## 2.5.1 — Organisation charts: hierarchy/flat and centralised/decentralised

**Requirement (§2.5.1, p.12):** charts; hierarchical and flat; centralised and decentralised structures.

**Teach it.** An organisation chart displays formal roles/reporting relationships, not every informal influence. A hierarchical structure has more levels; a flat structure has fewer. Centralisation keeps important decisions near the centre/senior managers; decentralisation gives more decision authority to lower levels, branches or teams. These are separate dimensions: a business can have several levels but delegate some decisions. More levels may clarify oversight but slow communication; fewer levels may speed decisions but widen managers’ spans. Decentralisation can improve local response but produce inconsistent decisions without controls.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** Draw a simple four-level chart with chief executive, regional managers, store managers and staff. Classify the hierarchy and state one decision that could be centralised and one decentralised.

**Answer / rationale.** Four levels are hierarchical relative to a flatter structure. Brand-wide pricing could be centralised for consistency; local staff scheduling could be decentralised for demand response, within rules. A chart shows reporting lines, not necessarily the location of every decision. Spec p.12; constructed, unscored.

## 2.5.2 — Accountability, span, chain of command and delegation

**Requirement (§2.5.2, p.12):** employee roles/responsibility in compliance/accountability; span of control; chain of command; delegation.

**Teach it.** Accountability means being answerable for duties/results; compliance means following applicable law, policy and procedures. Span of control is the number of subordinates directly reporting to a manager; chain of command is the formal route of authority/communication. Delegation assigns a task and authority to another employee, while the manager retains overall accountability for ensuring appropriate oversight. Wider spans can lower management layers but make coaching/control harder; narrow spans allow closer supervision but add hierarchy/cost. Clear authority, training and escalation routes support safe delegation.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** One supervisor directly manages 18 new staff across two shifts. A safety-check task is delegated to a trained team leader. Identify span, chain-of-command route, delegated responsibility and one control to retain accountability.

**Answer / rationale.** Span is 18 direct reports (if all report directly); the team leader reports/escalates through the supervisor in the stated chain. The leader conducts the check with delegated authority; the supervisor should retain accountability through training, a checklist, records and follow-up. The supervisor’s wide span may limit coaching, so appointing trained shift leads/adjusting reporting can improve oversight. Spec p.12; constructed, unscored.

## 2.5.3 — Functional areas and responsibilities

**Requirement (§2.5.3, p.12):** HR, finance, marketing and production duties as listed in the specification.

**Teach it.** HR workforce-plans and recruits/selects; trains; manages health and safety, welfare and employment issues; handles industrial relations, discipline/grievance procedures, dismissal, unfair-dismissal risk and redundancy. Finance manages wages/salaries, cash-flow forecasts, budgets and accounting. Marketing researches markets, plans products, sets pricing, promotes/sells/advertises, serves customers, manages public relations, packaging and distribution. Production manufactures, designs new products, controls quality and stock. Functions need coordination: marketing forecasts affect production/stock; finance budgets affect hiring and investment; HR capability affects quality/safety. In a small firm one person may perform multiple functions.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A large manufacturer is planning a new recyclable appliance. Allocate these decisions: forecast staff numbers; select/train technicians; set wage budget/cash forecast; research customer needs and choose a price; design packaging and distribution; manufacture prototypes; inspect quality; manage grievances; arrange stock records; plan advertising/customer service; consider a redundancy proposal and check fair process.

**Answer / rationale.** HR: workforce plan, recruit/select/train, health/safety/welfare/employment/industrial relations, grievance and redundancy/dismissal procedures. Finance: wages, cash forecast, budget, accounts. Marketing: research, product planning, price, package/distribute, promote/advertise, customer service and PR. Production: manufacture/design product, quality and stock control. Some activities overlap (finance supplies cost data to pricing; HR/production share safety); give functional lead plus reason. Spec p.12; every enumerated responsibility is included in the teaching map and applied through the scenario; constructed, unscored.
