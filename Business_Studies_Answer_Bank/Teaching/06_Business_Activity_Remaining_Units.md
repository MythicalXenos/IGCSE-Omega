## 1.2.1 — Main types of business ownership

**Requirement (§1.2.1, p.9):** sole trader; partnerships; limited companies (private and public); public corporations.

**Teach it.** A sole trader is owned by one person; a partnership is owned by two or more partners. These are unincorporated forms in the usual textbook examples, so owners can have personal liability for business debts; the exact liability of a partnership can depend on its legal form. A limited company is legally separate from its shareholders and gives limited liability subject to the law; a private limited company (Ltd) cannot offer shares to the general public, while a public limited company (plc) can offer shares publicly and may seek a stock-market listing. A public corporation is owned/controlled by the state, not a plc: it may be established to provide a public service, with public accountability and political objectives as well as financial considerations. Do not use “public company” and “public corporation” as synonyms.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** Classify: (A) one person owns an unincorporated repair shop; (B) three people jointly own an unincorporated design firm; (C) a shareholder-owned company whose shares are privately held; (D) a shareholder-owned company offering shares to the public; (E) a state-owned rail operator.

**Answer / rationale.** A sole trader; B partnership; C private limited company; D public limited company; E public corporation. Ownership/control and legal structure, not size or the word “public” alone, distinguish them. The question tests all five forms; no legal advice is offered.

**Official practice cross-check (identity-matched, bounded).** May 2023 4BS1/02R Q1(b), QP printed p.6, asks for a definition of public corporation. Matching P2R MS PDF p.6 accepts a business owned and controlled by the state/government. This checks only that definition; the other forms are practised in the constructed task.

**Checked record:** all four ownership types in the specification and the distinct public-corporation form are defined and discriminated. Constructed classification checked against the specification’s exact list; unscored.

## 1.2.2 — Ownership characteristics, public ownership and suitability

**Requirement (§1.2.2, p.9):** risk, ownership and limited liability; reasons for/against public ownership; ownership, control, finance, profit use, stakeholders/shareholders; appropriateness of forms.

**Teach it.** Legal form changes who owns and controls the business, who bears financial risk, what finance is accessible, and who receives or directs any surplus. Sole traders and many ordinary partnerships retain close owner control and can make decisions quickly, but may have limited finance and personal exposure to business debts. Company shareholders generally have limited liability, and companies can raise share capital; public share issues can dilute control and require disclosure. A public corporation can be funded/mandated by government and accountable to the public, which may support essential services or wider access; political priorities, bureaucracy, public cost and weaker commercial incentives can be drawbacks. Stakeholders include workers, customers, suppliers, lenders, communities and government; shareholders are one stakeholder group and are owners of a company. “Most appropriate” depends on scale, risk, capital needs, control preference, profit distribution and stakeholder purpose—not on a universal ranking.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A local founder wants to keep decision-making power while opening a second repair branch. A separate national water network requires very large capital, universal access and long-term infrastructure investment. Compare plausible forms of ownership for both. Include who owns/controls, one finance route, risk/liability, use of profit/surplus and a stakeholder effect; give one advantage and drawback of public ownership for the water network.

**Answer / rationale.** The repair founder could remain a sole trader or form a private limited company. Sole trader preserves direct control but leaves personal liability and may constrain finance; a private company can issue shares privately and generally limits shareholder liability, but new investors may influence decisions and expect returns. For essential national infrastructure, public-corporation ownership could support universal-service obligations and public accountability, using public finance; disadvantages may include fiscal burden and political influence on investment/pricing. A private company/regulated provider is also arguable if the response addresses access, funding and accountability. Surpluses may be reinvested, distributed to shareholders, or used for public purposes depending on form/mandate. Credit linked comparison and case-specific trade-offs; not a fixed “correct” legal recommendation.

**Checked record:** every listed consideration is required in the response prompt and answer; limited liability is not described as removing all risk, and stakeholder is distinguished from shareholder. Constructed, unscored.

## 1.2.3 — Franchise, social enterprise and multinational

**Requirement (§1.2.3, p.9):** franchises; social enterprises; multinationals.

**Teach it.** A franchise allows a franchisee to use a franchisor’s brand/system under an agreement, typically paying fees or a share of revenue and following operating standards. A social enterprise trades to achieve a social/environmental purpose and uses its resources/surplus to support that purpose; it may still earn profit. A multinational operates in more than one country, through owned operations, subsidiaries or other arrangements. These are organisational forms/characteristics that can overlap: a multinational may use franchises, and a social enterprise can operate internationally.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** Classify and explain: a local operator pays to run a branded café to the parent company’s rules; a recycling venture sells services and directs surplus to reduce local waste; a clothing company manufactures and sells through subsidiaries in four countries.

**Answer / rationale.** Franchise; social enterprise; multinational. In the first, brand/system rights and contractual standards matter; in the second, the purpose and use of surplus distinguish it from a conventional profit-only description; in the third, operations span countries. Organisations can fit more than one description, so classification follows the facts.

**Checked record:** all three forms explicitly tested; practice does not imply they are mutually exclusive. Spec p.9; constructed and unscored.

## 1.3.1 — Primary, secondary and tertiary sectors

**Requirement (§1.3.1, p.9):** primary extraction; secondary conversion into finished/semi-finished goods; tertiary provision of services.

**Teach it.** Classify the activity performed, not merely the company name or end customer. Primary activity extracts raw materials from the earth (e.g. mining, fishing, forestry). Secondary activity processes those materials into semi-finished or finished goods (e.g. milling timber into panels or assembling furniture). Tertiary activity provides services (e.g. freight, retail, banking, healthcare). A supply chain may include all three sectors.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** Trace one timber desk: (1) a company harvests trees; (2) a factory cuts and assembles the desk; (3) a logistics firm delivers it; (4) a retailer sells it.

**Answer / rationale.** (1) Primary extraction; (2) secondary conversion/manufacture; (3) tertiary transport service; (4) tertiary retail service. The log is raw material, the desk is the manufactured good, and delivery/sale are services. Rationale: identify the actual activity at each stage. Spec p.9; constructed, unscored.

## 1.4.1 — Location and relocation decisions

**Requirement (§1.4.1, p.10):** proximity to market, labour, materials and competitors; nature of activity; internet/e-commerce and fixed premises; legal controls and trade blocs.

**Teach it.** Location is a trade-off, not simply “near customers.” Compare transport and rent with access to buyers, suitable workers, inputs and competitor clusters. The activity matters: a mine is tied to a resource; a retailer values customer access; a data service may be less tied to a high-street site, although connectivity and skilled labour still matter. E-commerce can widen reach and reduce the need for customer-facing premises, but distribution, returns, warehousing and digital access still have locations/costs. Zoning, safety, environmental or employment rules can restrict sites; trade-bloc membership/rules can change the cost and ease of serving a regional market. The best site depends on the firm’s product, customers and cost structure.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A furniture maker is choosing between Site North, inside a fictional trade bloc and near timber suppliers, skilled machinists and several rival furniture makers, and Site South, outside the bloc with lower rent. Most customers live in three bloc countries; under the case rules qualifying goods made inside the bloc enter member markets without an 8% tariff, while imports from outside face that tariff. Site North’s workshop may require an environmental permit. The company will keep online sales. Recommend a location approach and discuss proximity to customers, labour, materials **and competitors**, activity, e-commerce/fixed premises, legal controls and trade-bloc effects.

**Answer / rationale.** Site North may give access to a skilled labour pool, suppliers and industry services; a competitor cluster may also improve information/supplier access, but intensify competition for workers and customers. Site South’s lower rent and any advantages in materials must be compared with freight, recruitment and the stated 8% export tariff. Locating within the bloc can improve market access only if the product meets the rules of origin; location alone does not guarantee tariff-free status. E-commerce widens customer reach but does not remove delivery/warehouse costs. Check the permit before committing. Recommend only after comparing total costs and market access; a smaller showroom or online-first model may balance reach and cost. Spec p.10; every listed factor is directly prompted and answered; tariff is a fictional case assumption, not a claim about a real bloc; unscored.

## 1.5.1 — Globalisation: opportunities and threats

**Requirement (§1.5.1, p.10):** concept of globalisation; opportunities and threats for businesses.

**Teach it.** Globalisation is the increasing integration/interdependence of economies and markets through trade, investment, technology, communication and movement of goods/services. For a business it can increase access to customers, suppliers, workers, finance and ideas; it can also expose the business to more competitors, supply-chain disruption, exchange-rate changes, differing regulations and reputational risk. Benefits and threats depend on the firm’s capabilities and the countries/markets involved.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A Dhaka-based software company begins selling subscriptions overseas and hires a cloud provider in another country. State two opportunities and two threats, linking each to this company.

**Answer / rationale.** Opportunities: a larger customer base can raise sales; overseas cloud expertise/capacity can help it serve users. Threats: global rivals may compete on price/features; a foreign-provider outage, data/regulatory issue or currency movement can raise cost/disrupt service. Valid alternatives accepted when causally linked. Spec p.10; constructed, unscored.

## 1.5.2 — Multinationals: business and host-economy effects

**Requirement (§1.5.2, p.10):** importance/growth of multinationals; benefits to the business of becoming one; benefits and possible drawbacks to the host country/economy.

**Teach it.** A multinational expands activity across borders. The business may reach new customers, spread risk, obtain inputs/skills, gain economies of scale or locate nearer a market; it also takes on investment, coordination, political, cultural and currency risks. A host economy may gain jobs, training, tax revenue, infrastructure, exports and supplier demand. Possible drawbacks include pressure on local firms, profit repatriation, environmental/resource costs, dependence on a large employer or bargaining power over government. Outcomes depend on conduct, regulation and local linkages; none is automatic.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A large battery producer plans a plant in a lower-income host country. Explain two benefits to the company, two potential host-economy benefits and two possible drawbacks for the host. Recommend one condition the host government should negotiate.

**Answer / rationale.** Company benefits could include access to a new market and lower logistics cost near customers, balanced against setup/political risk. Host benefits could include jobs/training and supplier/tax/export activity. Drawbacks could include pollution/resource strain or profit repatriation/competitive pressure on local firms. A credible condition might require environmental safeguards, training/local supplier opportunities or transparent tax compliance. Judge the conditions by enforcement and costs. Spec p.10; constructed, unscored.

## 1.5.3 — Exchange-rate calculations

**Requirement (§1.5.3, p.10):** calculate currency exchange using the rate and correct direction.

**Teach it.** Read the quote carefully. If £1 = US$1.25, then pounds ×1.25 gives dollars, while dollars ÷1.25 gives pounds. Keep the requested currency in the answer, show the operation and state any rounding. A quoted rate may differ in real transactions because of fees/spreads; use the rate the question supplies.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** The given rate is £1 = US$1.25. Convert (a) £80 to US dollars and (b) US$150 to pounds. Ignore fees.

**Answer / recalculation.** (a) £80×US$1.25/£ = **US$100**. (b) US$150÷(US$1.25/£) = **£120**. Units cancel to the requested currency. Do not multiply in both directions. Spec p.10; constructed, unscored.

## 1.5.4 — Exchange-rate changes and competitiveness

**Requirement (§1.5.4, p.10):** effects of rate changes on international competitiveness, importers and exporters.

**Teach it.** If the home currency appreciates, imported inputs generally cost less in home currency, but home exports become more expensive to foreign buyers at unchanged home prices; exporters may become less competitive. Depreciation generally makes imports more expensive but can make exports cheaper abroad. The actual effect depends on contract currency, hedging, pass-through, imported-input content, demand and competitor prices. Keep the perspective explicit: currency “up” is good or bad only in relation to the transaction.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A UK exporter charges £100 per item. Initially £1=US$1.20; later £1=US$1.50. A UK importer buys a US$150 component. Calculate the foreign price of the export and home-currency cost of the import at each rate, then interpret the appreciation.

**Answer / recalculation.** Export: £100 costs US$120 initially and US$150 later. Import: US$150 costs £125 initially (150/1.20) and £100 later (150/1.50). Sterling appreciation makes the imported component cheaper in pounds but the export dearer to US buyers if the UK price stays £100; imported-content exporters may have some offsetting cost reduction. Competitiveness is not determined by the exchange rate alone. Spec p.10; rates/figures constructed and recalculated.

## 1.6.1 — Government spending, public services and taxation

**Requirement (§1.6.1, p.10):** public services; taxation and constraints on public spending.

**Teach it.** Government spending can provide education, healthcare, transport and other services that support workers, customers and businesses. Taxes raise revenue but affect household disposable income, business costs/incentives and investment decisions depending on the tax. Public spending is constrained by available revenue, borrowing choices, debt-service costs and competing priorities. A contract or grant may benefit one business, while tax/regulation changes may affect many; do not treat spending as limitless or all taxes as having the same incidence.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A government considers funding technical-college courses and a road upgrade using higher business taxes. Explain a possible business benefit, a possible cost, and why the government may not fund every proposed project.

**Answer / rationale.** Courses may improve the supply of trained labour; the road may reduce delivery time/cost. Higher taxes can reduce retained profit/investment or be passed into prices, depending on the tax and market. Limited revenue, borrowing/debt costs and competing public needs force choices. Answers must distinguish potential effects from guarantees. Spec p.10; constructed, unscored.

## 1.6.2 — Infrastructure, legislation and trade policy

**Requirement (§1.6.2, p.10):** infrastructure provision; legislation; trade policy including trading blocs and tariffs.

**Teach it.** Infrastructure (transport, energy, communications) can lower operating/distribution costs and widen labour/customer access, although businesses may pay tax or user charges. Legislation establishes obligations such as safety, employment, consumer or environmental standards: compliance adds cost but can protect people, trust and fair competition. Trading blocs can reduce barriers among members and standardise some rules; tariffs are taxes on imports that can raise import prices, protect domestic producers, or provoke retaliation/raise input costs. Effects vary by business position in the supply chain and the policy design.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A furniture retailer imports timber from a country outside a regional trading bloc, sells to member-country customers, and uses road freight. Explain one effect each of (a) improved roads, (b) a new product-safety law and (c) a tariff on its imported timber.

**Answer / rationale.** Roads may shorten/cheapen deliveries; safety compliance may require testing/changes but reduce harm and build customer trust; tariff raises landed timber cost, possibly squeezing margin or raising prices, while benefiting competing domestic suppliers. The tariff effect on this importer is not identical to its effect on a local timber producer. Spec p.10; constructed, unscored.

## 1.6.3 — Interest rates: business and consumer spending

**Requirement (§1.6.3, p.10):** effects of interest rates on businesses and consumer spending.

**Teach it.** Higher rates usually raise interest costs on variable-rate borrowing and make new borrowing less attractive; they can also increase returns on savings. Households with debt may have less disposable income, and consumers may defer credit-financed purchases. A business can face weaker demand as well as higher finance costs; savers may spend more if income rises. Effects vary with debt structure, savings, demand sensitivity and timing; a rate rise does not affect every business equally.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A furniture retailer has a variable-rate bank loan and sells many items on customer credit. Explain two channels through which a rate rise could affect its profit.

**Answer / rationale.** Its loan interest expense may increase, reducing profit if other factors stay constant. Customers may cut credit purchases as repayments rise, lowering the retailer’s sales/revenue and possibly profit. A higher rate could benefit any cash savings, but do not assume this offsets the two stated exposures. Spec p.10; constructed, unscored.

## 1.7.1 — Social, technological, environmental and political factors

**Requirement (§1.7.1, p.10):** external social, technological, environmental and political factors affecting business decisions.

**Teach it.** Social changes (demographics, values, lifestyles) can change demand or workforce expectations. Technology can create new sales/production methods or make existing products/processes obsolete. Environmental conditions, expectations and rules can affect materials, waste, energy, location and reputation. Political decisions/stability, policy and trade relationships can change regulation, taxation, market access and uncertainty. These are external to a single firm but may be anticipated and responded to; identify a specific factor, decision and likely consequence.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A snack producer faces (1) rising demand for lower-sugar foods, (2) a new packaging technology, (3) pressure to reduce plastic waste, and (4) an election with proposed changes to food labelling and import rules. For each, name the factor category and one decision it could influence.

**Answer / rationale.** (1) Social—product formulation/marketing; (2) technological—equipment or packaging investment; (3) environmental—materials/waste choices; (4) political—compliance, sourcing or market-entry plans. The election proposal is uncertain until enacted, so scenario planning is more defensible than treating it as current law. Spec p.10; constructed, unscored.

## 1.8.1 — Measuring business success

**Requirement (§1.8.1, p.11):** revenue, market share, customer satisfaction, profit, growth, owner/shareholder satisfaction and employee satisfaction.

**Teach it.** Success is multidimensional. Revenue measures sales income, not profit; market share compares the firm’s sales with the total market; customer satisfaction can indicate repeat purchase but must be measured reliably; profit is a surplus after costs, while growth can refer to scale, sales or capacity. Owners/shareholders may value returns, risk and long-term value; employees may value pay, security, conditions and development. Measures can conflict: rapid growth can reduce short-term profit, and higher prices can raise revenue per sale while reducing volume or satisfaction. Compare measures over time and against objectives/context.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A chain reports revenue up 12%, market share up 2 percentage points and profit down 8%; customer complaints rise; employee turnover falls; shareholders receive a higher dividend but the share price falls. Assess whether the chain is successful.

**Answer / rationale.** The evidence is mixed: sales/share and employee retention improved, but profit, complaints and share price worsened; dividend may satisfy shareholders now but falling share price may concern them. Need clarify time period, market growth, satisfaction data and the owners’ objectives. A supported conclusion should weigh several measures rather than equate revenue growth with overall success. Spec p.11; constructed, unscored.

## 1.8.2 — Reasons businesses fail

**Requirement (§1.8.2, p.11):** cash-flow problems/lack of finance; **not competitive**; failure to adapt to changes in the market.

**Teach it.** Insolvency can occur when a business cannot pay debts as they fall due, even if it reports accounting profit; insufficient external/internal finance can prevent it meeting obligations or investing. A business that is not competitive may lose customers because its price, quality, service or offer compares poorly with alternatives. Failure to adapt to changing customer needs, technology or market conditions can make an offer obsolete. These causes can interact, but do not misstate the specification as “weak competition”: the business itself is not competitive.

**Practice — constructed, unscored.** A toy shop has positive accounting profit but waits 90 days for customers to pay, must pay suppliers in 30 days and has little cash/reserve. Online rivals offer a wider range; the shop continues selling toys that its customers no longer want. Identify and explain each of the three possible failure causes.

**Answer / rationale.** Cash-flow problem/lack of finance: payment timing creates a 60-day gap, so bills may fall due before receipts; positive profit does not ensure cash solvency. Not competitive: narrower choice than online rivals may lead customers elsewhere. Failure to adapt: continuing an unwanted range ignores changed demand. These may reinforce one another; the case does not prove actual insolvency. Spec p.11; constructed, unscored.
