# Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Business (4BS1)
## Official student exemplars and full-credit model answers

**Scope:** the searchable archive’s 38 4BS1 question papers, Papers 1 and 2 including R papers, from May/June 2019 to June 2026. The bank combines published student scripts and examiner commentary with clearly labelled models built from the matching mark schemes. It is for **International GCSE Business 4BS1**, not UK GCSE Business 1BS0 or another exam board.

## How to read the labels

- **OFFICIAL — full marks:** a real candidate response published by Pearson and awarded all marks for that item.
- **OFFICIAL — not full marks:** a real candidate response and examiner feedback. These are useful comparisons, not “perfect” answers.
- **MARK-SCHEME answer:** accepted content or worked answer in Pearson’s mark scheme; not necessarily a student script.
- **CONSTRUCTED full-credit target:** an original model written from the exact question, mark scheme and examiner guidance. It is **not** an official student answer, and no one can promise an exact mark from a constructed script. It shows the features a full-credit response should contain.

“Perfect” does not mean fancy English or one compulsory wording. Pearson accepts other valid answers; level-based answers are judged against the descriptors as a whole.

| Marks | Common command / family | What full-credit work generally does |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Multiple choice; define; state/identify; occasional one-mark numerical item | Gives one correct answer. A definition must be precise; a state response may need business context. |
| 2 | Calculate; outline one reason/benefit/method | A calculation shows substitution/working and the final answer. An outline gives one clear point plus a relevant detail or contextual link. |
| 3 | Explain one reason/way/benefit/disadvantage/impact | Identifies one point and develops how or why it leads to a consequence. Avoid listing several undeveloped points. |
| 6 | Analyse / assess / how: importance, benefits, usefulness, impact, reasons | Applies the idea to the business and develops logical cause-and-effect. Usually make two relevant, developed chains; a conclusion is not normally the main demand. |
| 9 | Justify which of two options to choose/use | Make the choice clear, develop relevant business-context reasoning, weigh a limitation or alternative, and justify the final decision. The two options do **not** need equal coverage. |
| 12 | Evaluate | Develop applied arguments, consider a limitation/counterargument, and finish with a supported judgement. In 4BS1 these questions can test all four assessment objectives; use the exact question’s mark scheme. |

### Variations within the level-based questions

- **6 marks:** “Analyse why/how…”, “Analyse the importance…”, “Analyse the benefits…”, “Analyse the usefulness…”, or “Analyse the impact…”. The wording changes the focus, but application and developed reasoning remain essential.
- **9 marks:** the archive repeatedly uses **“Justify which one of these two options…”**. The options can be about finance, motivation, marketing/research, pricing, production/quality, or growth. The answer method is the same; the best context-specific reasons change.
- **12 marks:** the archive uses “Evaluate the importance/benefits/impact/effects…”, “Evaluate the factors…”, “Evaluate whether…”, “Evaluate the extent to which…”, and “Evaluate why/how…”. Do not force one memorised essay onto all of them: answer the exact focus, balance it, and make a conditional judgement.

## 2. Literal first-sentence starters

You do **not** need an introduction. Start by answering the command word.

- **Define:** “A stakeholder is …”
- **State:** “One method is …”
- **Outline:** “One reason for **[business]** is …”
- **Explain:** “One way **[business]** could … is …”
- **Analyse:** “One benefit/impact of **[X]** for **[business]** is …”
- **Justify:** “**[Business]** should choose **[Option]** because …”
- **Evaluate:** “**[X]** can be important/effective for **[business]** because …” or “One important factor is …”

The opening can be plain. A direct first sentence is usually clearer than starting with “The business is very large…” or repeating the question.

## 3. Short-mark answer bank

### 1 mark: define, state or choose

**Define — MARK-SCHEME answer (2019 P1, Q1(b)):**

> Stakeholders are individuals or groups with an interest in the success or failure of a business.

**State — MARK-SCHEME answer (2019 P1, Q1(d)):**

> Shadowing a fellow shoe fitter.

The second answer earns its mark because the question asks for an on-the-job training method in the shoe-shop context. A generic answer may not be enough when the paper asks for context.

**Multiple choice — official QP/MS example:** in 2019 P1 Q1(a)(i), the method of secondary market research is **government reports**. The paper’s correct option is B. In the exam, select the option; do not spend time explaining it unless asked.

### 2 marks: calculate or outline

**Calculate — MARK-SCHEME answer (2019 P1, Q4(a)): markup on a 2,400 Ks pair of shoes sold for 3,200 Ks.**

1. Profit per pair = 3,200 − 2,400 = 800 Ks.
2. Markup = 800 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **33.3%** (exactly 33.33…%; Pearson’s mark scheme displays **33%**).

The scheme awards one mark for correct substitution into the formula and one for the numerical answer; the correct answer without working can also receive both marks. Still show the working, so you can earn the method mark if the final figure is wrong.

**Outline — CONSTRUCTED full-credit target (2023 P2, IKEA reading government reports):**

> A government report could tell IKEA that income tax is going to increase. If people have less disposable income, they may be less likely to buy IKEA furniture.

This gives one reason and links it to IKEA’s customers and products. The exact mark scheme accepts other relevant, contextualised reasons.

### 3 marks: explain one point

**Question type:** explain one way, reason, benefit, disadvantage or impact. Start with the point, then develop it. Do not use all three marks to list three separate ideas.

**CONSTRUCTED full-credit target (2023 P2, effective use of resources):**

> Using resources effectively can reduce waste. IKEA may then need to hold less inventory, which lowers storage expenses for the business.

The matching mark scheme allows a chain such as effective resource use → less waste → less inventory or lower expenses. A different valid chain can also earn full marks.

**OFFICIAL — full-mark student examples:**

- 2019 P1 Q1(f), **3/3**: a student uses reducing cost of sales/lean production and links less waste and fewer raw materials to a higher gross profit margin. See the local Pearson booklet, PDF page 6.
- 2019 P1 Q2(d), **3/3**: a student links a television advert to reaching a large audience, influencing purchases and increasing sales/revenue. See PDF page 11. The examiner notes that the candidate’s opening definition was unnecessary; the response could begin directly with the method.

### 6 marks: analyse one or more developed chains

**Question type:** analyse why/how something matters, its benefits/usefulness, or its impact. Use details from the business; move beyond “this is good” to explain what happens next and why it matters to the business.

**CONSTRUCTED full-credit target (2023 P2, IKEA franchises):**

> One benefit of franchising is that IKEA can sell its flat-pack tables and wardrobes around the world. This gives more customers access to IKEA’s products, so the business can make sales in more markets and grow. Each franchisee also pays IKEA a percentage of its profit for using the IKEA name and furniture. Income from many stores adds to IKEA’s revenue and wealth, making the business more successful.

This follows the matching mark scheme’s two useful routes: worldwide sales of IKEA products, and franchisees paying a percentage of profit for the brand/furniture, creating revenue for IKEA. It is a constructed target, not an official script.

**OFFICIAL — full-mark student examples:**

- 2019 P1 Q1(g), **6/6**: importance of satisfying customer needs; the candidate applies the shoe-shop setting and develops customer trust/loyalty and word-of-mouth into stronger sales and profit. PDF page 8.
- 2019 P1 Q4(b), **6/6**: usefulness of profitability ratios; the candidate explains what the business can learn and how it could use the information in decisions. PDF page 28.

The selected pages from Pearson’s 2019 P2 booklet are saved locally at `Sources/Official_Pearson/June2019_P2_Exemplar_Responses_Selected_Pages.pdf`. They include a full-mark 3/3 response for Q1(f) (original PDF p. 6), Q1(g) examples at 5/6 and 3/6 (pp. 8–9), Q2(d) examples at 2/3 (pp. 10–11), Q2(f) at 7/9 and 4/9 (pp. 13–14), Q3(e) at 6/9 and 5/9 (pp. 19–20), Q4(c) at 8/12 and 7/12 (pp. 25–28), and a Paper 2R Q1(g) at 5/6 (p. 30). ## 4. Nine-mark “Justify which option” bank

### What is distinctive about 9 marks?

The question asks for a decision. Do not begin with a long definition of each option. State your choice early, develop why it fits this business, and explain why it is better than—or preferable despite—the alternative. A conclusion should add a reason or condition, not merely repeat the choice.

**Starter:** “IKEA should choose job enrichment because …”

**CONSTRUCTED full-credit target — exact May 2023 P2 Q3(e), bonus or job enrichment:**

> IKEA should choose job enrichment to motivate its factory workers. It could train carpenters to take on a wider role, such as working in the packing department as well as making tables and chairs. Learning new skills and having more responsibility may make repetitive work more interesting and help employees feel valued. They may be more willing to stay at IKEA, helping the business retain experienced staff and avoid some recruitment and training costs.
>
> Bonuses could also encourage carpenters to make more tables and chairs, and the extra pay may persuade some workers to remain. However, some employees may not want the extra effort needed to earn a bonus. Job enrichment is not guaranteed to motivate everyone either, because some workers may prefer additional pay to more responsibility.
>
> Overall, job enrichment is the better long-term choice if IKEA wants to retain workers and develop skills that could support promotion. If its employees mainly value extra pay or IKEA needs an immediate increase in output, a bonus might work better. The choice therefore depends on what is currently causing the motivation or retention problem.

This model uses the exact mark-scheme context: carpenters, tables/chairs, training into packing, retention, skills and promotion, plus the possible preference for remuneration. It is **constructed from the MS/ER**, not an official 9/9 candidate script.

### Retrieved examiner examples

- **OFFICIAL — 9/9 student answer:** 2019 P1 Q3(e), choosing a source of finance for the shoe retailer. Pearson’s comments praise detailed application, a balanced argument about the two options, logical chains and a clearly justified judgement. Open the local 2019 exemplar booklet at PDF page 23 for the marker’s comments and the surrounding pages for the student response.
- **OFFICIAL — 7/9:** 2019 P1 Q2(f), choosing a motivation method. Useful near-full comparison; examiner says the judgement needed further development. PDF page 15.
- **OFFICIAL — 5/9 exact P2 comparator:** May 2023 P2 Q3(e), IKEA motivation. The report says the response covered both options but was not developed enough and had limited evaluation. It notes there are no separate knowledge marks for this question type; the answer must justify the selected option in context. The 6-page excerpt is saved locally.

Different topics—finance, motivation, price, production, research or growth—do not require different essay machinery. The reason and case details change; the “choice → chain → weigh up → supported decision” shape remains useful.

## 5. Twelve-mark “Evaluate” bank

### Four recurring prompt families

| Prompt family | What the answer must decide |
|---|---|
| Importance / benefits / usefulness | How valuable is the idea to this business, and under what conditions? |
| Factors / decision criteria | Which factors matter most, and how should the business weigh them? |
| Whether / extent / guarantee | Does the proposal help, how far, and what could stop it succeeding? |
| Impact / effects / why / how | What positive and negative consequences follow, and which are most significant? |

**A dependable shape:** direct answer to the focus → developed, applied argument → another distinct argument → limitation/counterargument → conclusion that prioritises and states a condition. Do not simply define the term, repeat the question, or write a one-sided list.

### Model A — importance of demographic segmentation (exact May 2023 P2 Q4(c))

**CONSTRUCTED full-credit target; built from the exact QP/MS/ER.**

> Demographic segmentation can help IKEA target customers more effectively because age, income and family size affect what furniture people need and can afford. For example, families with young children may need reasonably priced tables and chairs that are suitable for children. If IKEA designs and promotes products for these families, the range is more likely to meet their needs, which could increase customer satisfaction and sales.
>
> Income can also differ between countries. In a country with more high-income customers, IKEA could offer more expensive-looking tables and ornaments. Older customers may value reliable, comfortable chairs. Adapting the range to these groups could make IKEA’s stores more relevant to local customers and help the business compete.
>
> However, demographic groups do not all have the same preferences. Not every family has the same needs, and there is no guarantee that customers will buy what IKEA expects. If IKEA predicts demand badly, some products could remain unsold and increase costs. Age and income also do not reveal every customer’s tastes, so demographics alone may not be enough.
>
> Overall, demographic segmentation is important as a starting point because it helps IKEA identify large groups and tailor its products. It will be more useful if IKEA combines it with market research and customer feedback rather than assuming everyone in a group is alike. Its value depends on how accurate the data is and whether IKEA can adapt its range without creating excessive costs.

The exact-paper examiner report has a **10/12** candidate response: developed knowledge, application and analysis, but limited judgement. The local report excerpt shows it. Compare that real script with this constructed model; do not mistake the constructed wording for an official examiner answer.

### Model B — factors affecting factory location (May/June 2019 P2 Q4(c))

**CONSTRUCTED full-credit target based on the exact MS.**

> Tata Motor Company should consider whether a location has the skilled workers needed for a vehicle factory. The company needs employees such as engineers and designers. Locating near a suitable labour force could make recruitment easier and reduce some operating costs, helping the factory run efficiently.
>
> Transport infrastructure is also important. Tata needs a continuous supply of materials, and finished vehicles must be taken to their next destination. A site near a port, motorway or railway could make deliveries more reliable and reduce delays in moving cars to market.
>
> However, a location with skilled workers and good infrastructure may have higher land or infrastructure costs. Those extra costs could offset savings from easier recruitment and transport. Location is also only one factor in the success of a factory; other costs and business needs may matter more at a particular site.
>
> Overall, Tata should prioritise a reliable skilled workforce and transport links, but compare their benefits with the total cost of the site. These factors are most important if they prevent production or delivery problems; if a well-connected site is much more expensive, a cheaper site with adequate labour and transport could be the better choice.

The matching mark scheme accepts skilled labour, access for deliveries, engineers/designers, ports/motorways/rail, and evaluates higher land/infrastructure costs and the relative importance of other factors.

### Model C — whether to become a private limited company (May/June 2020 P1 Q4(c))

**CONSTRUCTED full-credit target based on the exact MS.**

> Becoming a private limited company could help TADS grow because Ollie may be able to raise money by selling shares privately to family and friends. The extra finance could be used to expand the music business. A company also has a separate legal identity, so TADS could continue if something happened to Ollie. Limited liability would protect Ollie’s personal possessions if the company ran into debt, reducing the risk of expansion.
>
> However, Ollie may not attract enough investors to raise the amount he needs. Selling shares would also mean sharing ownership and some control. Becoming a limited company brings more paperwork and requires some information to be made public, which competitors might find useful. If the expansion needs only a modest amount, a bank loan may be simpler and allow Ollie to keep full control.
>
> Overall, becoming a private limited company is worthwhile if TADS needs significant finance for a realistic growth plan and Ollie is willing to share ownership. If he needs only a small amount or strongly values control, a loan may be a better option. The decision depends on the scale of expansion and Ollie’s willingness to accept the extra responsibilities of limited-company status.

### A retrieved 12/12 answer

**OFFICIAL — full marks:** June 2024 P1 Q4(c), evaluating the importance of marketing to PPS so it can keep market share. Pearson awarded the real candidate response **12/12**. Its examiner comments praise the balanced, contextual argument, connected reasoning, and a conclusion that uses an “it depends” judgement. The actual script and comments are in `Sources/Official_Pearson/June2024_P1_Examiners_Report_Excerpts_12mark_Evaluate.pdf` (three-page excerpt).

This is a strong calibration example for impact/importance/benefit-style 12-mark prompts. Use the exact question wording and the answer script in the PDF; this guide does not present a reconstructed transcript as the candidate’s words.
