# Part 1 — The assessment, precisely

## Paper structure

| | **Paper 1: Investigating Small Businesses** | **Paper 2: Investigating Large Businesses** |
|---|---|---|
| Paper code | 4BS1/01 | 4BS1/02 |
| Weighting | **50%** of the total International GCSE | **50%** |
| Length | **1 hour 30 minutes** | **1 hour 30 minutes** |
| Marks | **80** | **80** |
| Per-question marks | **20 · 20 · 20 · 20** *(verified on all 38 papers)* | same |
| Structure | **four compulsory questions, each worth 20 marks** | same |
| Case study | a **small business — up to 49 employees** | a **large business — more than 250 employees** |
| Availability | June | June |

> *"Paper 1 considers the specification content from the angle of a small business and Paper 2 considers the specification from the perspective of a large business."*

### ⭐⭐ Two structural facts that should shape every revision session

**1. Every paper is four questions of exactly 20 marks each.** So the 20 marks of Question 1 and the 20 marks of Question 4 are **worth the same**. Questions are *ramped* — they get harder — but they are **not weighted**. **A mark is a mark wherever you find it.**

> *"Initially the examination paper asks questions testing Knowledge (AO1) and Application (AO2) but increase in difficulty towards the end of each question… an increased number of questions are presented that test the higher-order skills of Analysis (AO3) and Evaluation (AO4)."*

✅ **AND IT IS TRUE OF THE MARK SCHEMES, NOT JUST OF THE GUIDE — verified, 38 of 38.** Every archived paper splits **20 · 20 · 20 · 20**, with **no paper scoring a question at anything other than 20** (`python3 ../build_layer_c_tariffs.py` prints the check). **That is a stronger statement than it looks: an 80-mark paper could have been 25 / 15 / 20 / 20 and still totalled 80 — and none of them is.** So **every question is internally identical**, which is the reason the tariff skeleton never varies and the reason a technique learned on Question 1 transfers whole to Question 4.

**2. There is no choice.** *"four compulsory questions."* You cannot skip a topic. And: *"Questions can be taken from any part of the specification… it appeared that some candidates had not covered the full specification."*

## Assessment objectives

| AO | Description | **Weighting** |
|---|---|---|
| **AO1** Knowledge | Recall, select and communicate knowledge and understanding of business terms, concepts and issues. | **27–28%**\* |
| **AO2** Application | Apply knowledge and understanding using appropriate business terms, concepts, theories and calculations effectively in **specific contexts**. | **38–39%** |
| **AO3** Analysis | Select, organise and interpret business information from sources to investigate and analyse issues. | **22–23%** |
| **AO4** Evaluation | Evaluate business information to make reasoned judgements and draw conclusions. | **11–12%** |
| | | **100%** |

> \* *"A maximum of 15% of total qualification marks will assess knowledge recall."*

### 🎯 What the weighting means in marks

Out of a full 160-mark qualification:

| AO | Marks (approx.) | Which is |
|---|---|---|
| **AO2 Application** | **~62** | the single biggest block |
| **AO1 Knowledge** | **~44** | but only **24** of those can be plain recall |
| **AO3 Analysis** | **~36** | |
| **AO4 Evaluation** | **~18** | the smallest — but the one that decides Level 3 |

**AO2 is worth nearly as much as AO1 and AO3 combined.** That single fact explains why context appears in every rule in this bank.

> **And note the recall cap:** of AO1's ~44 marks, at most **15% × 160 = 24 marks** test straight recall. The remaining ~20 AO1 marks require **selecting and communicating** knowledge inside an answer. **Knowledge you have not deployed earns nothing** — which is exactly what the examiners say, report after report.

| **4.3.1 Product** | *"**There is no requirement to consider the profitability of the product** over the product life cycle."* |
| **5.1.1 Economies of scale** | *"There is **no requirement for this topic area to be taught in depth** with every conceivable source of economies of scale considered… **Two examples will be enough** e.g. bulk buying, managerial economies of scale."* |

# Part 3 — Topic-by-topic: exactly what is required

Condensed from the guide's Content Guidance. **The precise wording matters** — these are the definitions and distinctions the examiners will mark against.

## Theme 3 — Business Finance

### 3.1.3 External sources of finance
Know **advantages and disadvantages** of: overdrafts, trade payables, loan capital, share capital, venture capital, crowdfunding. Also **why a company may decide to float on a stock market**. Be able to **classify short-term vs long-term** and choose the *appropriate* source for a situation.

> Pearson's own worked example: *"Using a **bank loan, rather than an overdraft**, to fund the purchase of an expensive building that the business is expected to hold for a **long period**."*

### 3.2 Cash flow
> *"poor cash flow is **the main reason a business may fail**"* and *"Students critically need to recognise that **cash and profit are related, but not identical concepts**."*

> *"• **Profit is recorded straight away after a sale**
> • **Cash (inflows and outflows) is recorded when it is received or spent.**"*

**The guide's own example:** a website developer builds a site with 60-day payment terms. The sale is recorded as **profit immediately**, but **cash outflows begin at once** and the cash inflow arrives after 60 days.

> **This is the examinable version of the cash-vs-profit distinction — learn the 60-day example, it is the one Pearson chose.**

### 3.3 Costs and break-even — the formulae
Must be able to calculate:

| Formula | |
|---|---|
| Revenue | selling price × sales volume |
| Fixed costs | sum of all fixed costs |
| Variable costs | variable cost per unit × quantity sold |
| Total costs | fixed costs + variable costs |
| Profit / loss | revenue − total costs |
| Contribution per unit | selling price − variable cost per unit |
| **Break-even output** | **total fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit** |

- Fixed costs include: *"rent, advertising, insurance, interest on bank loans and salaries."*
- Variable costs include: *"raw materials, wages and packaging."*
- *"Students should **show the units** for any calculations."*

**Effect to know:** *"consider the effects of the business increasing the selling price of its product (**break-even output will fall**)."*

### 3.5.1 Accounting ratios
*"Students should know the correct units for these calculations and that **ratios have no units**."*

### 3.5.2 Liquidity
> *"Liquidity is the ability for a business to **meet its current liabilities with its current assets**."* Assessed using the **current ratio** and the **acid-test ratio**.

> *"a low acid-test ratio may indicate that the business is **holding too much inventory (stock)**, especially if the current ratio is healthy."*

## Theme 4 — Marketing

### 4.1.2 Methods of market research
- **Primary:** *"survey, questionnaire, focus group and observation"* — plus a **test market**.
- **Social media** as a means of collecting market research data — *"for example through feedback from Facebook and Twitter accounts."*
- **Secondary:** *"data from other sources, such as MINTEL, and individual national government reports"* — plus the Internet and market reports.
- **Quantitative:** *"numerical and can be analysed statistically."*
- *"Small businesses are unlikely to be able afford extensive market research exercises."*

### 4.1.3 Qualitative data
> *"Qualitative data comes from **interviews and in-depth studies**, where the information gathered **cannot easily be converted to data**."*

### 4.2.1 Marketing and orientation
- **Market orientation** = *"giving the consumer the most highly valued mix of goods and services"* → builds relationships → **customer/brand loyalty** → **market share at the expense of its rivals**.
- Know the difference between **product orientation** and **market orientation**.
- **Niche** = *"focusing on a small specialised market segment"* — *"a strategy that small businesses tend to follow due to their lack of economies of scale."*
- **Mass** = *"much broader, larger market segments"* — *"normally more competitive… therefore **price tends to become a critical component**."*

### 4.2.3 Segmentation
> *"the division of a market into groups, each of which has **distinct customer preferences**. Segments to be considered are by **location, demographics, lifestyle, income and age**."*

**Five segments — memorise the list.**

### 4.3.2 Price — all five strategies, defined

| Strategy | Pearson's definition |
|---|---|
| **Cost plus** | *"adding a percentage mark-up on to the average total cost of the product. This is a simple pricing strategy that **does not take into consideration the market** (e.g. the amount of competition, willingness to pay etc.)"* |
| **Penetration** | *"pricing at a level **close to average total cost or below** average total cost to allow the product to **build market share**. Usually used for **new products** that have just entered the market."* |
| **Competitive** | *"used in markets where there is **lots of competition**. Involves **low prices with small profit margins** since to price at a higher level would involve **loss of sales to competitors**."* |
| **Skimming** | *"normally used when the product has **significant USPs/differentiation** and the product **can be priced at a high level with a large profit margin**, perhaps because there is **limited competition** when the product was first launched."* |
| **Promotional** | *"**short-term discounts** to the price of the product to try and encourage initial sales or increase sales for a specific period of time. The price charged will be **close to the average total cost or below it**."* |

> ⭐ **This directly answers the Nov 2021 failure**, where only a third of the cohort could define **price skimming** and candidates *"confused this strategy with other pricing strategies"*. **Skimming = high price, significant USP, limited competition at launch.** Do not confuse it with penetration (low price to build share) — they are opposites.

**Life-cycle link:** *"in the **decline** phase it is likely that **promotional pricing** will be used as an extension strategy, whereas in the **introduction** phase **skimming or penetration** pricing may be used."*

### 4.3.3 Place
> *"Place involves the **method of distribution** used to get the product to the consumer. **Place does not mean geographic location.**"*

Only two methods are in scope: **retailers** and **etailing (direct supply)**. **Retailing** suits products needing immediate purchase (*"food"*) or advice/comparison (*"electrical products"*).

### 4.3.4 Promotion
Channels: **advertising, sponsorship, product trials, special offers and branding**.

> *"The aim of promotion is to encourage sales but also to **enhance the perception of the product so that a higher price can be achieved**."*

> *"**Public relations are not trying to promote a specific product but provide a positive brand image for the whole business.**"*

**Use of technology in promotion** — a low fixed cost method. Know **viral advertising**. Know that targeted advertising uses **search history**.

## Theme 5 — Business Operations

### 5.1 Economies and diseconomies
- **Internal economies:** *"provide cost advantages to larger firms since the average total cost will fall as output increases."*
- **External economies:** *"allow the average total cost of a business to fall as **the entire industry increases in size**."* ← **the distinction is: internal = the firm grows; external = the industry grows.**
- **Diseconomies:** *"the business increases its output and average total costs increase… because the business has grown too large and it is now suffering from **poor communication and poor co-ordination**. These both lead to **mistakes** which cause the average total cost to increase."*

### 5.2.1 Production processes
Know advantages/disadvantages of **job, batch and flow**. The guide's own examples:

> *"hand-crafted footwear would be produced on a **job** basis since the much higher production costs can be recouped through a very high selling price"* · *"a chocolate bar is likely to be mass produced using a **capital-intensive** production process"* · *"McLaren sports cars are produced in **batches** since a flow process would produce too many of them when the market for super cars is limited in size."*

**Productivity** must be calculable: **output per person per hour**, and *"improved productivity will decrease average total costs."*

### 5.2.2 Lean production
Covers **JIT**, **Kaizen** and **waste minimisation**.

| | Detail |
|---|---|
| **JIT advantages** | *"lower warehousing costs, improved **liquidity** since cash is no longer tied up in stock/inventory"* |
| **JIT drawbacks** | *"cannot cope with surges in demand, requires very strong supplier relationships"* |
| **JIT best used** | *"in a **dynamic market where inventory depreciates rapidly**"* |
| **Kaizen** | *"continuous improvement"* — impacts *quality, efficiency/productivity and quality* |
| **Kaizen limit** | *"hard to implement due to the need for **cultural change within the business**"* |

> *"students must understand that lean production is about trying to produce the **highest quality product for the lowest average total cost** so that the business can take advantage of increased profit margins."*

### 5.2.3 Impact of technology in production
> *"**Robots are expensive, but significantly improve productivity**, so the high fixed costs of them are divided up over many more units of output."*

> *"Robots also provide a **consistent standard of production** and are best used for **simple repetitive tasks** e.g. welding joints in a car."*

### 5.3 Factors of production
**Enterprise (entrepreneurship), Capital (machinery), Land (to include raw materials), Labour (workers)** — four factors.

- **Capital-intensive:** *"products are primarily made by machines e.g. **soft drinks**."*
- **Labour-intensive:** *"products are primarily made using workers e.g. **clothing production**."*
