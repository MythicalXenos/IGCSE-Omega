## Part 1 — Research: what people who have been in this exact position did

### 1.1 What students who actually got 9s report doing

A teacher (Mr Salles) surveyed 50+ students who achieved grades 7–9. The recurring behaviours, in his
words:

- **"They wrote lots of answers. They got their teachers to mark these. This is my number one
  tactic."**
- "They were tactical about what quotes to use... **focused on key quotes, rather than volume of
  quotes**."
- "Their most valuable resource was student answers... **They read top grade answers and saw how the
  mark scheme is applied.**"
- **"They realised that many of the marks are simply about writing speed, so practised question
  timings and writing more quickly."**
- "They would experiment with the question order of the language exam."
- For Language writing: they "pre-planned or pre-written the story".

**Read that list against what this learner has actually done in two days: three toolkits, zero marked
answers.** The gap is not knowledge. It is the loop.

### 1.2 What the Level 4 descriptors actually reward

Across exam-board guidance and grade-8/9 exemplar commentaries, the consistent pattern:

- Level 4 keywords in reading: **"perceptive"** and **"detailed"**.
- Reached by **selection first**: "narrow down and focus in on three or four really, really
  interesting bits... single words or short phrases, not huge chunks. **The longer your quote, the
  fewer marks you'll get.**"
- Then by **development**: "writing in more detail about each detail selected."
- The engine: **"inherent contradictions"** — "It is both life-giving and destructive; it is beautiful
  and terrible. **These ideas drive the analysis.**"
- And **micro ↔ macro**: a Level 4 answer "can move between the micro and the macro."
- One board report: **"Responses at Level 4 were rare."**

### 1.3 What the learning science says about installing it in one day

| Finding | Source | Practical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Practice testing + distributed practice are the only two "high utility" techniques; rereading, highlighting, summarising are "low" | Dunlosky et al. 2013 | Do questions, not notes |
| Explicit strategy instruction, modelled then faded, effect size **0.82**; SRSD variant **1.14** (0.84 for stronger writers) | Graham & Perin 2007 | The *procedure* is the intervention |
| Feedback effect **0.70–0.79**, and feed-forward ("where to next") beats task comment | Hattie & Timperley; Wisniewski et al. | Correction must name the next step, not judge the work |
| **Comparing your own flawed model to an expert model produces more conceptual change than studying the expert model alone** | Gadgil, Nokes-Malach & Chi 2012 | Put your Level 3 paragraph beside the Level 4 one and name the difference |
| Massed practice holds up for a test within 24 hours; sleep loss costs up to 40% of retrieval | Kornell 2009; Stickgold 2005 | Cramming is legitimate today. Losing sleep is not |
| Level 4 needs *fewer, deeper* quotations | exemplar guidance + this repo's measurements | 3-word quotes, chosen, repeated on |

- **Volume** — see §2.3, which the earlier plans never checked.

### 2.3 The lever nobody costed: how many words the paper actually needs

Word counts measured from the 14 authentic June 2023 scripts in this repository:

| Question | Full/near-full script | Words |
|---|---|---|
| Q3 | 9/10 | 449 |
| Q6 | 10/10 | 394 |
| Q7 | 15/15 | 730 |
| Q8 | 30/30 | 592 |
| Section C | 30/30 | 531 |
| **Total** | | **2,696** |

A realistic grade-9 target is **~2,400 words in 180 minutes**, which is **13.3 words per minute
sustained for three hours**, including reading, planning and checking.

If this learner's hand-writing speed is 10–12 words per minute — common — then **no technique can
save them**: they physically cannot reach the volume at which Level 4 references and "a range of
comparisons" exist. That would explain, better than any analysis deficit, why the same candidate
produces 6/10 in a practice paragraph and 4/10 in an exam.

**This is testable in three minutes and it decides which plan is correct.**

### 2.4 The simulation

Monte Carlo, 200,000 trials, empirical component distributions from four mocks:

| Scenario | Mean | P(9) | P(8+) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current habits | 55.9 | 0.0% | 0.0% |
| Consistency + template interventions combined | 61.9 | 0.3% | 25% |
| Ceiling raised to Level 4 in Section A + AO5 discipline | 71.3 | 92.7% | 100% |

**Conclusion:** consistency work cannot reach 69. One level up in the levels-based questions can, and
when it happens the grade follows almost automatically. The distribution is bimodal and the boundary
sits between the Level 3 state and the Level 4 state.

## Part 4 — The plan

### Step 0 — the three-minute diagnosis (do this first)

Write continuously for **3 minutes** on any topic. Count the words. Multiply by 20.

| Result | Meaning | What the plan becomes |
|---|---|---|
| **20+ wpm** | Speed is not the constraint | Focus on Level 4 moves and timing |
| **14–19 wpm** | Tight but viable | Volume targets + Level 4 moves |
| **under 14 wpm** | **Volume is the ceiling** | Paragraph counts down, quality up; do not attempt 6-paragraph Q7 |

Also count the words in your last mock answer, if you have it. Divide by the minutes you were given.

### Step 1 — the four levers, in order of marks returned

| # | Lever | Cost | Expected gain | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Clock discipline**: 8 read / 4 retrieval / 14 Q3 / 14 Q6 / 20 Q7, hard stop at 40 min | 5 min | up to +7 | Their own 23 vs 15 |
| 2 | **Volume targets**: Q3 400w, Q6 350w, Q7 550–600w, Q8 550w, C 500w | free (a rule) | +2 to +5 | 14-script measurement |
| 3 | **The Level 4 sentence**: one per paragraph — either the contradiction sentence or the macro sentence | 30 min of cycles | +1 per question | Gadgil; exemplar guidance |
| 4 | **AO5 four-pass proofread** on B and C | 8 min | +2 to +4 | 18 AO5 marks |

### Step 2 — the Level 4 sentence, concretely

Every analysis paragraph already has three sentences (choice → effect → question words). **Add exactly
one more**, chosen from these two and no others:

**(a) The contradiction sentence.** *"This sits against ___ earlier, where the writer said ___."*

**(b) The macro sentence.** *"Which is why the extract does not end on ___ but on ___."*

Two sentence stems. No new vocabulary, no new device knowledge. That is the entire Level 3 → Level 4
transition, and it is why 6/10 becomes 7/10.

### Step 3 — the contrastive pair (the Gadgil loop, 3 cycles)

Repeat three times, **12 minutes each**:

1. **Read** a scored exemplar paragraph from `Verified_Candidate_Responses/June_2023_Section_A.md`
   (pick the 9/10 Q3 or the 10/10 Q6).
2. **Write** your own paragraph on the *same* quotation, 6 minutes.
3. **Compare** the two side by side and write down **one difference** — not "mine is worse", but
   "theirs does X that mine doesn't".
4. **Rewrite** yours with that one difference added, 4 minutes.

This is the highest-value 36 minutes available, because it is the only procedure in this file that
both the research (Gadgil 2012: comparing a flawed model to an expert model beats studying the expert
model) and the learner's own data (6/10 max, needs 7/10) point at simultaneously.

### Step 4 — the two writing sections, where vector 3 wins the grade

**Section B (needs 23/30).**
- Underline the audience noun and the form noun before writing.
- **One paragraph per bullet, each bullet a different job: what / why it matters / what to do.**
- Speech is their strength — keep it as the default if the option exists; otherwise the article is the
  nearest relative.
- 550 words. Proofread.

**Section C (needs 23/30).**
- **10+ paragraphs**, 480–550 words. The full-mark scripts have 11 and 12.
- Intro: claim → why it matters → the question restated. **Preview nothing.**
- Conclusion: concede → consequence → closing image. **Summarise nothing.**
- Body: six different jobs (strongest for / second for / strongest against / dismantle it / unseen
  consequence / sharp example).

# STEP 0 RESULT - speed test complete (1 November 2026)

**Written output: 80 words in 3 minutes = 26.7 words per minute.** The learner notes this may be a
slight underestimate.

## The volume hypothesis is falsified

Section 2.3 proposed that handwriting speed might be the binding constraint. It is not. The paper
requires ~2,400-2,700 words in 180 minutes, i.e. **13-15 wpm sustained**. This learner writes at
**26.7 wpm** - roughly **double** the requirement, a full-paper capacity of about **4,800 words**.

Consequences:

1. **Volume is removed from the list of causes.** Spare capacity is about 2,400 words.
2. **Overall time pressure is removed too** - 2,400 words consumes ~90 of the 180 minutes, leaving ~90
   for reading, planning and checking. A Q7 scoring 2-4/15 is therefore *not* a question that ran out
   of time to be written.
3. **The remaining explanations are paragraph count and the Level 4 ceiling.** At this speed six
   paragraphs are easy. A low Q7 is most likely one **started late or written in two paragraphs**.
4. **The worst use of the spare capacity is longer paragraphs.** Extra words in one paragraph earn
   nothing. Extra *paragraphs* do.

## Recalibrated budgets, derived from this learner's own speed

At 26.7 wpm one 70-word paragraph takes **~2.6 minutes**.

| Question | Slot | Plan | Write | Words available | Paragraph target (~70 words) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q3 | 14 min | 2 | 11 | ~290 | **4** |
| Q6 | 14 min | 2 | 11 | ~290 | **4** |
| Q7 | 20 min | 3 | 16 | ~430 | **6** (72 words each) |
| Q8 | 50 min | 5 | 40 | ~1,070 | 3 bullets, **6 paragraphs** |
| Section C | 50 min | 4 | 44 | ~1,180 | **10** (44 words each) |

The asymmetry matters: Section C has surplus capacity, Section A does not. **Section A is the only
part of the paper where words are genuinely tight** - and it is where the marks are being lost.

## Revised diagnosis

| Candidate cause | Status |
|---|---|
| Handwriting speed | **Eliminated** - 26.7 wpm against 13-15 needed |
| Time pressure overall | **Eliminated** - ~90 minutes spare |
| Not knowing what to write about | Partially live - addressed by the five slots |
| Paragraph-count discipline | **Primary suspect** - 4 paragraphs is a decision, not a capacity |
| Level 4 ceiling (6/10 max) | **Live** - one sentence type short |
| Section B/C AO5 | **Live** - 18 marks, cheap |

## Revised probability

With volume eliminated and ~90 spare minutes, the earlier 15-20% for grade 9 becomes **25-30%**,
contingent on paragraph count being applied and one Level 4 sentence type being added. The ceiling is
no longer physical. It is procedural.

**Revised diagnosis — content type, not content quantity.** The script contains three repeating paragraph types:

- **Type V (vague):** "this helps the reader visualize / feels more personal / is descriptive and vivid". Occurs 12x including whole paragraphs (Q3 ¶4-5, Q6 ¶1, Q7 ¶1-2, Q7 ¶6). **Zero marks.**
- **Type P (provenance):** "adapted from a novel / a travel book" — 6 occurrences, one of them (Q7 ¶7) occupying the slot where the perspective conclusion belonged.

**Q7 why-7:** correct T1→T2→contrast shape present in ¶3/¶4/¶5 with quotation from both texts, so above the 6/15 one-text cap. But **no paragraph reaches viewpoint** — "Text One wants you to be awed, Text Two wants you to laugh at the man writing it" is never said.

**Lever change (adopting the learner's own move over the plan's).** §4's mandatory "one contradiction or macro sentence per paragraph" was executed **zero** times. Retire it from the mandatory slot. Replace with the single closed question **"what did the writer NOT say?"** — name the plain alternative, state the distance. It is subtractive: delete the six Type V / Type P phrases (Q3 ¶4-5, Q6 ¶1, Q7 ¶1-2, ¶6) and spend the space on the swap. **Net word count may fall while the mark rises.**

**Ceiling revision.** A now scores ≈23/30 — his recorded best-ever A. Test reading (b); it is nearly free.

**Also fix:** "Neither writers ever use direct address" is factually false (Text Two uses "you" at lines 23, 28, 34). "Neither writers" → "Neither writer". No written doubt in answers ("I guess", "I dk", "(Or if this counts)").
