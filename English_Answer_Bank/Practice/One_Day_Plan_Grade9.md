## 3. Section A: the missing structure

### The unit of writing is **one choice**, not one paragraph of the text.

Not sentence by sentence. Not paragraph by paragraph. **One paragraph = one thing the writer did.**

**How many paragraphs:** five for Q3/Q6 (about 90 words each, 400–450 words total). Six for Q7.
The archive's full-mark Q3 was 449 words in 6 paragraphs; the full-mark Q7 was 730 words in 8.

### The closed checklist — you do not need to "know everything to look for"

Scan for these **ten**, in this order. You only need four or five per answer.

| # | Thing to find | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Strong verb** | a verb doing more than it needs to ("slashed", "attacked", "plunged") |
| 2 | **Loaded adjective** | an adjective that judges, not just describes ("breathtaking", "pitiless") |
| 3 | **List of three** | three items separated by commas |
| 4 | **Contrast** | two opposite things in one sentence or two adjacent sentences |
| 5 | **Pronoun / direct address** | "we", "you", "I" |
| 6 | **Short sentence** | noticeably shorter than its neighbours |
| 7 | **Repetition** | the same word or idea twice |
| 8 | **First / last sentence** | always available, always structural |
| 9 | **Statistics or fact** | a number, a study, a name |
| 10 | **Punctuation for effect** | a dash, an exclamation, a colon before a list |

### The paragraph formula (fill the five blanks)

> The writer uses **choice** — "**words**". This **effect phrase**, because **what the word does that
> a plainer word would not**, so the reader **realises / accepts / sees** that **the question's own
> words**.

Worked on checklist item 1:

> The writer uses the verb "slashed" — "wadis slashed as if with a giant knife". This conveys a sense
> of violence, because "slashed" is a word for flesh rather than rock, and "cut" would have described
> the land without hurting it, so the reader realises that the landscape is beautiful and brutal at
> the same time.

**Five of those = 6/10.** Not 9, but 6 is the plan.

### Q7: the comparison structure

Six paragraphs. In **every** paragraph:

> **Axis.** Text One does ______ through "____", whereas Text Two does ______ through "____".
> **So what.** The difference shows that ______.

Use these six axes in this order — you never have to invent one:

1. **Genre and purpose** (fiction vs travel book; to impress vs to entertain)
2. **Stance** (guest being shown around vs man alone on foot)
3. **What they select to describe** (landscape vs people and places; vast vs small)
4. **Attitude to difficulty** (danger described, not suffered vs suffered in detail)
5. **Language register** (admiring, heightened vs comic, self-mocking)
6. **How each ends** (verdict vs bathos)

**Never write a paragraph about one text only.** One-text answers are capped at 6/15 by the mark
scheme.

## 4. Section B: the four forms, and the repetition fix

**From November 2025 the only permitted forms are:** article for a school/college magazine or website
· speech or talk to peers · contribution to a website · letter.

You know speech. Here are the other three, minimally.

| Form | Open with | Conventions to include | Close with |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Article** | A headline + a one-line standfirst, then a first sentence that states the angle | subheadings, direct address, a rhetorical question | a recommendation |
| **Website contribution** | A short, blunt first sentence ("This is not a small problem.") | short paragraphs, direct address, a bulleted or numbered list | a call to action |
| **Letter** | "Dear Editor," / "Dear Sir or Madam," + reason for writing in sentence one | formal register, "Yours faithfully/sincerely", reference to the publication | a request |

**The repetition fix — give each bullet a different job.** Repetition comes from treating the bullets
as three topics. Treat them as three **functions** instead:

| Bullet position | Its job | What you write |
|---|---|---|
| Bullet 1 | **What** — describe or explain it | facts and examples |
| Bullet 2 | **Why it matters** — consequence | cause and effect |
| Bullet 3 | **What to do / what happens if** — action or warning | imperatives, advice |

Same content, three different jobs, so nothing overlaps. **One paragraph per bullet.** The bullets
*are* the AO1 grid: 10 marks.

**Register, if you "just write normally":** change three things only — the pronoun ("we" for peers,
"you" for readers, "I" for a letter), sentence length (short for a talk, longer for an article), and
one direct address per paragraph. That is enough for AO4 Level 4.

**Word count:** 500–550. More does not help.

## 5. Section C: introductions and conclusions that don't repeat

You named the exact cause yourself: *"I don't know what to say in the introduction without saying
stuff for each of my arguments."* That is why it repeats — you are writing a preview, then the
argument, then a summary of the argument. Three passes over the same material.

**Cut the preview. That is the whole fix.**

| Part | Its job | Its job is NOT |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction (3 sentences) | stake a position + say why it matters now | to list your arguments |
| Body (6–8 paragraphs) | one argument each, developed | to restate the introduction |
| Conclusion (3 sentences) | zoom out — what follows from this | to summarise the paragraphs |

### The introduction, three sentences, no preview

1. **A claim, stated flatly.** "Nobody is bored any more, and that is not a good thing."
2. **Why it matters / who it affects.** "For a generation raised on a screen, nothing slower than a
   notification now registers as interesting."
3. **The question you are answering, in your own words — but not the answer, and not the list.**
   "The question is whether that is a price worth paying."

That is 50 words, it establishes stance, and it has previewed nothing. **Nothing to repeat later.**

### The conclusion, three sentences, no summary

1. **Concede the strongest opposing point, briefly.** "There are, of course, worse things than
   entertainment that arrives instantly."
2. **State the consequence of your position.** "But a mind that has never had to sit with a difficult
   hour has not been trained for anything."
3. **Close on an image or a short, final verdict — not a summary.** "We are not bored; we are being
   kept busy, which is not the same thing."

**The rule that kills repetition:** the introduction may not contain any word that appears in a body
paragraph's first sentence. The conclusion may not contain any point already made in a body paragraph
— only what *follows* from it.

### Body paragraphs, so nothing overlaps

Each body paragraph gets **one job**, and the jobs rotate:

| Paragraph | Job |
|---|---|
| 1 | The strongest argument **for** your position |
| 2 | A second, different argument **for** (different kind — personal, social, practical) |
| 3 | The strongest argument **against** |
| 4 | **Dismantle** paragraph 3 |
| 5 | A consequence nobody has mentioned yet |
| 6 | A short, sharp example or image |

Six jobs, six paragraphs, no overlap. **10+ paragraphs total including intro and conclusion** — the
archive's two full-mark Section C scripts have 11 and 12 paragraphs, at roughly 480 words.
