# Full essay — "We only regret the chances we do not take." To what extent do you agree?

Working file. Night of 2 Nov 2026. Exam 3 Nov, 14:00.

## The two words doing the work
- "only" -> sweeping word. Makes the statement falsifiable with one counter-example.
  The essay's job: decide whether the counter-example is the exception or the rule.
- "regret" -> a feeling about a choice, and a comparative one. Not "cost". Not "consequence".
  An essay about costs has not answered a question about regret.

## The distinction that settles it (candidate's own idea, sharpened)
A regret from something you did has a known outcome. It is finite: you can name it and close it.
A regret from something you did not do keeps its imaginary outcome, and imagination is generous.
It never closes, because you never found out.
Therefore the statement is true more often than it looks - not because failing is painless,
but because failure ends and wondering does not.

## The boundary (what makes it a "to what extent" answer)
The rule holds only for recoverable chances. For irreversible ones - money you cannot earn
back, an injury, a person who will not wait - the calculation flips and the statement fails.

## Five blocks
1. Opening (40-70 w): the moment of the offer, both endings visible. Position at the end.
2. Reason one (90-110 w): the chance that was never found out. Something specific.
3. Reason two / the other side's best case (90-110 w): the project you were handed and dropped.
   Blame, lost promotion, possibly the job. "That is a genuine loss." Then: but it is a loss
   you can name.
4. The distinction (90-110 w): ended vs unfinished. Then the boundary.
5. Landing (20-40 w): question's subject + position, own words only. No "In conclusion".

## Faults to avoid, carried over from the drafts
- "We often have the opportunity..." = defining the topic. Cut on sight.
- "Taking chances is a gamble. It brings forth an uncertain outcome." = definition. Cut.
- "bring forth" = archaic, reads borrowed.
- "regretful" describes a person; outcomes are "regrettable".
- "opportunity cost" applies equally to acting and not acting, so it distinguishes nothing.
- comma splices, random capitals, "may have lose".

## Getting unstuck on the opening

Diagnosis: the opening stalls because it is being asked to set up the whole essay.
It only has to make sentence two easy to write.

Three methods that work at the desk:
1. Say it out loud, then type what you said. The blank page is a formality problem -
   you are writing "an essay" instead of talking. Answer a friend, transcribe, tidy.
2. Write the bad opening on purpose, then delete sentence one and start from sentence two.
   The real opening is usually hiding in the warm-up.
3. Blunt answer first. "I mostly agree, and not for the reason people usually give."
   Scored scripts open this plainly. Overthinking, not skill, is the blocker.

## Five openings for this prompt, different registers
A. Scene: "You know the moment: someone offers you something - a place, a role, a move -
   and for a second you can see both endings."
B. Blunt: "I agree, and I'd go further than the statement does. The chances we take hurt,
   but they hurt once."
C. Half-concession: "Most people say this after the fact, about a chance already gone.
   It's easier to believe before you've taken one and lost."
D. Interrogation: "The word to notice is 'only'. One word, and it either rules out every
   other kind of regret or it doesn't."
E. Concrete: "There's a form you didn't send. Everyone has one - an application, a message,
   a place on a course - and it has been sitting at the back of your head ever since."
