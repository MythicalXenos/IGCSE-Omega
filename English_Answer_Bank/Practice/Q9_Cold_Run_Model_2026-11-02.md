# Q9 cold run — full worked example, 2 Nov 2026

Purpose: replace micro-drills with one full worked example on an unseen prompt, then
one cold run by the candidate. Transfer, not topic-memorisation.

Prompt used for the model: "The best things in life are free." Discuss.

## 3-minute start (works on any prompt)
1. Four places: work / school or study / family or friends / alone.
2. One line each, concrete, with a person or object in it.
3. Pick three. The fourth place becomes your objection paragraph.
4. Write the first sentence before you know the ending.

## Plan produced
- Opening: nobody names a purchase when asked what made their week good.
- Family: a Sunday at a grandparent's table.
- Alone: sleep, walking, the first warm week.
- Objection: free time is not evenly shared; a nurse on a double shift.
  Answer: the walk is still free; what costs money is the time to take it.
- Ending: nobody remembers the receipt.

## The model answer (~400 words)
Ask anyone what made their week good and nobody names the thing they bought. They
name the walk, the phone call, the hour they spent doing nothing at all. The best
things in life are free, and the strange part is that most of us spend our lives
trying to pay for them.

Take the clearest example: a Sunday at your grandmother's. The food costs something,
but the food is not the point. The point is that four people are in one room and
nobody is checking their phone. You cannot buy that atmosphere. You can buy a better
dinner, in a better restaurant, with worse conversation. Money buys the setting; it
does not buy what happens in it.

The same is true of what you do alone. Sleep is free. So is walking, and so is
sitting outside in the first warm week of the year, which is a pleasure people fly
to other countries to find. I know a man who pays sixty pounds a month for a gym and
feels best on the twenty-minute walk there.

None of that is the whole truth. Freedom to enjoy free things is not evenly shared:
a nurse on a double shift has no free evening to walk in, and "the best things are
free" can sound like advice from someone who has never been poor. That is a fair
objection, and it does not defeat the claim. It sharpens it. The walk is still free.
What costs money is the time to take it.

And that is where the phrase is usually misused. People quote it to mean that
wanting money is shallow, which is neither true nor the point. It means something
narrower and more useful: the parts of a good life that money cannot reach are the
parts most worth protecting. Nobody remembers the receipt. They remember who was at
the table.

## Why it works (five decisions, plain terms)
1. Terms of the prompt in sentence three, in the candidate's own sentence.
2. Every paragraph has one picture you could photograph.
3. Three paragraphs contain a sentence of six words or fewer.
4. The objection is two sentences, then it is answered, then it is used to make the
   point sharper rather than conceded away.
5. The ending adds a narrowed meaning instead of summarising.

## Reusable shapes (fill with own content; do not lift the words)
- Opening: "Ask anyone what [thing] and nobody says [obvious answer]. They say [two
  small concrete things]." Then the claim in your own words.
- Objection paragraph: "None of that is the whole truth. [One specific person who
  breaks the rule.] That is a fair objection, and it does not defeat the claim. It
  sharpens it."
- Ending: one narrow claim plus one picture. Never "In conclusion".

## Accuracy pass (3 minutes, backwards)
Sentence punctuation; comma splices; apostrophes; homophones; capital I; capitals at
sentence starts; subject-verb agreement; tenses; missing words; missing a/an/the;
paragraphing.

## Candidate draft + correction (2 Nov 2026)

Prompt: "We only regret the chances we do not take."

Draft as written:
"We often have the opportunity to take a risk, a chance - at something. A gamble, It
either brings success or ends in failure. Choosing not to take the gamble can bring regret."

Faults:
- Sentences 1-2 define the topic instead of taking a position. Nothing is at stake.
- "A gamble, It" = comma splice + capital after comma.
- "a chance - at something" - dash dangles, "at something" is vague.
- No sentence contains the prompt's terms inside a claim of the candidate's own.
- All three sentences similar length; no short landing.

Kept: the last sentence is a real claim and is the best line in the draft.

Rewrite (same register):
"You know the moment: someone offers you something - a place, a role, a move - and for
a second you can see both endings. Take it and one of them happens. Refuse, and nothing
happens at all, which is why the chances we don't take are the ones we end up regretting."

Three changes only: specific moment instead of "we often"; punctuation fixed; claim moved
to the end where it lands.

## The decisive distinction for "chances we do not take" (block 4)

Candidate's own insight, sharpened: the prompt is not asking whether chances exist or whether
failure hurts. It is asking which regret is heavier.

The distinction that decides it:
- A regret of ACTION has a known outcome. It is finite. You can name it, grieve it and close it.
- A regret of INACTION keeps its imaginary outcome, and imagination is generous. It never closes.

Boundary condition (this is what makes it "to what extent" rather than a slogan):
the rule holds only for recoverable chances. For irreversible ones - money you cannot
earn back, an injury, a person who will not wait - the calculation flips and the
statement fails.

Note on term use:
- "opportunity cost" is an economics term meaning the value of the next-best option given up.
  Both taking and not taking a chance carry one, so the term does not distinguish between
  them. Using it as if it does is the "over-ambitious vocabulary" fault.
- "regret" is not "cost". A layoff is a cost. Regret is a feeling about a choice, and it is
  comparative: you regret one thing more than another. An essay about costs has not yet
  answered a question about regret.
- "regretful" describes a person. An outcome is "regrettable".

The word doing the work in the prompt is "only". A sweeping word (only, always, never,
everyone, best, too) makes the statement falsifiable with a single counter-example. The
essay's job is to decide whether the counter-example is the exception or the rule.

## Corrected paragraph (candidate's argument, kept; made about regret)
"Failing costs you something real. Take a project you were handed and then dropped: the blame
lands on you, the promotion goes to someone else, and in a bad month the job goes with it.
That is a genuine loss. But it is a loss you can name. You know what happened, why, and what
you would do differently. The chance you never took gives you none of that. It stays in your
head at its best, unfinished, and you keep finishing it for years."
