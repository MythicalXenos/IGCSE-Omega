## TEXT ONE — *Salmon Fishing in the Yemen* (adapted from a novel by Paul Torday)

*In this passage, the narrator describes his first impressions of the Yemen.*

```
 1  We are here in the Yemen at last.
 2  The landscapes are breathtaking – towering cliffs that are golden in the sunlight
 3  and purple in the shade, deep valleys known as wadis slashed as if with a giant knife
 4  cutting thousands of feet between sheer rock walls, with an occasional thread of water
 5  at the bottom surrounded by date palm, gravel plains that are an endless expanse of
 6  dull brown, marked here and there by the white crust of the salt flats where moisture
 7  beneath the sand leaches salt to the surface. These are dangerous places where a
 8  vehicle might sink if driven across them. On one trip we caught tantalising glimpses of
 9  a sea of sand: the beginning of the Empty Quarter, a quarter of a million square miles of
10  uninhabited desert.
11  And the towns are as wonderful as the desert. From the desert, driving towards a town
12  through the haze and dust, it is as if one is approaching Manhattan. Many‑storeyed tower
13  houses white with plaster that from a distance look like skyscrapers, poke above the walls
14  of ancient fortifications or seem to totter on the edge of brown cliffs. They are beautiful
15  and unlike anything I have ever seen or heard of. Once one is in a town it is a din of
16  shouting voices, a riot of colour, unimaginable smells of drains and spices, and then you
17  turn the corner and there is a garden, hidden away behind the houses.
18  We spent the first few days here touring the country. In the Empty Quarter we saw the
19  beginnings of the dunes, an endless landscape of sculpted sand, dunes like low hills,
20  dunes like long fingers, which shift and change endlessly so that no track through them
21  ever lasts for more than a few minutes before it is obliterated in the restless wind that
22  stings one’s skin with grains of sand.
23  We drove into the mountains along crumbling tracks of loose gravel, always with a
24  precipitous slope on one side, lurching up steep winding roads along which it seemed
25  impossible from below that any vehicle could travel. We found tiny villages, perched at
26  the foot of great cliffs and in permanent shadow, where a few herdsmen lived tending
27  their goats. We saw deep pools of water coloured an unearthly blue‑green, oases where
28  date palms fringed the water’s edge, and where brown‑skinned boys in their coloured
29  futahs, a sort of skirt wrapped around like a sarong, jumped in and out of the water.
30  The next day we drove into the mountains of the Heraz. The mountains of Heraz rise in
31  huge ramparts above terraced slopes where farmers eke out a basic existence growing
32  millet and maize. From below it looks impossible for anyone to penetrate the mountains
33  on foot, let alone in a vehicle. But, as we had noticed before, cunning tracks made their
34  way round the side of huge shoulders of hillside, snaking between boulders the size of
35  churches, careering down loose and crumbling slopes and up the other side.
36  Suddenly we went from bright sun into thick mist, and drops of water covered the
37  windows and windscreen. We could hardly see twenty yards in front, but then the mist
38  began to clear. In front of us we caught glimpses of a fortified village standing on a prow
39  of rock. We drove up the track to the village. Perhaps a hundred tower houses stood on
40  top of a cliff, with another cliff above the village soaring up into the mist. It made me
41  think of some forgotten, hidden world from a childhood story. We drove through a gate
42  in the walls surrounding the village, and along narrow lanes of sand and gravel.
43  It was as if we had travelled back in time hundreds of years. The streets were empty, but
44  occasionally a child would peer at us from a darkened doorway. A few chickens scattered
45  before the wheels of our Land Cruiser. We turned uphill up another lane and came to
46  a set of beautiful carved wooden gates set in a high wall, which opened inwards as
47  we approached.
48  Inside the whitewashed walls was a garden of paradise, cool and mysterious. Water
49  rippled from a fountain and splashed over the edge of a basin, cascading into marble
50  channels that formed a grid of running water going backwards and forwards across the
51  garden. Palms and almond trees provided shade, and a spiky grass grew everywhere,
52  with bougainvillea climbing the white walls, and oleander and euphorbia and other
53  colourful shrubs, the names of which I do not know, planted here and there alongside
54  the channels of flowing water.
55  It is a magical place.
```

## TEXT TWO — *Notes from a Small Island* (adapted from a travel book by Bill Bryson)

*In this passage, the American writer describes a journey along part of the south coast of England.*

```
 1  Handfast Point is a grassy cliff that ends in a sudden drop of perhaps 200 feet to seriously
 2  frothy seas. It takes a special blend of nerve and foolishness to creep up to the edge
 3  and have a look. Just beyond it stand two stranded pinnacles of limestone known as
 4  Old Harry and Old Harry’s Wife, all that remains of a land bridge that once connected
 5  Dorset to the Isle of Wight, 18 miles away across the bay and just visible through a cloak
 6  of salty mists.
 7  Beyond the headland, the path climbed steeply to Ballard Down, a taxing slog for an
 8  old puffed‑out unfit person like me, but worth it for the view, which was sensational –
 9  like being on top of the world. For miles around, the Dorset hills rolled and billowed,
10  like a shaken‑out blanket settling on to a bed. Country lanes wandered among plump
11  hedgerows and the hillsides were prettily dotted with woodlands, farmsteads, and
12  creamy flecks of sheep. In the distance the sea, bright and vast and silvery blue, stretched
13  away to a mountain of tumbling cumulus clouds.
14  At my feet far below, Swanage huddled against a rocky headland on the edge of a
15  horseshoe bay, and behind me lay Studland, the marshy flats of Poole Harbour and
16  Brownsea Island, and beyond that a hazy infinity of meticulously worked farmland.
17  It was beautiful beyond words, one of those rare moments when life seems perfect. As
18  I stood there, spellbound and quite alone, a bank of cloud drifted in front of the sun,
19  and through it there poured magnificent spears of shimmery light, like escalators to
20  heaven. One of them fell at my feet and for one moment I would almost swear I heard
21  celestial music.
22  I went over to a stone bench that had been thoughtfully conveyed to this lofty summit
23  for the benefit of weary chaps like me – it really is extraordinary how often you encounter
24  some little kindly gesture like this in Britain – and took out my Ordnance Survey map.
25  As a rule, I am not terribly comfortable with any map that doesn’t have a You‑Are‑Here
26  arrow on it somewhere, but the Ordnance Survey maps are in a league of their own. This
27  one even included the stone seat on which I sat now. It astounds me to be able to look at
28  a map and know to the square metre where my buttocks are deployed.
29  In my idle perusal, I noticed that a mile or so to the west there stood a historic obelisk1.
30  Wondering why anyone would erect a monument in such a remote and challenging
31  spot, I struck off along the crest of the hill to have a look. It was the longest mile I can
32  remember walking. I passed through grassy fields, through flocks of skittish sheep, over
33  stiles and through gates, without any sign of my goal drawing nearer, but I doggedly
34  pressed on because – well, because if you are stupid you do. Eventually, I arrived at a
35  modest, wholly unremarkable granite obelisk. Pursing my lips and referring once more to
36  my map, I noticed that just a bit further on was something called the Giant’s Grave, and I
37  thought: ‘Well, that sounds interesting.’ So I plodded off to see it. I never found the Giant’s
38  Grave. I think I was close, but I can’t be sure.
39  Thus it was that I spent a quiet, sweatily perplexed afternoon tramping through a
40  large, forgotten, but very green and pretty corner of Dorset, looking for an inland route
41  to Swanage. The more I plunged on, the less defined did the footpaths become. By
42  mid‑afternoon, I found myself increasingly crawling under barbed wire, fording streams
43  with my pack on my head, falling down, and longing to be elsewhere. Occasionally,
44  I would pause to rest and try to identify some small point of similarity between my
45  map and the surrounding landscape. Eventually I would rise, purse my lips and strike
46  off in an entirely new direction. By such means did I find myself, late in the afternoon
47  and somewhat to my surprise, arriving footsore, travel‑soiled and decorated about the
48  extremities with interesting rivulets of dried blood, in Corfe Castle.
49  Corfe Castle is a popular and pretty place, a cluster of stone cottages dominated by the
50  lofty, jagged walls of its famous and much‑photographed castle.
```

*Glossary: obelisk – a column with a pointed top.*
