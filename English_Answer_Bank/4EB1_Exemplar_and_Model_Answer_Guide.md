## Paper structure

- **Section A: Reading — 40 marks.** Q1–Q7: short retrieval (Q1, Q2, Q4, Q5), language analysis (Q3, Q6), and comparison (Q7).
- **Section B: Reading and Writing — 30 marks.** Q8: use ideas from both texts in a new task for a specified audience and purpose. The mark scheme awards AO1 10, AO4 12 and AO5 8.
- **Section C: Writing — 30 marks.** Choose **one**: Q9 discursive/argumentative, Q10 narrative, or Q11 descriptive. Each is assessed for AO4 (20) and AO5 (10).

## Reusable methods

### Q1, Q2, Q4 and Q5: short retrieval

1. Read the line reference in the question before searching the text.
2. Find a detail that answers the exact wording.
3. Give one point for a one-mark question, or two distinct points for Q5. Stop there.

Do not spend analysis time on these questions. A correct point from outside the stated lines may not earn credit, and a long answer can waste time.

### Q3 and Q6: 10-mark language/structure analysis

Use this paragraph chain:

**Writer’s choice → short quotation → zoom in on a word or structural choice → effect/idea → link back to the question.**

Choose a few strong examples from across the passage. Explain how the choices work; do not just name a metaphor, list techniques or retell events. “This interests the reader” is too general unless you say what it makes the reader think, feel or notice. Use short quotations and your own explanation.

#### Constructed model — June 2023 P1 Q3

**Real question:** “Explain how the writer presents her ideas about the importance of spending more time outdoors.” This is a real June 2023 past-paper task; the source text is in the linked QP. The quotations below are from that text and its official mark scheme. The response is **constructed**, not a past-candidate script.

The writer first sounds like someone her young readers can relate to. She jokes about watching “cat videos on Facebook” and bingeing on Netflix, so her advice does not feel like a lecture from someone who never uses a screen. The informal examples build a connection before she asks readers to change their habits. The direct address “listen up” then catches the reader’s attention and signals that the problem matters.

She also combines a warning with practical encouragement. Referring to “psychological studies” makes her point sound supported, while the commands “ditch the Wi-Fi” and “get yourself outdoors” give readers clear actions. The positive promise that “you’ll start feeling much happier” leaves the reader with a benefit rather than only a warning. Overall, the friendly tone and clear steps make spending time outside sound both worthwhile and manageable.

**How to learn from the official response:** Pearson’s June 2023 P1 report shows a real candidate response scoring **9/10** on this question (Level 5, but not full marks). The examiner praises discriminating references and perceptive understanding, while noting that analysis was not quite enough for 10/10. See the score table below.

#### Constructed model — June 2023 P1 Q6

**Real question:** “How does the writer present his experiences of his walk?” References are from the exact source text, adapted from Bill Bryson’s *A Walk in the Woods*, and from the official mark scheme. This model is **constructed**.

At first the walk sounds almost peaceful: the stream is “chuckling” and the sky is “hearty blue”. That mood is undercut by “brittle ice”, the repeated “brown” and the “unyielding” cold. The contrast makes the setting feel less welcoming than the opening image suggests. The writer then describes his body struggling: “bug-eyed, breathing hard” and a heart “kabooming alarmingly”. The exaggerated sound is funny, but it also makes his exhaustion vivid.

The writer’s self-mocking tone continues when he says he was “hopelessly out of shape” and his pack “weighed way too much”. Repeating “hopelessly” and “way too much” makes the climb seem relentless, while the humour keeps the account entertaining. Later, the repeated “ages and ages” stretches out the time, and the phrase “It was hell” circles back to his opening complaint. The reader sees a walk that offers a spectacular view but feels punishing to the person making it.

**How to learn from the official response:** the Pearson report reproduces a real candidate answer awarded **10/10**. The examiner highlights analysis of sentence structure, negative language, rhetorical questions, imperatives and juxtaposition, with embedded references. See printed report p.20; the response is shown on pp.17–19.

### Q7: 15-mark comparison

Compare **throughout**. A useful paragraph shape is:

> Both writers… / However, Text One… while Text Two… / A brief reference from each text / What the difference or similarity reveals about each writer’s view, purpose or tone.

Make several developed comparisons, not two separate summaries followed by one sentence saying they are different. Support both sides of each comparison. Avoid comparing punctuation for its own sake or writing which text you prefer.

#### Constructed model — June 2023 P1 Q7

**Real question:** “Compare how the writers of Text One and Text Two present their ideas and perspectives about being outdoors.” The response below uses references from the exact paper’s texts, including Naomi Dunbar’s article and Bill Bryson’s walk. It is **constructed**, not an official candidate answer.

Both writers show that being outdoors can affect you, but Text One presents it as an accessible way to improve wellbeing, while Text Two makes a walk seem like a comic struggle. Text One begins with the writer’s positive personal experience and uses friendly advice, including the reassurance that a walk can be “a stroll in the park”. This makes the activity sound realistic for ordinary readers. By contrast, Bryson’s “chuckling stream” initially suggests a pleasant setting, but the cold quickly becomes “unyielding” and he admits he is “hopelessly out of shape”. His account focuses on how difficult the walk feels in practice.

The tone also separates the two texts. Dunbar’s numbered points and direct address make her article sound encouraging; it ends with the promise that readers may feel “much happier”. Bryson uses humour and exaggeration, such as his pack weighing “way too much”, to entertain while showing his exhaustion. Even though he reaches a view he calls “sensational”, his trail still “ran on”. So Text One persuades readers that outdoor exercise is a manageable benefit, while Text Two gives a more mixed, humorous picture of the effort involved.

**Official calibration:** a real June 2023 P1 response earned **15/15**. The examiner praises a varied, comprehensive range of comparisons, analysis of the writers’ perspectives and balanced references; the answer compares throughout. See printed report p.30; script pp.25–29.

### Q8: reading-to-writing task, 30 marks

Before writing, spend a short time checking the task’s **audience, form, purpose and all three bullet points**. Select useful ideas from both texts, then explain/adapt them in your own words. Use the requested register throughout: a talk should sound spoken to its audience; a letter should look and sound like a letter. Avoid copying whole sentences from either extract.

#### Constructed model — June 2023 P1 Q8

**Real task:** give a talk to peers about the importance of walking and getting outside, including types of walks, benefits and possible problems. This is an original model based on both real texts in the paper and the mark scheme; it is **not** a candidate script and has no official score.

Hi everyone. I know that after lessons and homework, going outside can feel like one more thing to fit into the day. But a walk does not have to mean climbing a mountain. It could be a short loop around the park at lunch, a walk by the water with a friend, or a longer trail when you have more time.

There are good reasons to try it. A bit of fresh air can help you reset when your mind feels crowded, and moving regularly is good for your health. You might even notice that it is easier to concentrate when you return. The first text makes the point that an ordinary stroll can be enough; you do not need expensive equipment or a dramatic view.

Of course, walks are not always easy. The second writer describes cold weather, steep hills and feeling unprepared. Start with a route that suits your fitness, check the weather and take water and comfortable shoes. If you are short on time, keep the walk short. The goal is not to prove how far you can go. It is to make getting outside something you can actually keep doing. Who would be willing to try a ten-minute walk this week?

**Official calibration:** a real June 2023 P1 Q8 response scored **30/30** (AO1 10/10, AO4 12/12, AO5 8/8). The examiner says all bullet points were covered in detail and purpose/audience stayed sharp. See printed report p.40; script pp.36–39.

### Section C: choose one 30-mark task

All three choices reward effective communication and accurate, controlled writing. Leave enough time for the one you choose; do not try to answer all three.

#### Q9: discursive or argumentative response

Take a clear position, develop reasons and examples, consider a counterpoint fairly, then end with a reasoned view. Keep each paragraph connected to the statement. When the task says “To what extent do you agree?” or “Discuss”, a balanced answer can still reach a firm conclusion.

**Constructed mini-model — June 2023 P1 Q9, “Fresh air and exercise are the keys to happiness.”**

I partly agree. A walk or another form of exercise can improve someone’s mood, give them a break from schoolwork and help them stay healthy. Getting outside can also make a difficult day feel less closed-in. For many people, it is one practical way to look after themselves.

However, exercise is not the only source of happiness. Friends, family, having a purpose and feeling safe can matter just as much. Some people may be unwell, live somewhere with unsafe streets or poor air, or simply be unable to exercise easily. Telling them that happiness depends on going outside would be unfair.

So fresh air and exercise can be important parts of a happier life, but they are not the only “keys”. They help most when they are available and enjoyable, alongside supportive relationships and other things that give people meaning.

*This is a constructed response, not an official script or a mark prediction.* The Pearson June 2023 report includes a real response to this exact Q9 that scored **17/30** (AO4 11/20, AO5 6/10); the examiner suggests more development and a more persuasive use of form, tone and register. See printed p.48; response pp.45–47.

#### Q10: narrative

Plan one central problem or decision, a believable turning point and an ending that connects to the title. Keep the time span manageable. Build character through actions and selective detail; use dialogue only where it changes the situation. A twist is optional, not compulsory.

**Constructed mini-model — June 2023 P1 Q10, “A Difficult Decision”.**

The envelope was already open when I found it on the kitchen table. Inside was the letter about my place on the school trip. I had been chosen—but the fee was due on Friday, and Mum had quietly put the heating bill beside it.

For two days I told myself I did not care about going. At school, Sami asked why I had stopped talking about the trip. I nearly blamed the form; instead, I told him the truth. He did not offer to pay. He simply said, “Ask the teacher. There might be another way.”

That afternoon I explained everything to Ms Rahman. My voice shook, but she listened. There was a small fund for students who needed help, and she helped me apply. I still had to decide whether to accept it. When the letter arrived, I showed Mum first. She read it twice, then smiled and put the heating bill away.

I went on the trip, but the difficult decision had not been whether to go. It had been whether to admit I needed help.

*This is a constructed story, not an official script.* A real candidate response to the exact Q10 scored **30/30** (AO4 20/20, AO5 10/10). The examiner praises control of structure and tone, the effective ending, precise vocabulary and varied sentence structures. See printed p.55; script pp.51–54.

#### Q11: description

Stay focused on the subject. Organise details by viewpoint or movement (for a place), by appearance and behaviour (for a person), or by the senses. Use precise details rather than piling on adjectives. It can have a reflective final line, but it should not turn into a story with a sequence of events.

**Constructed mini-model — June 2023 P1 Q11, “Describe your favourite walk”.**

My favourite walk starts beside the canal, where the path is narrow enough that the reeds brush my sleeves. In the morning the water looks like dull glass, broken by small circles when a fish rises. A bicycle bell rings behind me; then the sound fades, and all I can hear is the soft scrape of my shoes on the gravel.

The path bends under a bridge and opens into a patch of sunlight. The air smells of wet leaves and earth. I always slow down there. The trees lean over the water, making the space feel separate from the traffic and the noise beyond them. At the far end, the canal widens and the sky seems to take up more room.

Nothing dramatic happens on this walk. That is why I like it: for a little while, the day feels quieter and easier to manage.

*This is a constructed descriptive response, not an official script.* A real candidate response to the exact Q11 scored **23/30** (AO4 16/20, AO5 7/10). Pearson notes that it stays focused on experiences, sights and sounds; spelling errors held back its technical score. See printed p.62; script pp.58–61.

## Source and storage policy

The full archive remains the source of QPs, source-text booklets, mark schemes and the two June 2023 examiner reports. This answer-bank folder stores the workbook, this guide and its DOCX copy, scripts to rebuild them, and a compact source index. It does **not** duplicate PDFs. The official candidate examples are valuable calibration, not a promise that the same wording is required. Mark schemes permit valid alternatives. The constructed models use the exact June 2023 past-paper tasks and real source-text references where relevant, but have not been independently marked and are not labelled “perfect”.
