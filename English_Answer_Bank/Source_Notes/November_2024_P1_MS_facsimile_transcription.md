# November 2024 P1 (4EB1/01) mark scheme — facsimile transcription, printed pp. 14–20

**Why this file exists.** `4EB1_01_2411_MS.pdf` has a damaged text layer: on printed pp. 4–20 every body-text character is drawn as a **2×2-pixel image object** (pp. 5–20 carry between 68 and 260 of them per page on top of a handful of real text spans). A text-extraction route therefore returns almost nothing, which is how the earlier "empty mark scheme" verdict was reached and how this defect came to be described in the bank as a defect in the **published content**.

**That description was wrong, and this file corrects it.** The printed pages are complete and legible. Rendering them as images at 120–150 dpi with PyMuPDF recovers every word. The defect is in the **file's text layer**, not in Pearson's marking content, and it affects any automated reader — including Pearson's own hosted copy, which has the same damaged layer. Nothing was ever missing from the paper.

**Method.** Each page below was rendered to PNG at 140–150 dpi and read as an image. Only what is visibly printed is transcribed; layout is flattened into lists. Transcription check: the rendered AO4 and AO5 descriptors match, word for word, the same grids printed in the mark schemes for May/June 2019, January 2020, May/June 2020, January 2022 and the other 4EB1 papers, which is an independent consistency check on the reading.

**Correction of record (29 October 2026):** statements in the bank that this mark scheme is "published defective … every grid page carries only question numbers, AO labels, level labels and bullet glyphs" are accurate **only as descriptions of the text layer**. Where the bank said the defect was "verified in Pearson's hosted copy via a text-extraction route", that sentence proved the wrong thing — the text route fails on both copies precisely because the layer is broken. The answer sets for Q8, Q9, Q10 and Q11 have been re-pointed at this paper's own grids using the transcriptions below, and the borrowed descriptors are retired.

> **Q6.** Reward responses that explain how the writer presents her ideas about New Year's celebrations. Responses may include:
> - the opening paragraph presents an historical context: 'since 2000 BC', '4,000-year-old holiday', reinforced by the superlative 'oldest-celebrated', to present how long we have been celebrating the New Year

> - the use of inclusive language, 'we', and direct address, 'you', engages the reader
> - the repetition of 'new' reminds the reader that this is a time of positive change
> - the use of the comparatives 'happier' and 'healthier' suggests that things will/can improve
> - the listing of activities which could make you happy, 'reading more, taking more photos', presents ideas which are attainable and reassures readers that they can do these things
> - a personal anecdote, 'Last year, I made a resolution…', is used to show how changes can have positive effects and gives the reader a connection to the writer
> - the short, imperative sentence, 'Stop doing things that make you unhappy.', has an impact and makes the reader reflect on their lifestyle
> - the use of alliteration in 'respectable resolutions' emphasises the importance of giving up bad habits

> - the advice offered is reassuring, 'Make goals that are measurable and attainable', 'Just remember', to encourage the reader to change

> - the use of balanced structure, 'place to start', 'time to finish', gives reassurance that some resolutions may take longer to achieve
> - the tricolon 'A plan, a support system and a desire' gives encouragement and ends the text positively
