# Study techniques, filtered for time — and applied to 4EB1

**Compiled 29 October 2026.** Two questions: which learning techniques actually work (evidence, not
folklore), and which of those pay fastest on IGCSE English Language B. Everything below is either a
cited finding or explicitly labelled as my judgement. Sources are at the end.

---

## 1. The evidence base, in one table

The two strongest sources for this subject are Dunlosky et al. (2013), a review of ten common
techniques rated by utility, and Graham & Perin's *Writing Next* (2007), a meta-analysis of writing
instruction with effect sizes.

| Technique | Evidence | Time cost | Verdict for you |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Practice testing** (retrieving from memory, doing questions) | **High** utility — Dunlosky | Low per item | **Keep. Core.** |
| **Distributed practice** (spread over days, not one block) | **High** utility — Dunlosky | Neutral (it's a schedule, not extra work) | **Keep. Core.** |
| **Interleaved practice** (mixing question types in one session) | Moderate utility — Dunlosky | Neutral | **Keep.** Mix Q3/Q6/Q7 rather than drilling one |
| **Self-explanation** ("why did I do that step?") | Moderate utility — Dunlosky | Low | **Keep.** This is the "because" muscle |
| **Elaborative interrogation** ("why is this true?") | Moderate utility — Dunlosky | Low | **Keep.** Same family |
| **Writing strategy instruction** (explicit steps for planning/drafting/revising) | **0.82** — Graham & Perin. SRSD variant **1.14**; strongest for struggling writers (**1.02**) | Medium to learn, then permanent | **Highest-value thing on this list.** |
| **Specific product goals** (concrete criteria for the piece, not "do your best") | **0.70** — Graham & Perin | Very low | **Keep. Nearly free.** |
| **Sentence combining** (join kernel sentences into complex ones) | **0.50** — Graham & Perin | Low, 5 min drills | **Keep** for AO5 sentence variety |
| **Study of models** (analyse a good exemplar, then emulate) | **0.25** — Graham & Perin (small but positive) | Low | **Keep**, but *analyse*, don't just read |
| **Worked examples → completion problems → independent** (cognitive load theory) | Robust; "one of the best known and most widely studied" effects (Sweller) | Medium | **Keep. This is the ladder for the thing you can't yet do.** |
| **Summarisation** | **Low** utility — Dunlosky (note: 0.82 in *Writing Next* for *teaching students to write summaries* — different question) | Medium | Drop as a revision method |
| **Highlighting / underlining** | **Low** utility | Low, but negative return | **Drop** |
| **Rereading** | **Low** utility | Medium | **Drop** |
| **Imagery for text learning** | **Low** utility | Low | Drop |
| **Keyword mnemonic** | **Low** utility | Low | Drop |
| **Traditional grammar instruction in isolation** | **−0.32** (negative, statistically significant) — Graham & Perin | Medium | **Drop.** Use sentence combining instead |
| **Note-making / copying up notes** | Not a learning technique; no strong evidence | High | Drop as revision |

**The one-line summary:** the four things students do most (reread, highlight, summarise, copy notes)
are the four with the weakest evidence. The two with the best evidence (retrieval, spacing) feel
harder and less productive while you do them, which is exactly why they work.

## 2. The time-efficiency filter

Time efficiency here = **marks moved ÷ minutes spent**. That changes the ranking a lot.

| Rank | Activity | Minutes | Why it wins |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Marking your own answer against the grid** | +5 per answer | The mark schemes are public and levels-based, so you can locate your own level. Turns every practice answer into feedback. |
| 2 | **Criteria-first writing** (underline the audience noun and form noun; one paragraph per bullet) | 0 (during writing) | *Specific product goals* effect size 0.70, and it costs nothing extra. |
| 3 | **A fixed procedure per question type** | ~15 min to learn, then free | *Strategy instruction*, 0.82. Removes in-exam decision-making. |
| 4 | **Worked example → completion → independent** on one paragraph | 12 min | Builds the sentence-level skill without demanding generation from nothing. |
| 5 | **Timed single questions** (12 min Q3, 20 min Q7), not full papers | 12–20 min | Retrieval + timing + interleaving in one. |
| 6 | **The 90-second proofread drill** | 2 min | AO5 is 18 marks across the paper and this is the cheapest access to it. |
| 7 | **Sentence combining** | 5 min | 0.50, and it fixes the comma splice and the endless sentence at the same time. |
| 8 | Full timed paper | 180 min | Worst ratio of the lot until the individual questions are secure. |

**Blunt conclusion:** a full past paper is the *least* efficient use of an hour you have, until each
question type is individually secure. Twelve-minute questions, marked, beat three-hour papers.

## 3. What IGCSE changes

Four structural facts about IGCSE (not GCSE, not A level) that decide which techniques transfer:

1. **The texts are unseen.** You cannot revise content, so content-recall techniques (flashcards,
   quote banks, mind maps of themes) are close to worthless here. What transfers is **procedure**.
   This is the single most important consequence on this page.
2. **Marking is levels-based with "best fit" and "all bullets secure".** The descriptors are published.
   So the highest-value knowledge is the *descriptor wording itself* — "clear", "thorough",
   "perceptive" — because that is the target you are aiming your sentences at.
3. **It is time-pressured (3 hours, 100 marks).** Automaticity matters: a routine you don't have to
   think about frees working memory for the actual thinking.
4. **The mark schemes and examiner reports are public.** Almost no other subject hands you the
   examiner's own words. This makes self-marking unusually reliable here.

## 4. What 4EB1 specifically rewards

Applying the above to the actual paper:

| Activity | Targets | Marks in reach | Minutes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Both texts in every Q7 paragraph | Q7 Level 2 cap (6/15) | up to 9 | 0 extra, just discipline |
| A fixed 3-quotation plan for Q3/Q6 | 20 marks of AO2 | up to 4 each | 12 per answer |
| Underline audience + form noun before Q8 | AO4 12 marks | 2–4 | 0 |
| One paragraph per bullet, both texts | AO1 10 marks | 3–5 | 0 |
| Proofread pass on B and C | AO5 18 marks | 2–4 | 4 total |
| Sentence combining drills | AO5 sentence variety | 1–2 | 5 per session |

**Where the marks are not:** memorising literary device names. Naming a device earns nothing on its
own — the descriptors reward *explaining effect*, so a device list is a vocabulary, not a technique.

## 5. The fading ladder — the answer to "I can't generate that sentence"

The reason the earlier lesson failed is instructive and it is a known effect. You were asked to do the
**last** step of a sequence with none of the earlier steps in place.

The evidence-backed order (Sweller; Renkl & Atkinson) is:

1. **Worked example** — read a complete, annotated model answer. Do not write anything.
2. **Completion problem** — a model with the final sentence removed; you write only that sentence.
   Paas found learners given half-worked problems performed *as well* as those given fully worked
   ones, with less mental effort.
3. **Faded completion** — the model is stripped back further; you write two sentences.
4. **Independent** — you write the paragraph from scratch.

Each step removes support only after the previous one is secure. Doing step 4 first is what produced
the "I can't do the mental gymnastics" reaction — and that reaction was correct. The task was
mis-sequenced, not beyond you.

## 6. The minimum effective routine

**Daily, 10 minutes (non-negotiable, this is the one that compounds):**

- 2 min: take one sentence from any text in `Paperlords_Archive`.
- 6 min: write rungs 1–5 for it, or complete one sentence in a faded model.
- 2 min: check against the grid; mark it.

**Twice a week, 25 minutes:** one timed question (alternate Q3, Q6, Q7), then mark it yourself against
the descriptor bullets. Write down the *one* rung you stopped at.

**Once a week, 45 minutes:** one Section B or C piece, timed, then proofread, then self-mark on all
grids.

**Once a fortnight, 3 hours:** a full paper, only once the individual questions are secure.

Do not add to this until it is actually being done. The failure mode is planning a heroic schedule,
doing it for four days, and stopping.

---

## 7. What to stop doing, explicitly

- Rereading the texts or these notes. Low utility, and it feels like work.
- Highlighting.
- Making notes from revision guides.
- Watching videos about the paper. (Low-intensity, high-familiarity, no retrieval.)
- Doing full papers and not marking them. Unmarked practice trains the wrong thing.
- Learning device names without effect sentences attached.
