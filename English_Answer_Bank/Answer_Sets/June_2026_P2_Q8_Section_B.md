## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed talk to peers

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content (printed p. 16) and the verified band descriptors. **No report or script exists in any accessible route.** Not a Pearson script, not examiner-marked, **no score claimed.**

> **MY PHONE DIED FOR THREE DAYS. HERE'S WHAT I LEARNED.**
>
> Before I start: check your screen time. Go on. It's in settings. Everyone in this room has done it before, and everyone has had the same reaction, which is a small, quiet noise of disbelief.
>
> Right. Now put it away, and I'll tell you what I found out when mine stopped working.
>
> **What they're actually good at**
>
> I'm not going to stand here and pretend smartphones are the enemy, because that would be dishonest and also you'd stop listening.
>
> They are, at their core, extraordinarily useful objects. I got here this morning using one: a bus timetable, a map, a message from a friend saying the room had changed. My mum's tickets for our holiday are on hers. My grandmother, who spent forty years queuing at banks, now does her banking on a screen the size of a playing card and thinks it's wonderful.
>
> They keep us together. You can speak to someone on the other side of the world for nothing, at any hour, and see their face while you do it. They entertain us, they get us home when we're lost, they tell us the time without anyone having to wear a watch, and they let a shy person send a message instead of standing in a corridor trying to find the words.
>
> That is not a small list. Any talk about phones that skips it is not a talk, it's a complaint.
>
> **What they're actually doing to us**
>
> But let's look at the numbers, because the numbers are grim. Research in the United States has put average daily screen time for children at around six hours, for eleven-to-fourteen-year-olds at nine, and for fifteen-to-eighteen-year-olds at seven and a half. Nine hours. That is a full school day plus homework, spent looking at glass.
>
> That time has to come from somewhere. It comes from sleep, from reading, from the thing you were going to make, from the person sitting opposite you at dinner. And it isn't just volume — it's the interruption. A phone on the table is a conversation with a third person in it.
>
> Then there's the comparison problem. Everyone else's life arrives filtered and edited, and yours arrives unfiltered and with a maths test in it. That is a machine for feeling inadequate, and we are all feeding it daily. The evidence linking heavy use to anxiety and low mood in teenagers is not trivial; researchers were documenting rising depression among adolescents well before the pandemic, and pointing at the same decade the smartphone arrived.
>
> And at the worst end: the phone is a delivery system for bullying that follows you into your bedroom. When I was small, a bad day at school ended when you got home. That door no longer closes.
>
> **What I actually do about it**
>
> Here is the part I can defend from experience, because my phone died on a plane to Croatia and I spent three days without one.
>
> The first day was awful. No boarding pass, no banking app, no map, no way to tell the time. I borrowed money. I asked a stranger for directions. I found a taxi at an actual rank, which was the strangest thing I did all week. And I discovered something uncomfortable: the world is not built for people without a phone. It's not that we are weak; it's that the infrastructure moved.
>
> But by day two my brain adapted, and here is what actually helped — none of it dramatic.
>
> **One:** charge it somewhere that isn't your bedroom. Mine lives in the kitchen overnight now. Not because I'm disciplined; because I'm lazy, and I won't walk downstairs at midnight.
>
> **Two:** set a real limit, not a vague intention. A number, in the settings, with an alarm. Vague intentions lose to engineered apps every single time, and the companies built those apps with teams of very clever people whose job is to beat your willpower.
>
> **Three:** put something in the gap. The reason we reach for the phone is that there's a two-minute hole in the day. Fill it — a book, a walk, a piano you never learned, a conversation with the person next to you. Blank space always loses to a screen.
>
> **Four:** know what you'd do if it broke. Screenshot your ticket, keep a card, memorise one phone number. Dependence is only frightening when it's unplanned.
>
> **And one thing I won't say**
>
> A lot of adults will tell you the answer is to take your phone away. I don't think that works, and neither does the evidence — bans are difficult to enforce, they push use underground, and they don't teach anybody anything. What actually changes behaviour is understanding what the app is doing to you and deciding, yourself, what you'll allow.
>
> So: check your screen time tonight. Then set one limit and one rule. Not because a teacher told you to — because you're the only person in this room who can.
>
> Thanks for listening. Now put your phones away and talk to the person next to you. Go on.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Positive:** the MS list is short and concrete — communication, emails, tickets, buying things, the time, entertainment, photos, maps, a simpler life. A candidate may add accessibility (text-to-speech, translation, hearing aids), safety (walking home with someone on the line), or schoolwork (a calendar, a camera for homework).
- **Negative:** MS and both texts supply volume of use, distraction, education, face-to-face loss, mental health, cyberbullying, addiction, influencer marketing, and over-reliance. A candidate may argue the *opposite* — that phones are simply a tool and the problem is app design — which is Text One's own position.
- **Advice:** MS allows rules, limits, parental guidance, online-safety education, government action and back-ups of documents. **A candidate may also argue for a ban** and score fully on that bullet; the MS lists it first. The one thing to avoid is asserting the ban works while also asserting it doesn't.
- **Unused Text One material:** the irony of learning about screen time on a screen; screen-time trackers; the CDC figures as a national average rather than an individual target; the Florida bill banning accounts for under-14s and requiring parental permission for 14–15s; Paul Renner's comment that children cannot see they are being sucked into addictive technology; the argument that adults need guidance on *talking* to young people about use.
- **Unused Text Two material:** the phone glitching then dying; 58 checks and about four and a half hours of scrolling a day; “ten-year-olds have iPhones now”; the phantom itch of not knowing if anyone has messaged; skipping TikTok at bedtime and mentally swiping through memories; the realisation that a phone is replaceable but the reliance is not.
- **Register:** a peer talk may be funny and blunt; it should not sound like a public-information film, and it should not slip into an article about screen time.
