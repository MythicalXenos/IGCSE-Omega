## Design notes, derived from the MS and the two locators (no report text available)

1. **The third bullet is a rhetorical problem, not a content problem.** “Rewards for the carers” cannot be answered in the third person without sounding either sentimental or clinical. The model answers it in the first person — a speaker who has actually done a shift at a wildlife rescue — which is also the most efficient way to hit AO4's “perceptually and subtly” communication band.
2. **The 9/30 locator is the warning.** It was marked down with wording close to the source texts. The model therefore quotes nothing from Theroux and nothing from the Sheldrick material; both are reworked into spoken sentences with the candidate's own detail added.
3. **The two texts are used for different bullets, not blended.** Theroux supplies the *relationship* material (recognition, attachment, the animal that chooses you), the Trust supplies the *procedure* material (rescue, milk formula, 24-hour care, reintegration). Blending them produces a talk that sounds like a leaflet; separating them produces an argument.
4. **Length and pacing:** roughly 600 words, because a talk of this kind needs a spoken rhythm — short paragraphs, one direct question, one deliberate pause (“I'll be honest with you…”).

## [CONSTRUCTED GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed talk

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content and the two published locators, using verified details from both texts with no quoted wording. **Not** a Pearson script, **not** examiner-marked, **no score claimed.**

> Last spring I spent four Saturdays at a wildlife rescue about forty minutes from here, and most of what I did was hold things. I held a heat pad under a box of ducklings. I held a syringe at the right angle for a hedgehog that had been hit by a car. I held a towel over a cage so a jackdaw would stop panicking. I am telling you this at the start because it is the honest version of what looking after orphaned animals involves, and because the last thing I am going to talk about is what you get out of it.
>
> **How you help them**
>
> The first thing an orphan needs is not affection. It is warmth, food and protection, in that order, and getting the order wrong can kill it. A rescued bird or mammal arrives cold, dehydrated and often injured, so you warm it slowly, you get fluids into it, and you keep it away from noise, cats and your own helpful hands.
>
> Then the food, which is more specialised than people expect. Baby elephants cannot drink just any milk; they need a formula developed specifically for them, fortified with extra nutrition, and they are fed every three hours around the clock. That is the reality of it — not a cute photoshoot, but somebody awake at 2am with a bottle.
>
> Then companionship. Orphans do not just need to be kept alive, they need to be kept company: someone in the room, something to play with, something to explore. At the elephant nursery the keepers sleep in the stockades with the calves so they are not alone, and they walk with them in the bush, take them into varied surroundings, play games with them and cover them with blankets when it is cold and sunscreen when it is hot. You can do the smaller version of that at home with a box, a hot-water bottle wrapped in a towel, and time.
>
> **What they get from it**
>
> Safety, obviously. But look at what else. An orphan that is warm, fed and not frightened starts to behave like an animal again — it plays, it groans with contentment, it preens, it sleeps properly. A goose that was a handful of fluff in a cage will, within a month, grow feathers, and within a few weeks after that will answer to its name. It will follow you. It will defend you, in its own small way, from other geese. Its character comes back. That is what a caring environment actually delivers: not survival, personality.
>
> **What you get out of it**
>
> Now the part nobody warns you about. You get attached, and that is the reward and the risk in the same package. The writer Paul Theroux describes raising a goose called Willy, and what stays with you in his account is not the feeding — it is the recognition. Willy knew his name. Willy spotted him from a hundred yards away and called out. Willy sat with him on a low wall while he stroked the down on the bird's breast, and when Theroux felt uninspired and useless as a writer, the goose was a consolation.
>
> That is the first reward: company, from something that did not have to give it. The second is competence — a sense of responsibility that turns out to be genuinely satisfying, because you can see what your hands did. The third is quieter. There is a particular peace in sitting with an animal that has stopped being afraid of you.
>
> And the fourth is the one that gets people hooked. You watch progress. You see the day it feeds itself, the day it flies, the day it stops needing you. At the elephant nursery they take this all the way: from about the age of three the orphans move to reintegration units, they are gradually weaned, they learn wild behaviour from wild herds, and eventually they live independently — though they come back to visit their human family, sometimes with their own calves. Imagine being the person who made that possible and then being visited.
>
> **One thing to take away**
>
> You do not have to go to Africa. There is a rescue within an hour of this school that needs people to hold towels and wash syringes, and there is a bird on the pavement outside in June that needs a box and a phone call. Start with the box. Everything else — the warmth, the food, the company, the attachment, the day it flies off — follows.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Subject alternatives:** the MS explicitly allows wider examples — a fox cub, a lamb, a kitten, a baby hedgehog, a nestling fallen from a nest, a seal pup. A talk built on one local case, developed properly, satisfies all three bullets.
- **Third bullet alternatives:** the MS's seven rewards are all available; “a sense of responsibility” and “watching the positive development” are the easiest to evidence, while “when wild animals and birds return with young” is the most distinctive and is used here.
- **Register:** a talk may be warm, funny, brisk or earnest. What the bullet structure demands is that the speaker is a person, not an information board — the rewards bullet cannot be met by describing what other people feel.
- **Unused Text One material:** the “living ornament” description (p. 3, lines 30–34), the night-time image of white geese on moonstruck grass (p. 3, lines 39–42), and the observation that animals behave differently in front of strangers (p. 3, lines 43–49) would all support a more lyrical talk.
- **Unused Text Two material:** the rescue logistics (vehicles, aircraft, helicopter, stretchers, drips, p. 4, lines 1–8) and the mini-matriarchs (older female orphans who help raise the babies, p. 5, lines 18–22) are strong, concrete and unused here.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO1:** all three bullets are separately developed, with Text Two carrying help and Text One carrying the relationship; every specific detail is traceable to a booklet page and none is quoted.
- **AO4:** a talk, not an essay — direct address, one rhetorical question, an anecdote that earns the third bullet, spoken signposts as subheadings, and a closing action the audience can take.
- **AO5:** varied punctuation (semicolons, dashes, a colon, a parenthetical list), controlled sentence rhythm, correct apostrophes, no comma splicing, and the tricky vocabulary of the sources (*dehydrated*, *fortified*, *reintegration*, *weaned*) used accurately.
- **Not claimed:** no examiner mark, level or score. Pearson's 30/30 and 9/30 on this question belong to scripts this route cannot read; the model has not been marked and is not equated with either.
