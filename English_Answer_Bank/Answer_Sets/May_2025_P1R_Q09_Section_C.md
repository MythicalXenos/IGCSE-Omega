## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed discursive answer

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 20) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 21–22), with method taken from this paper's own report (pp. 46–48). **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **More interested in celebrities than in their friends and family?**
>
> The statement sounds like a complaint a parent would make, and like most complaints it is half true in a way that hides the more interesting half. People are certainly more interested in celebrities than they were, and they are certainly more *aware* of them. Whether that awareness has replaced anything is another question, and it is the question the statement actually asks.
>
> Start with the strongest version of the yes case, because it is stronger than it looks. A follower can learn from an influencer: how to cook a thing, how to train for a race, how to leave a bad situation with some dignity. The parasocial relationship is also undemanding in a way that real ones are not. Nobody's mother has ever delivered an eight-minute story about a stranger's kitchen and then asked you to look at her phone. The celebrity does not ask. That is the appeal, and it is not stupid — it is rest.
>
> But notice what has been smuggled into the claim. Interest is being measured by **visibility**. A person's Instagram appears on a screen ten times a day; a friend appears on a sofa twice a week. If you count appearances, the celebrity wins by a distance. If you count what a person would do if you rang them at two in the morning, the arithmetic reverses completely. The MS for this paper puts the same point more drily: celebrities and influencers “did not know them”. Interest that cannot be returned is not the same kind of interest as interest that can.
>
> There is also a distinction between attention and investment. I can tell you the name of a footballer's dog and nothing at all about my cousin's new job, and one of those gaps is embarrassing in a way the other is not. The dog is a fact with no consequence. The job is a fact I might be asked about, and being asked is what a family is.
>
> So: to a small extent I agree. For some people, at some times — the lonely, the very young, the person whose family has already let them down — the screen may genuinely have replaced the people. But for most of us the relationship is not a substitution; it is an addition, and additions are not usually the thing that breaks a household. What breaks it is not celebrity at all, but the habit of taking the people who cannot leave for granted.
>
> The statement, in the end, gets the direction of the danger backwards. It worries that we love strangers too much. The likelier problem is that we notice them too much, and the people who would actually answer the phone too little.
>

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Stance:** agree (the majority position, and perfectly viable if supported); disagree (as here); or “to a small/considerable extent”, which is the honest reading of “to what extent”.
- **Content the MS rewards:** interest in people/ideas from other perspectives; celebrities “more interesting than people you are familiar with”; learning from influencers; family/friends knowing you and advising you; obsession; bad influence.
- **Techniques the report rewards:** rhetorical questions, counterargument, analogy, a clear introduction/conclusion, a logical order of points.
- **What to avoid:** a one-sided rant; agreeing then contradicting yourself; a list of “Instagram is bad” complaints; unsupported statistics; a conclusion that repeats the introduction word for word.
