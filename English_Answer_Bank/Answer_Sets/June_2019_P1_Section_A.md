### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Fretter first focuses on Montel, describing him as an “outstanding” sailor who has won the “prestigious” Young Sailor of the Year trophy. This opens with one recognisable success before widening to the whole sailing programme. Jon Holt’s direct quotation then lists what Montel did: he did “EVERY” fundraising talk, took “EVERY” sailing opportunity, charmed sponsors and persuaded companies to support the project. The capitals and repeated “EVERY” stress how persistent he was; the accumulating examples present the award as earned through effort as well as sailing ability.

The article sets the group’s achievements against the barriers they faced. Fretter gives precise details: “over 70 per cent” of pupils receive free school meals, “over 50 different first languages” are spoken at the school, and “around 73 per cent” are classed as disadvantaged. These statistics, followed by the pupils being “markedly different” from a typical ocean-racing crew, establish the social context. Fretter then says they became the “first state school to compete in the race”. That contrast makes their participation itself seem significant before the reader even considers their result.

The account also follows a clear progression from modest beginnings to demanding competition. The first boat was a “dilapidated 22ft yacht on eBay”; Holt recalls, “we didn’t have a boat that could do that” when the boys wanted to enter long-distance races. The students helped raise the money through 40–50 talks, and the yacht they later raced needed considerable work. Against that starting point, finishing “142 out of 368 boats” in the Fastnet Race and going on to Cowes Week make their achievement more impressive. The figures give the account a factual basis, while the sequence explains what the pupils had to overcome. Fretter also says “over 1000 pupils have gone sailing with Greig City Academy since it began the programme”, showing that the project’s reach extends beyond the original crew.

Holt’s recollections add a personal, enthusiastic view of the experience. He admits he expected that parts of the race might be too much for the students, then says they were “absolutely buzzing” and remembers Montel surfing downwind in very big waves. The question “When’s the next race?” captures their eagerness to continue. Fretter therefore presents achievement not only as a result or award, but also as the students’ resilience, new skills and appetite for another challenge. The positive ending leaves the programme sounding both successful and inspiring.

**MS alignment check:** This response develops the MS’s points about the positive description of Montel, repetition of “EVERY”, the school’s context and statistics, the contrast between the first yacht and later races, the pupils’ determination, their learning and Holt’s enthusiastic quotations (indicative content, MS p. 5). The AO2 Level 5 descriptor requires perceptive analysis with discriminating references (MS p. 6); the indicative list is not exhaustive. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Dowshen opens with two short questions: “Bored?” and “Looking for a new challenge?” Their direct address makes the reader consider their own free time immediately. He then lists familiar sports and clubs, from basketball and tennis to music and science. The range makes it easy for different readers to picture an option, while the heading “What are the benefits of after-study activities?” moves the article from choice to persuasion.

The writer repeatedly uses “you” and “your” to make the benefits feel personal. The list of “physical, creative, social, political, and career interests” suggests that activities can develop more than one part of a person’s life. “You’ll find friends” and “like-minded people” foreground belonging, while meeting people “different from you” and breaking down barriers offers a wider social benefit. Mentioning college and job applications also gives a practical reason to take part, alongside enjoyment and friendship.

The question-shaped subheadings guide the reader through decisions: how to find a suitable activity, how to get involved and what to do about taking on too much. Within those sections, imperatives such as “Review the activities”, “Think about different roles” and “Ask questions” turn encouragement into clear steps. “Don’t limit yourself to the familiar – try something new” uses a direct command to nudge the reader towards trying an unfamiliar option.

The final section makes the advice reassuring rather than pressuring. Dowshen recognises that it is easy to join too many activities and reminds readers that “Everyone needs downtime”. The phrase “If an activity adds lots of stress to your life, it’s not for you” gives permission to choose carefully or stop. Ending with the need to “keep a balance” acknowledges school, work, social life and health; this practical tone makes the invitation to participate seem manageable.

**MS alignment check:** This response develops the MS’s points about the opening questions, lists, question subheadings, benefits, direct address, imperatives, informal tone and the reassuring advice in the final paragraphs (indicative content, MS p. 8). The AO2 Level 5 descriptor is on MS p. 9. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers present after-study activities positively, but they do so from different angles. Fretter reports on one real sailing programme and shows what a particular group achieved; Dowshen addresses young readers directly and advises them about the many activities they might try. Text One is mainly an informative account with an inspirational tone, whereas Text Two is more openly persuasive and practical.

Fretter builds her case through a specific example. She begins with Montel’s award, then uses Holt’s detailed account and the school’s circumstances to show why the team’s progress is notable. Statistics about disadvantage, the “dilapidated 22ft yacht” and the first-state-school milestone create a clear contrast between the pupils’ starting point and their later participation in the Fastnet Race and Cowes Week. Dowshen, by contrast, does not follow one group’s story. His opening “Bored? Looking for a new challenge?” addresses the reader, and his lists of sports, clubs and benefits present a wide range of possible choices.

Their structures serve those different purposes. Fretter moves from Montel to the school context, then recounts how the pupils obtained and restored a boat and what they experienced at sea. Holt’s direct speech—including “they were absolutely buzzing” and the pupils’ “When’s the next race?”—lets readers hear the team’s enthusiasm from someone involved. Dowshen organises his advice under questions such as “How do I find the right activity for me?” and “How do I get involved?” His repeated “you” and imperatives like “try something new” make the guidance feel personal and actionable.

The writers also present the benefits in distinct ways. Fretter demonstrates them through the sailing crew’s achievements and the skills Holt says they gained, including resilience and interpersonal skills. Dowshen spells out potential benefits for any reader: friendships, meeting people from different backgrounds, developing interests and strengthening college or job applications. Both use positive examples to encourage participation, but Fretter makes the case through a real programme’s outcome, while Dowshen sets out reasons and steps the reader can apply to their own life.

Their endings maintain the contrast. Holt’s account closes with pupils already eager for “the next race”, leaving the reader with their excitement. Dowshen ends with advice to balance activities with schoolwork, work, social life and health, so his encouragement is tempered by a warning not to overcommit. One text celebrates what a determined group has achieved; the other encourages readers to choose an activity that suits them.

**MS alignment check:** This comparison is sustained across both texts, addresses ideas and perspectives as well as method, and balances references. It develops the MS’s contrasts between Text One’s informative account of one activity and Text Two’s persuasive guidance about many options; it also recognises the enthusiasm and positive perspective shared by both (indicative content, MS pp. 10–11). The AO3 Level 5 descriptor is on MS p. 11. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.
