> **Challenge is a tool, not a medicine**
>
> There is a sentence in every school assembly that sounds like this one, and it is nearly true, which is what makes it dangerous.
>
> Consider what the case for actually rests on. A challenge, usefully pitched, does something no amount of encouragement can: it shows you where your limit is and then, occasionally, moves it. The student who has taught herself to sit with a problem for forty minutes without giving up has learned something more durable than the content of the problem. Overcoming something difficult builds a particular kind of confidence — not the confidence of being told you are capable, but the confidence of having been incapable on Tuesday. That is why people who have faced real challenges often go on to do difficult things; they have evidence.
>
> So far, so agreeable. But the statement says *all*, and it says *need*, and both words are doing more work than the evidence supports. Look at what the mark scheme for this kind of question quietly admits: challenges can be daunting or overwhelming, people can be frightened or intimidated, and some people simply do not respond well. This is not weakness; it is arithmetic. A challenge that arrives before a person has any of the resources to meet it is not a lesson, it is a defeat with a lesson's branding. I have watched a capable student fail a test she was set two years too early and conclude, permanently, that she was bad at the subject. Nothing about that experience taught her anything except how to avoid the subject.
>
> And the last point is the one that decides the argument for me. People who do not like challenges may find different, or better, ways to learn. That is not a concession; it is a description of most of human progress. Reading is a way of learning without being challenged by a tiger. A good teacher, a demonstration, a worked example — all of these are ways of acquiring skill with the difficulty temporarily removed, and there is no shame in that. Challenge is one route to learning. It is not the road.
>
> So, to what extent do I agree? Substantially, and with the sentence repaired. We all need *some* difficulty, at the right moment, with support nearby. We do not all need to be challenged in the same way, and we certainly do not need to be challenged constantly. A challenge you can survive teaches you something. A challenge that defeats you teaches you to stop, and no amount of assembly language changes that.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Positions that score:** agree with a condition about pitch and support (as here); agree fully using the confidence and success evidence; disagree by defending scaffolded, low-risk learning; agree only for older or self-selecting learners.
- **MS material to use:** realising potential; being the best you can be; confidence from overcoming; successful people have faced challenges; challenges daunting or overwhelming; people frightened or intimidated; some do not respond well; others find different or better ways to learn.
- **Support that works:** the idea of “desirable difficulty” pitched correctly; a personal or observed example of a badly timed challenge; the value of worked examples and scaffolds.
- **What to avoid:** an entirely one-sided answer; “what doesn't kill you makes you stronger” repeated; a story; a list of famous people who overcame things with no analysis; ignoring the word “all”.
