## Matching MS criteria

**Indicative content (MS p. 17):** “Candidates may choose a range of walks which may be real or imaginary. Answers may include descriptions of the walk and what they saw, heard, smelled and felt. Candidates should be rewarded for their powers to evoke a sense of what the walk involves and descriptions of senses and feelings, using effective vocabulary. Examiners should be open to a wide range of interpretation.”

**AO4 — 20 marks, Questions 9, 10 and 11 (MS p. 18):** *Communicate effectively and imaginatively, adapting form, tone and register of writing for specific purposes and audiences.*

- **Level 3 (9–12):** communicates clearly; clear sense of purpose and understanding of the expectations/requirements of the intended reader; appropriate use of form, tone and register.
- **Level 4 (13–16):** communicates successfully; a secure realisation of purpose and the expectations/requirements of the intended reader; effective use of form, tone and register.
- **Level 5 (17–20):** communication is perceptive and subtle; task is sharply focused on purpose and the expectations/requirements of the intended reader; sophisticated use of form, tone and register.

**AO5 — 10 marks, Questions 9, 10 and 11 (MS p. 19):** *Write clearly, using a range of vocabulary and sentence structures, with appropriate paragraphing and accurate spelling, grammar and punctuation.*

- **Level 3 (5–6):** develops and connects appropriate information and ideas; structural features and paragraphing make meaning clear; varied vocabulary; irregular spellings accurate; accurate and varied punctuation.
- **Level 4 (7–8):** manages information and ideas cohesively and deliberately; wide, selective vocabulary with only occasional spelling errors; punctuation positioned for clarity with sentence structures managed for deliberate effect.
- **Level 5 (9–10):** manipulates complex ideas using a range of structural and grammatical features; extensive vocabulary used strategically; punctuation accurate to aid emphasis and precision, with sentence structures chosen selectively for effect.

## What the script does — and where it falls short

These observations are mine, keyed to the published criteria and the general Q11 commentary; they are **not** Pearson commentary and carry no marks.

**Strengths the criteria recognise:**

- **Sustained single-route structure.** The walk runs in sequence — pine wood, slope and stream, gravel path and stallion, sheep fields, forest, hilltop view — so the reader is moved through a place rather than given a static scene. This answers the general examiner complaint (p. 56) about candidates who “described a scene… without including details of walking through the scene.”
- **Sensory range, especially sound.** “Ravens caw,” “the sound of a gurgling… clear stream,” “the whistling wind, the caw of ravens,” “almost silent as I walk along a pine needle blanket.” The MS explicitly rewards what candidates “saw, heard, smelled and felt.”
- **Graphic layout used as technique.** The one-line paragraphs (“A busy farmer herds sheep…”, “Past more fields. / Past more sheep.”) are the “short paragraphs” Pearson names as a feature, and they control pace.
- **Bracketed aside for tone.** “a formidable (although small in size) forest” is the exact phrase Pearson quotes as evidence of “crafting”; the parenthesis deflates the adjective and creates a wry, individual voice.
- **Withheld climax.** The view is delayed until the final two paragraphs, which is why the ending (“none will show — maybe some — but never all the true beauty of the view”) reads as earned rather than listed.

**Weaknesses consistent with Level 4 rather than Level 5:**

- **Spelling.** “furr-tree” (fir), “tracture” (probably intended “tractors”), “conopy” self-corrected to “canopy”. Pearson says “quite a lot of spelling errors,” which is precisely what keeps AO5 at Level 4, where Level 5 requires only rare errors.
- **Word-class slips and unclear phrasing.** “their content grazing” (intended “contented”), “by the gaze of their protective mothers” (for “under the gaze”), “by this was nature after all” (for “but this was nature”). Meaning survives, but precision dips.
- **Lapses in verb tense.** Present tense throughout (“I walk,” “I jog,” “I come”) except “The only noise to be heard was… this was true peace,” where the past tense intrudes without purpose. This is the kind of inconsistency that separates secure control from a “sophisticated” register.
- **Repetition under pressure.** “dots… spots… dots… dotted” in the final description is a deliberate attempt at patterning that partly collapses into repetition of the same word.

## General Q11 commentary (ER printed pp. 56–57), kept separate from this script’s judgement

- A significant number of candidates “did not focus on the descriptive nature of the task and wrote narrative responses focusing on re-telling the events of a day out rather than describing the walk itself.”
- Many described “a scene, sometimes in some detail, without including details of walking through the scene.”
- More successful candidates created “a sense of movement along the walk,” used “an impressive range of vocabulary,” and maintained “a clear focus on describing a walk using visual and auditory experiences.”
- Less successful responses were “undeveloped or unclear,” spent too long “getting to the walk,” or reused “a prepared essay of a described scene” not made relevant to a walk.
- Common language errors listed at the end of p. 57 include missing basic sentence punctuation, comma splicing, missing or misused apostrophes, homophone confusions, missing capitals and subject–verb agreement problems.

This is question-level feedback, not an extra score attribution for this candidate; the published reasons for 23/30 are the p. 62 comments above.
