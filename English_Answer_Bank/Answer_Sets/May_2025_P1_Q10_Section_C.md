- Most candidates “develop[ed] a basic storyline with an identifiable beginning, middle and end; introduce[d] a central character or situation that related in some way to a ‘first impression’… attempt[ed] a resolution or twist that reflected a change or revelation about the first impression. They tried to bring the qualities of surprise, drama, suspense and excitement to their narratives.”
- **More successful:** “entertaining and engaging responses that were sharply focused on the task… a clear narrative structure… fewer characters which were well-developed and a well-thought-out plot, designed to entertain… figurative techniques and wide-ranging vocabulary deliberately to achieve particular effects… a range of structural techniques… for example through flashbacks, varied pacing, and well-placed dialogue.”
- **Less successful:** “over-complicated or muddled storylines and weak endings that were not closely related to the events that had unfolded… lengthy with repetitive and unfocused plot ideas… plots from films, television series and computer games which were barely adapted for purpose.”

**Consequences for the model:** **two** characters, one room and one hour of story time; the title withheld until the final sentence *and earned there* rather than bolted on; the twist turning on a misreading, not on a coincidence; dialogue doing structural work; and punctuation varied on purpose.

## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed story

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 21), the AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 22–23) and this paper's own report (pp. 47–48). **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **The First Impression**
>
> The girl in front of me in the queue for the sixth-form open day had brought a clipboard. Not a folder, not a phone with a list on it — a clipboard, with a printed timetable clipped to it and three different colours of highlighter laid across the top like cutlery.
>
> ‘You’re early,’ she said, without turning round.
>
> ‘So are you.’
>
> ‘I’m organised. It’s different.’ She did turn then, and looked at me the way a doctor looks at a rash. ‘You’ve got the wrong building. Biology’s in C Block.’
>
> I hadn’t asked. I had been standing in that queue for eleven minutes, and she had decided, without evidence, that I was a person who got the wrong building. I decided, also without evidence, that she was one of those people who would be running a country by thirty and would not be much fun at parties in the meantime.
>
> Her name, it turned out, was Fiyona, and she was in three of my four classes by September. She sat at the front. She answered everything. When Mr Okafor asked for a volunteer to take the minutes of the debate club, her hand was up before the question had ended, and I said something to the boy next to me — quiet, or so I thought — about people who liked the sound of their own handwriting.
>
> She heard. Of course she heard. She heard everything, that was the whole problem with her.
>
> What I did not know, and could not have known from a clipboard, was that Fiyona had spent the summer translating her mother’s hospital letters. She had been the one who rang the consultant, the one who wrote things down, the one who learned that a person who does not write things down loses them. Organisation was not a personality; it was a survival strategy with a highlighter on top.
>
> And what she did not know about me was that I had been the new boy in three schools and had learned to make a joke of a stranger before the stranger could make one of me.
>
> We were made to work together on the debate club motion in October. She arrived with a clipboard. I arrived with nothing, late, and a joke about people who bring clipboards. She laughed — properly, head back — and then said, ‘You do that to everyone, don’t you. Get in first.’ Which was fair, and unkind, and completely accurate.
>
> We won the debate. She took the minutes. I took the credit, then gave it back.
>
> She is my closest friend now, and I have never once told her what I thought of her in that queue in June. But I think about it whenever I meet someone who is too much of something — too loud, too neat, too eager — and I remind myself that a first impression is not a photograph of a person. It is a photograph of the half-second in which you were looking.
>

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Titles/angles:** a first impression of a teacher, a step-parent, a flatmate, a border officer, a rival at a tournament; the narrator as the one *judged*; a first impression of a place rather than a person — the MS specifies no content.
- **Reveal point:** last sentence (safest and explicitly permitted); or a mid-story reveal with the title echoed at the close.
- **"True or imaginary":** both are permitted in the QP itself. An autobiographical story is not advantaged.
- **What to avoid:** a plot borrowed from a film or game; more than three characters; a twist that requires new information invented in the final paragraph; the title used as the first line and then explained.
- **Length:** “approximately 400 words”. The 30/30 exemplar is longer than 400 (it occupies four report pages including formatting); length is not the mark-earner — control is. This model sits at 500.
