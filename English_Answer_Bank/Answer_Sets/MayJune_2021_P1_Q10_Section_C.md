> **Doing the Right Thing**
>
> The rule in the pharmacy is written on the wall behind the till in a laminated card: NO REFUNDS WITHOUT A RECEIPT. On the Thursday before Eid, a man came in with no receipt and a box of insulin that had been in a fridge that had stopped working.
>
> I am twenty-two and I have worked there for five months, and I want to say straight away that I know exactly what the rule is for. People steal things and bring them back. People buy a dress, wear it, and return it. The card on the wall is not there because my manager is cruel; it is there because he has been robbed four times and once by a member of his own family.
>
> The man put the box on the counter and said, *The fridge went off in the night. I have the money for a new one but not until Tuesday.*
>
> The box was cold. I remember that, because I checked it with the back of my hand, and it was cold, which meant he had kept it in something cold after the fridge died, which meant he had been thinking about it since the early hours. He was wearing a shirt that had been ironed carefully. That is the detail I cannot get out of my head — the ironed shirt.
>
> My manager was in the back, doing the order. He would have said no. He would have been within his rights, and the shop would have lost forty-two pounds either way, because the box was worth nothing now: the insulin had been out of a regulated fridge for six hours and I could not put it back on the shelf and I could not sell it.
>
> That was the whole argument, and it took me about nine seconds. I could not sell it. I could only throw it away or give it to him. The rule existed to stop me being cheated, and there was nothing left to cheat me out of.
>
> So I took the box back, which is what the rule allows, and I gave him a replacement from the shelf and put the old one in the bin with a note for the pharmacist, and I wrote the loss in the book in my own handwriting, and I told my manager when he came out and he looked at the book for a long time and then said, *It is your pay.*
>
> He did not mean it. He meant that he had been put in a position where he had to decide whether to be the sort of man who docks a twenty-two-year-old for this, and he had decided he was not, and he was annoyed about it.
>
> I lost nothing. The man came back on Tuesday and paid for the new box and bought a card, and my manager never mentioned it again. But I have thought since about how easy it would have been to say *no receipt* — how completely correct it would have sounded — and about how the rule and the right thing were pointing in the same direction all along, and only one of us had checked.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Interpretations of the title:** a moral dilemma at work; a loyalty conflict between friends; a small honesty nobody sees; a decision that costs the protagonist and is never rewarded; doing the right thing badly.
- **Structures:** one shift, one decision (as here); two characters arguing a case; the decision made in the first paragraph and its cost explored afterwards.
- **Ways to make “right” contestable:** give the opposing character a real justification; let the protagonist be tempted; let the outcome be quiet rather than triumphant.
- **What to avoid:** a saint with no price to pay; a villain with no reason; a film plot (the wallet in the street, the exam cheat, the drowning child); gore; a dream; the title bolted onto an unrelated story.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO4:** the decision is argued inside the narrative (there is nothing left to sell; the rule's purpose no longer applies), so the title is a conclusion rather than a caption; the manager is given a defensible position and a real irritation.
- **AO5:** varied sentence lengths, a colon introducing the laminated rule, deliberate repetition (“I could not sell it. I could only throw it away”), and concrete objects carrying the moral weight — the cold box, the ironed shirt, the loss book.
