## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed discursive answer

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's **own mark scheme** (printed p. 18 content and pp. 19–20 grids) and its **own examiner report**. **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **The best things in life are free — and that is the problem**
>
> The statement is true in the way that a good slogan is true: it survives the first reading and collapses on the second. Nothing on the list is free. Not the sea, not a friendship, not the afternoon you spend with your grandmother. All of it is paid for, just not at a till.
>
> Take the sea first, because it is the easiest case. Standing on a beach costs nothing, which is why the statement sounds convincing. But to stand on that beach a person needs a bus fare, a day off, and the sort of life that permits a day off. The water is free; the access is not. This is the distinction the statement hides. Almost nothing in the world is free of charge, and almost nothing is free of cost — and the second kind of cost is the one that decides who gets to enjoy the first.
>
> Friendship is the sharper example. It arrives without an invoice, so we call it free. But it is maintained by a hundred small payments: the phone call you do not feel like making, the birthday you remember, the two hours you spend listening to a problem that is not yours. People who do not make those payments do not have friends; they have acquaintances who are drifting. The price is not money, but it is a price, and it is charged regularly.
>
> Here the defence of the statement has something real to say. Money is not the only currency, and the things that cost no money are not therefore worthless — they are, in fact, the things that cannot be bought at all, which is why a rich person can be poor. That is a genuine and important point, and I do not want to lose it. My disagreement is with the word “free”, not with the list.
>
> There is also a counter-argument that has to be taken seriously, because it is the one most people my age actually make: that you need money for most of what you enjoy — the concert, the trip, the new phone — and that a life without those things is narrow. I think this is true and also sad, and it is worth noticing why. We have built a world in which the cheapest pleasures require the most expensive infrastructure. A public park is free; a city with a public park is not.
>
> So I would rewrite the statement rather than agree or disagree with it. The best things in life are not free — they are **unpriced**, which is a better and stranger word. They cost time, attention, patience and the willingness to be bored with somebody. Those are not things a person can save up. They can only be spent as they arrive, and they run out.
>
