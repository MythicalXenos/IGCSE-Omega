## What the November 2021 report adds — quoted

**AO1:**

- “The majority of candidates used the bullet points provided… They took a systematic approach to the bullet points and wrote about them in order.”
- “some candidates **did not cover the first bullet point** ‘Reasons why people might want to change their outlook’ sufficiently and sometimes did not refer to it at all. The coverage of the third bullet point ‘How some negativity might be good for us’ was **occasionally rushed and relied too heavily on the texts**.”
- Better candidates: “used all three bullet points, taking ideas from the texts and **extending and personalising** these, to produce convincing and persuasive contributions… dealt with the first bullet point **inventively**… with relevant and personal examples linked to their own life experiences and **perhaps returned to these ideas at the end** of their response.”
- Weaker candidates: “Often, they **only focused on one bullet point, generally the second**. They lifted too many of their arguments from the texts and they also **referred to the authors of the articles, quoting lines from them** which reduced the effectiveness of their response.”

**AO4:**

- “Most candidates were able to adapt the material for the audience and purpose, with **suitable salutations and valedictions**… although a significant number wrote in an **essay register which they then topped and tailed with a greeting and a valediction**.”
- “Occasionally candidates used sub-headings. It was **not entirely obvious if these were for the benefit of the examiner**… or if the titles were to be considered as part of the talk.”
- Better: “lively and engaging style… **inclusive address and an informal tone**… some were quite entertaining often adding personal experiences and using humour or empathy.”
- Weaker: “often only acknowledging the register at the beginning and ending of their response… often writing in a style that **resembled an article or essay**.”

**AO5:** common errors were missing basic sentence punctuation; comma splicing; missing or misused apostrophes; homophones; misspelling of basic vocabulary; not capitalising ‘I’; missing capital letters at sentence starts; verb tense errors. Weaker candidates wrote “long, one sentence paragraphs”.

**Design consequences, stated plainly:** the talk has **no bookend structure** — the spoken address continues through the middle, not only at the edges; the first bullet point opens the talk with real examples and is explicitly returned to at the end, because the report says the best answers did exactly that; nothing is attributed to “an article I read”, because the report says naming the authors weakened responses; and there are no subheadings, to avoid the ambiguity the report describes.

## [CONSTRUCTED GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed talk

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content and the November 2021 report guidance, using verified details from both texts. **Not** a Pearson script, **not** examiner-marked, **no score claimed.**

> Two weeks ago I watched someone I like very much lose a whole evening to a maths deadline. Not to the work — to the worrying about the work. She sat down at four, opened the document, and at nine o'clock she had written a title and rearranged her pens twice. Nothing about her ability changed in those five hours. Only how she felt about it.
>
> That is why I want to talk about outlook. Not because I think any of us should be permanently cheerful — I will come back to that — but because the way we frame a problem quietly decides whether we start it. Here are the reasons you might want to change yours. Stress that turns into paralysis. Deadlines that expand to fill the time you give them. Feeling stuck at home and losing touch with people you used to see. Being ill, or worried about money at home. Being angry about something unfair and not knowing where to put it. None of those is solved by thinking happy thoughts, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. What changes is whether you still have room to act.
>
> So how do you do it, practically? First, do not try to install positivity like an app. You cannot pick up a violin and play; you cannot wake up and decide to be an optimist. You practise, in very small bursts. Notice one good thing on the walk home. Write down, at the end of the day, one thing that actually went right — not five, one. When you are stressed, take a breath, list what you have already got done, and tell yourself you will do the next bit as well as you can. That is not positive thinking in the poster sense. It is just refusing to let one bad hour describe the entire week.
>
> Second, aim your effort outward. Hold the door, send the message, help someone with the thing you are good at. It is the least efficient advice in the world and it works better than almost anything else, because being useful is the fastest cure for feeling useless. Third, use your face. Smiling genuinely reduces stress, and it is contagious, which means you get to infect people.
>
> Now the part I actually care about, because it is the part people get wrong. Positivity is not always good. If you tell yourself everything is fine when it is not, you are not coping; you are postponing. People who use cheerfulness as a defence do not feel the anxiety that would have pushed them to act, and the bad job, the looming deadline, the problem with a friend stays exactly where it was. Too much positivity can even be a warning sign: relentless, ungrounded elation is one of the symptoms of mania.
>
> Negative feelings have jobs. Anger at an injustice is what makes people campaign. Being in a low mood can make your arguments sharper and more persuasive, because you check your own claims before you make them. Worry makes you careful; it makes you prepare; it stops the problem developing. Pessimists are often the ones with the contingency plan.
>
> So the goal is not to become a permanently sunny person. It is balance: take the optimistic view where it helps you start, and keep the realistic one where it helps you finish. Stop and smell the roses — but look at the pavement first.
>
> Which brings me back to that Tuesday evening. My friend did not need to be told to cheer up. She needed someone to say: you have done this before, start with the easiest question, and I will check it at eight. That is what a useful outlook sounds like, and it is something we can all offer each other.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **The MS is non-exhaustive**; a candidate may discuss bereavement, exam failure, body image, family illness, moving country, or online comparison as reasons to change outlook — provided the other bullets get real treatment.
- **Structure:** a talk may run bullets 1 and 2 together (reasons and remedies) and give bullet 3 a punchy separate section, or reverse it. What the report penalises is skipping bullet 1 or rushing bullet 3 by lifting from the texts.
- **Unused Text One material:** the article's own scepticism about “turn that frown upside down” advice (p. 2, lines 3–8), the meditation exercise imagining a crowd of people who all want to be happy (p. 3, lines 32–34), and nurturing relationships through supportive messages (p. 3, lines 48–53) are all legitimate and unused here.
- **Unused Text Two material:** the *Positive Power of Negative Thinking* research on defensive pessimism, and the finding that people with low self-esteem felt worse after repeating “I'm a lovable person” (p. 5, lines 1–3), would support a sharper version of bullet 3.
- **Register:** a talk to peers may be warm, blunt, funny or confessional; the report's successful description (“inclusive address and an informal tone… humour or empathy”) is compatible with all of them.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO1:** all three bullets are developed, with the two the report says are usually neglected (first and third) given the most space; both texts are used and reworked, with personal material added; nothing is attributed to the source authors.
- **AO4:** the spoken register runs the full length of the piece, including the middle, so it is not an essay with a greeting bolted on; “we” and “you” are used throughout; no subheadings; the closing returns to the opening anecdote, which the report identifies as a feature of better answers.
- **AO5:** full punctuation range including semicolons, dashes, a colon and a short embedded quotation punctuated correctly; varied sentence lengths for speech rhythm; articles all present; consistent tense; correct apostrophes.
- **Not claimed:** no examiner mark, level or score; no authentic script exists in any accessible route for comparison.
