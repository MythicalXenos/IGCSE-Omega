## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed discursive answer

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 17) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 18–19), with method from this paper's own report (pp. 42–44). **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **Too much, compared with what?**
>
> The word doing all the work in this statement is “too”. Nobody is really arguing about whether animals deserve care; they are arguing about a quantity, and a quantity only means something against a scale. So the honest answer is: too much compared with what, and decided by whom?
>
> Begin with the case for the statement, because it is not frivolous. There are people in this country choosing between heating and eating, and there are dogs in coats that cost more than a week's shopping. There are also animals kept in conditions that no amount of spending can justify, which suggests the money is not really about the animal at all — it is about the owner's feelings, worn on the outside. A society that can fund a designer harness but not a food bank has its proportions wrong, and it is fair to say so.
>
> But the argument has a weakness at its centre: it treats spending on animals and spending on people as one budget. They are not. The person who volunteers at a shelter on Saturday is not thereby unavailable to their grandmother on Sunday, and the money in a vet's till does not come out of a hospital's. Where the two genuinely compete — a household where a pet's insurance is paid before a child's shoes — that is a real problem, but it is a problem of one family's priorities, not a fact about a country.
>
> There is also an obligation that the statement quietly ignores. We use animals. They work for us, they are bred for us, they are eaten by us, and the least we owe them in return is decent care. Calling that care excessive is a strange position for the beneficiary of it to take. An animal cannot negotiate its own terms; the time and money we spend is the only terms it will ever get.
>
> What is genuinely excessive is narrower than the statement suggests: it is luxury masquerading as care — the toy the dog destroys in an afternoon, the outfit that exists for a photograph. That is waste, and it is worth naming, but it is a small slice of what people spend on animals, most of which goes on food, neutering and vet bills.
>
> So I disagree, to a moderate extent. Not because every penny spent on an animal is noble, but because “too much” is being applied to a category that mostly consists of ordinary, necessary expense. The sharper claim is not that we spend too much on animals. It is that we have become very good at spending on things we can photograph, and less good at spending on things we cannot.
>
