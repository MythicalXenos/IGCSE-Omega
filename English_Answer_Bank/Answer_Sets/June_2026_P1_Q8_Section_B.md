## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed school-website article

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content (printed pp. 16–17) and the verified band descriptors. **No report or script exists in any accessible route.** Not a Pearson script, not examiner-marked, **no score claimed.**

> **SIGN YOUR NAME LIKE YOU MEAN IT**
>
> *A defence of the pen, from someone whose own handwriting looks like a crime scene.*
>
> I want to start with a confession. Until about a year ago I could not read my own handwriting. Not "couldn't read it quickly" — couldn't read it. I found an old history essay last term and gave up after the first line. I had written it. It was about the Tudors. That was all I could establish.
>
> So this is not a lecture from someone with beautiful handwriting. It is an argument from someone who fixed theirs, badly and slowly, and who thinks you should too.
>
> **Why it matters (and no, this is not nostalgia)**
>
> Handwriting is the only schoolwork you produce that nobody else could have produced. A typed page is a typed page: same font, same margins, same corrected-by-machine sentences. Your handwriting, though, is a signature even when it is not signing anything. Teachers can tell your work from a hundred others across a desk. Your friends can tell a note from you at a glance, before they have read a word.
>
> There is harder evidence than sentiment. Writing by hand is slower than typing, and that is the point — it forces you to compress, choose, decide what actually matters. Note-taking by hand has been shown to make people process material more deeply than typing it, and there is a strong link between the physical act of forming letters and remembering what you formed. Ask anyone who has revised for an exam by copying out a quotation. They remember it. That is not magic; it is your hand teaching your brain.
>
> And it is yours. A page in your own hand carries effort you can see: the crossings-out, the insertions, the argument changing its mind halfway down. That is what thinking looks like before a machine tidies it up.
>
> **Why we've all quietly stopped**
>
> Here I have some sympathy, because the reasons are good ones.
>
> Typing is faster, and it is easier, and it lets you move a paragraph without rewriting a page. Spelling and grammar tools catch what you would otherwise lose marks for. When your homework is on a laptop, nobody can see that your "r" looks like a "v". Phones make it worse: voice notes are now the default way a lot of us communicate, and you cannot send a voice note in ink.
>
> Add to that the fact that almost nothing rewards handwriting any more. Primary school had a pen licence and a wall chart. Secondary school has a deadline. Nobody comments on your lettering unless it is bad enough to cost you a mark in an exam — and by then the habit is five years old and set like concrete.
>
> **The bit that actually costs you**
>
> Bad handwriting is not a personality quirk. It is a tax you pay on everything you write.
>
> It slows your reader down, and readers who are slowed down stop being generous. An examiner with three hundred scripts and forty minutes does not study your page — they guess. Guess wrongly and you lose a mark you had earned. That is not unfair. It is human.
>
> It also says things about you that you did not intend. A page of illegible scrawl reads as rushed, and rushed reads as careless, and careless reads as not bothered — even when you stayed up until midnight. People do judge it. Some of them will assume laziness, or worse, that you were never taught. Neither is true, and both are avoidable.
>
> And there is a nastier, slower cost. If you cannot read your own notes, you will not revise from them. If you cannot write quickly enough to keep up in an exam, you will lose content, not just presentation. The pen is not a moral issue; it is a piece of equipment you will need in a silent room in May, with no autocorrect and no backspace.
>
> **What to do about it (fifteen minutes, not a personality change)**
>
> One: write one thing by hand every day, and make it small. A shopping list. Three sentences of a diary. The date. Volume beats intensity — fifteen minutes daily will change your hand in a month, and one heroic hour a week will not.
>
> Two: slow down, and stop squeezing. Most bad handwriting is not a lack of talent; it is a lack of space and time. Use lined paper with room in it, and let the letters sit on the line instead of fighting it.
>
> Three: fix your grip. If the pen is a grappling hook and your knuckles are white after a paragraph, your hand is doing work your arm should do. Loosen it. Your future self, sitting an exam for two hours, will thank you.
>
> Four: write to somebody. A note to a grandparent, a card, a letter. Nothing improves handwriting faster than knowing a real person has to read it, and nothing in a school week gets kept for twenty years except the things people wrote by hand.
>
> **One last thing**
>
> You will not be marked on your handwriting for the rest of your life. You will be judged on it, though, in small ways, for a long time: on a form, in a job application, on a card that someone keeps in a box for thirty years after you have forgotten writing it.
>
> So sign your name like you mean it. Start tonight. It is fifteen minutes, and it is the cheapest upgrade available to you.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Importance:** MS offers twelve separate points, and a candidate may use only a few well. Strong alternatives include handwriting as a record of identity, as a memory aid, as a courtesy to the reader, or as the last un-automatable part of schoolwork.
- **Why not:** the MS's seven reasons are all usable in one paragraph, and a candidate may add the loss of a pen-licence culture, the ubiquity of phones, accessibility needs, or the fact that exams are now the only handwritten task most students face.
- **Problems:** MS allows both the reader's experience (hard to read, untidy, bad impression) and the writer's (seems lazy, seems uneducated, seems not to care). A candidate may argue the opposite — that judging handwriting is itself unfair — provided the bullet is still addressed.
- **Unused Text One material:** loving the sound of the nib and the smell of ink; the 2012 Toshiba laptop with the dead battery; the meeting with no electricity; the colleague's “did you go to school?”; the Darwin-and-snakes joke about evolution; the closing admission that the writer is typing the story on his phone.
- **Unused Text Two material:** the mother's letter from Vietnam in looping black ink, kept in a box; recognising a distinctive letterform and failing to forge a signature; the year 6 boy whose writing looked typeset; the worry about how a keyboard generation will archive its life; the belief that handwriting is “a tool to represent personality”.
- **Register:** a school-website article may be funny, blunt, self-mocking or campaigning. What it must not be is a formal essay or an assembly speech.
