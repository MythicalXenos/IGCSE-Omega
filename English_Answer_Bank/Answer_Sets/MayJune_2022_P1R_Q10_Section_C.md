## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed story

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 19) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 21–22), with method from this paper's own report. **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **The menu**
>
> My grandfather always ordered the same thing in the same restaurant, and I had never once found that strange.
>
> He had his table, his chair, his waiter, his dish. Chicken biryani, no salad, extra raita. He ordered it in the same words every Friday and the waiter, Rafiq, had long ago stopped writing it down. My father said it was because he was seventy-eight and men of seventy-eight were allowed to be fixed in their habits; my mother said it was because he liked being known. Both of them were wrong, and neither of them was in the restaurant that evening.
>
> The reason my parents were not there was that Rafiq had left to work in another city and there was a new waiter, a boy of about nineteen, who came to the table with two menus and put one in front of my grandfather and one in front of me. I remember the noise of the room — the fan, the chairs, someone laughing near the door — and I remember my grandfather's hands on the menu, flat, the way you put your hands on a table when you are waiting for something to be over.
>
> He said, *You choose for me.*
>
> I said, *You always have the biryani.*
>
> He said, *Then you order it.*
>
> And then, because the boy was standing there with his pad and his pen and his bright, patient face, my grandfather did something I had watched him do a hundred times without seeing it: he picked up the menu, held it at the distance at which a person holds a newspaper, and looked at it as though it were a letter from a country he had never visited.
>
> He cannot read it. He has never been able to read it. Not the menu, not the letters my mother leaves on the hall table, not the instructions on his own medicine. He has run a business, raised four children, and memorised every road between his house and the mosque, and he has done all of it by listening, by asking, and by keeping his hands flat on the table. The biryani was not a habit. It was the one dish he could order without reading a word.
>
> I ordered for both of us. I did not look at him while I did it and he did not look at me, and we ate, and he told me a story about a goat, and when we got up to leave he put his hand on my shoulder for a second longer than usual, which is his entire vocabulary for *thank you* and *say nothing*.
>
> That was the evening I found out what my grandfather had been hiding in plain sight for fifty years.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Interpretations the report lists:** lost treasure or artefacts; rescuing an animal or a person; a ghost; being adopted; an illness; self-discovery; discovering how to live better; discovering the truth. Abstract readings are explicitly present in this paper's report, so “self-discovery” is a legitimate choice rather than a soft one.
- **Structures:** one evening, one room (as here); a discovery made by a child about an adult; a discovery the narrator makes about themselves; a discovery that reframes a single earlier sentence.
- **Reveal placement:** last line (MS “NB”), provided the preparation is visible on a second reading.
- **What to avoid:** treasure-map plots; a long cast; a timescale of years; “it was all a dream”; gore; a reveal attached to a story it has nothing to do with; a plot recognisable from a film.
