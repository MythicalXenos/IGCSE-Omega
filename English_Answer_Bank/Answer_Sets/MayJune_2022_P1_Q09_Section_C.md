> **Changed, yes — but mostly in the margins**
>
> The statement sounds unarguable, which is exactly why it is worth arguing with. Of course English has changed because of the internet. The interesting question is the adverb: *greatly*. My grandmother, who has never sent a text message in her life, and my eleven-year-old cousin, who has never written a letter, speak the same language, and I do not think either of them would struggle for long in the other's world.
>
> Start with what has genuinely changed, because pretending otherwise is dishonest. English has always been restless; it took its grammar from one invasion, its vocabulary from three more, and its spelling from a printing press that arrived before the dictionary did. The internet has simply accelerated a process that was already running. What is new is the speed of coinage: a word can be invented on a Tuesday and be understood by a million people by Friday. Emojis are new. So is the fact that a full stop at the end of a one-word reply now reads as coldness — a piece of punctuation doing grammatical work in 1900 and emotional work in 2022. That is a real change, and it is fascinating.
>
> But has the language changed *greatly* in its bones? Consider what the internet has not touched. We still mark plurality with an *s*. We still form the past tense with *-ed*, and still break the rule for the verbs we use most. We still put the subject before the verb, and still raise our pitch at the end of a question. A teenager writing “bare jokes” in a group chat and a barrister addressing a court are using the same syntax; only the wardrobe is different.
>
> And this is where the statement overreaches. Text language is not a replacement for Standard English; it is a second register. Nobody writes “ROFL” in a job application, not because they cannot, but because they know better — and knowing better is the skill. The danger is not that the language is being damaged; the danger is that a student who only ever writes in one register will sound equally wrong in a classroom and in a group chat.
>
> So I agree with half of the statement and resist the rest. English has changed because of the internet, in its vocabulary, its tone and its punctuation. But it has changed the way a coastline changes: new shapes at the edge, the same bedrock underneath. The language has not been broken. It has been given more rooms to live in.
