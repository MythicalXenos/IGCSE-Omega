**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 8) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 20–21), with method informed by the June 2025 report. **No report or script exists for this paper.** Not a Pearson script, not examiner-marked, **no score claimed**.

> **The view from my window**
>
> From this window I can see two-thirds of a car park, one tree, and a length of wall that somebody has painted the colour of a duck egg. That is the whole of it. There is no sea, no mountain, no cathedral. It is the view of a person who lives on the second floor of a building that was put up quickly in 1974 and has been apologising ever since.
>
> At six in the morning it belongs to the birds and the bin lorries. The car park is a flat grey field with white lines on it, and the lines glow faintly in the half-light the way lines do when the tarmac is wet. The tree — a sycamore, I think, though I have never been certain — holds about forty pigeons and makes a noise like a radio tuned between stations. Somebody's kettle goes on in the flat below mine and the sound comes up through the floor as a small polite cough.
>
> By nine the cars have arrived and the day has a texture. Engines, doors, the particular slam of a boot being shut with a shoulder. Opposite, a woman in a blue coat reverses into the same space every morning with the confidence of a person who has done it four thousand times. The wall changes colour through the day: duck egg at ten, milky at noon, and by four o'clock a kind of pale butter where the sun gets over the roof.
>
> Afternoon is when the car park is at its most interesting, because afternoon is when people sit in their cars and do not get out. A man eating a sandwich and reading something on his phone. A woman with the engine off and the window down, doing nothing at all for eleven minutes. I have never spoken to either of them. I know their habits better than I know my own.
>
> And then the light goes, and the sodium lamps come on one by one with a hum you feel rather than hear, and the tarmac turns the colour of weak tea, and the whole rectangle becomes a photograph of itself.
>
> I did not choose this view. It was simply the one attached to the flat I could afford, and for the first month I thought it was nothing: a car park, a tree, a wall. But a fixed window does something odd to you. You begin to notice the small variations — which space fills first, which pigeon is lame, the evening the sycamore came into leaf — and the noticing becomes a kind of company. There is more here than I would have said, and I have looked at it every day for four years.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Views:** real or imaginary (MS): a hospital window, a train window, a classroom, a kitchen overlooking a yard, a hotel in another country, a view of nothing but a wall. A candidate may describe a view they dislike — the MS does not require the view to be attractive, only that its “effects” are evoked.
- **Structure:** across a day (as here); across the seasons; a single frozen moment; a view described once and then revisited after a change (a building demolished, a tree cut down).
- **Balance:** MS asks for the view **and** “the effects it had on them”. Omitting the second half caps AO4; overloading it turns description into reflection.
- **What to avoid:** narrating a story that happens outside the window; listing features; stacking evaluative adjectives; describing several views.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO4:** a sustained descriptive mode, a controlled first-person voice, a single frame and a clear emotional arc traced through light rather than event; the effects on the observer are shown as consequences of specific details.
- **AO5:** wide punctuation range used for effect, deliberate sentence rhythm (including one-sentence paragraphs), precise concrete vocabulary, and no inflated adjectives.
