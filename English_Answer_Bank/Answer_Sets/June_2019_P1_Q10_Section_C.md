## What this paper's own report says about Q10

- **Interpretations:** “winning a lottery, personal goals, sporting events, winning competitions and passing exams”; some “quests, journeys and challenges”; some “morality based topics with the prize being realising they were lucky, loved or safe”.
- **The trap the report names first:** “Some examiners commented that a few candidates **confused ‘The Prize’ with ‘The Price’**.”
- **The gore warning:** kidnap, murder, ransom and “gruesome tales of death, ghosts and gun-running” — “These unpleasant plots sometimes struggled to maintain focus on the title.”
- **Openings:** “Some examiners commented on **weak openings and endings**”; “a number of responses that started in a very pedestrian way, e.g. ‘**I got up and brushed my teeth**’.”
- **The late reveal, assessed properly:** “Often candidates chose to reveal the prize at the end … whilst in the majority of cases this appeared to be a deliberate strategy, occasionally it appeared that they had put this in at the end, **perhaps having lost focus** and realising that they needed to make a connection to the title”. And: “narratives often **started well, but then lost momentum**.”
- **What the strongest did:** “**More engaging responses often opened with the candidate in the present, reflecting on the ‘prize’ and its significance** in their lives”; “Successful candidates **avoided the obvious interpretations** of the title and chose a prize which was, sometimes, abstract, e.g. self-esteem, recovered confidence, a personal goal such as **reconciling with an estranged friend or relative**”; “Many were thought provoking and had twists or cliff-hangers for effect.”
- **AO5:** “Often, these candidates successfully included **some dialogue** in their responses and they used vocabulary, punctuation and sentence structure to create a sense of place and/or character.” Weaker candidates misused “more varied and selective vocabulary … **misunderstanding the meaning of words**”.
- **Less able:** “showed evidence of **prepared essays** with little adaptation and also used films, television programmes, computer games and books for plot lines.”

**Consequences:** the model opens **in the present on the prize itself**, inverts the obvious sporting script so the cup is *lost*, keeps only a small amount of dialogue, and provides a twist that is prepared rather than bolted on. No gore, no borrowed plot, no “I got up”.

## Craft decisions taken from the evidence

| Decision | Reason |
|---|---|
| Opens on the prize, in the present tense, and states that the prize was *not* the cup | “opened with the candidate in the present, reflecting on the ‘prize’” |
| An abstract prize — recognition, not a trophy | “chose a prize which was, sometimes, abstract” |
| The narrator **does not win** the race | Avoids the obvious competition plot the report says stronger candidates avoided |
| Two short exchanges of dialogue | “successfully included some dialogue” |
| Deliberate circular structure, not a late afterthought reveal | “perhaps having lost focus” is the failure mode the report separates from a planned reveal |
| Nothing gruesome, nothing borrowed | Report names both as focus-destroying |

## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed story

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 16) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 17–18), with method from this paper's own report and from the 30/30 examiner notes (printed p. 43). **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **The Prize**
>
> The prize was not the cup. The cup went home with a girl called Fen, who swam a better race than I did and deserved it, and I have no argument with that. The prize was four words, said by a woman who no longer keeps anything, on a Tuesday in March.
>
> My grandmother taught me to swim. Not in the way that mothers teach children to swim, by holding them up in a hotel pool and lying about how deep it is, but properly, at six in the morning, in a leisure centre that smelled of chlorine and floor cleaner and the radiators they had turned on too high. She swam the county backstroke in 1961. There is a small cup on her windowsill that says so, and when I was eight I thought it was the most valuable object in the world.
>
> She moved into Rowan Court in October. She is not unhappy there, which is the best that can be said, and she calls me by my mother's name about half the time.
>
> So when the gala came round, I entered, and I trained for it with a seriousness that surprised my coach. I want to be honest about why. It was not for the cup. It was because I had worked out that if I won something she had won, there might be a conversation in it — one conversation, twenty seconds long, in which she knew who I was.
>
> The race was four lengths. I went out too fast, which she had told me a hundred times not to do, and by the third length my arms had gone heavy and the water had stopped being something I could push against and become something I was only inside of. Fen came past me on the last turn. I watched her feet, and the bubbles coming off them, and the black line sliding away under me, and I finished third.
>
> Afterwards, in the changing room, I sat on the bench for a while with my towel over my head and waited to feel the thing I had expected to feel. It did not arrive. What arrived instead was a sort of clarity, the way you see a room properly for a second when the lights go out.
>
> On the Tuesday I went to Rowan Court with my third-place medal in my pocket, which I had decided in advance was pathetic and had brought anyway. She was in the chair by the window with a blanket over her knees, watching the car park. I sat down. She did not look at me.
>
> Then she said, without turning her head, “You turned too early.”
>
> I had not told her about the race. My mother had not told her either; I checked afterwards. It is possible that she was talking to the car park, or to 1961, or to nobody. I have decided not to spend any more time on that question, because it is not the question that matters.
>
> The prize was four words from a woman who has lost almost everything, and I have them still.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Prize types (report's list):** lottery; personal goal; sporting event; competition; passing exams; quest or journey; a moral prize (“realising they were lucky, loved or safe”); an abstract prize such as self-esteem, recovered confidence, or “reconciling with an estranged friend or relative”.
- **Structures:** opening in the present on the prize and flashing back (as here); a linear quest; a story where the prize is won and turns out to be wrong; a story where the prize is refused.
- **Late reveal:** permitted by the MS, and credited when engineered — the report distinguishes a planned reveal from “having lost focus”.
- **What to avoid:** confusing “prize” with “price”; an “I got up and brushed my teeth” opening; plot from a film, game or prepared essay; kidnap/murder/ransom gore; a weak ending unconnected to the events; a lengthy fight or chase that abandons the title; thesaurus vocabulary misused.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO4:** the title is interpreted obliquely (a prize that is lost, then received in another currency) and the form is held from the first sentence to the last, closing on a deliberate return rather than an appended explanation.
- **AO5:** the opening sentence is short and declarative; sentence lengths then vary deliberately, with a long three-clause sentence for the third length and clipped ones in the changing room; dialogue is two lines, both doing work; vocabulary is plain but exact (“the water had stopped being something I could push against”).
