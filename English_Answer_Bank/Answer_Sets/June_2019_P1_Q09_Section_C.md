## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed discursion

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 16) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 17–18), with method from this paper's own report (printed pp. 33–36). **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **Hard work is necessary. It is not enough.**
>
> The statement in front of me is not that hard work helps, which nobody would argue with, but that you *have to* work hard to get what you want. That word is the whole question. If hard work is a necessary condition, the statement is true. If it is a sufficient one, it is obviously false, and I do not think any honest person who has watched a hard-working parent go nowhere believes it.
>
> Start with what the statement gets right, because it gets a great deal right. Effort is the only part of success a person actually controls. Talent is handed out unevenly and cannot be earned; opportunity depends on who your parents know, which school you were sent to and which country you were born in. Work is the one variable you can move yourself. It is also the variable that compounds: six weeks of revision beats six days, and the difference is not mysterious. And there is a moral point underneath the practical one. Working hard at something is how a person shows that it matters to them, and a goal nobody has worked for is usually a goal nobody has thought about.
>
> Now the part the statement leaves out. Consider two children born on the same street, one of whom inherits a business and one of whom inherits a debt. Both can work hard; only one of them is playing a game where hard work converts into what they want. Nobody seriously claims the first child out-worked the second. The same holds for plain bad luck — the illness, the accident, the year an industry closes. The report on this paper found that the most common objection candidates raised was inherited wealth, and it is the right objection, because it is not an argument about effort at all. It is an argument about the exchange rate between effort and reward, and that exchange rate is set by things no individual controls.
>
> There is a second difficulty, which is that hard work without direction can be worse than none. I have watched people work fourteen-hour days on a plan that could not have succeeded, and their effort did not rescue the plan; it only made the failure more expensive. What the statement calls for is not simply labour but judgement about where to spend it, and judgement is not something you can grind out by volume.
>
> So to what extent do I agree? Largely, but not completely, and the distinction matters. Hard work is close to a necessary condition: almost nothing worth having arrives without it, and a person who does not work will usually fail even with every advantage. It is nowhere near a sufficient one. I would rewrite the statement this way: you have to work hard to have a serious chance of getting what you want, and you also have to be lucky, and you have to be pointed in the right direction, and even then the answer is not guaranteed. That is a weaker claim than the one I was given. It also happens to be true.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Stances:** agree strongly with the absolute (and then argue that effort *is* the only fair currency); disagree strongly on the luck/inheritance ground; the qualified middle taken here.
- **The report's counter-argument:** inherited wealth — worth a full paragraph wherever the answer agrees overall.
- **Institutional angles:** schooling and tutoring; the country or economy you are born into; luck in health; the difference between *wanting* and *deserving*.
- **What to avoid:** telling the story of a hard-working relative as the body of the answer; “some people are lucky, but…” as a single sentence of throat-clearing before twenty lines of agreement; a conclusion that lists both sides again instead of deciding; slogans about “hustle”; the word *nefarious* or its relatives.
