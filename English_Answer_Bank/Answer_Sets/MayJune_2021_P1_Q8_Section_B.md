## Design decisions in the absence of a report

With no examiner commentary to lean on, the choices below are taken directly from the three MS grids and from the prompts of the two texts:

1. **The bullets are unequal in difficulty and the model treats them accordingly.** Positive aspects and problems are *descriptive*; the advice bullet requires *judgement* (“what to do and not to do”). The model gives advice the last and longest section, because that is where AO1 “well-focused comments” and AO4 “task sharply focused on purpose” are easiest to demonstrate — a talk that ends on description has usually spent its persuasion budget on scenery.
2. **It is a talk, not an essay.** Direct address, a rhetorical question, signposting sentences, one deliberate self-deprecating anecdote, and a closing appeal. The 2020 talk-form set established this register; here it must also be *entertaining*, because the subject is leisure travel.
3. **Both texts are used, and used differently.** Text One supplies the cautionary tale (it is a comic account of a badly planned journey); Text Two supplies the advice framework (nine headed pieces of guidance). A strong answer turns Text One into the *evidence* for Text Two’s rules.
4. **Nothing is invented as fact.** Every specific claim about the two texts is verifiable in the booklet; the one first-person recollection is a generic, plausible experience of the kind a 16-year-old might offer, not a fabricated report of the source writers’ lives.

## [CONSTRUCTED GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed talk

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content and grids, using verified details from both texts. It is **not** an examiner-marked script, **not** a Pearson publication, and **no score is claimed**.

> Good morning, everyone — and yes, I know it is Monday, and no, this is not going to be a talk about revision.
>
> It is a talk about travelling, which is a subject this year group has strong opinions about and, in most cases, no experience of. So let me start with the honest version. Travelling is the best thing I have ever spent money on, and every trip I have taken has gone wrong at least once. Both of those statements are true, and I want to convince you that the second one does not cancel out the first.
>
> **Why it is worth it**
>
> Ask anyone who has been somewhere and they will not describe the hotel. They describe the moment the place stopped being a photograph and became a place. One writer I read describes arriving in a Bolivian town after a terrifying flight and feeling, simply, relieved: “A dump yes, but dismal no.” Notice what he is doing there — he has spent three pages complaining and his conclusion is still that he was glad he went.
>
> And the pleasures are not all scenic. There are helpful strangers, sometimes the very people you could not talk to. There is the contrast that hits you when you come out of somewhere wild and the ordinary world is suddenly neon-bright and loud. There are the other travellers, who will tell you where to eat, which bus to avoid, and how to get your money back. There is the specific pride of managing something you were nervous about. That is the real return on a trip: not photographs, but the discovery that you can operate in a place where nothing works the way it does at home.
>
> **What will go wrong**
>
> Now the honest half. You will probably get ill. Altitude sickness, if you go high enough, feels like being drunk with none of the benefits — headache, nausea, disorientation. You will be rushed, because you booked too little time and now three countries have to fit into six days. You will be tired at airports, and airports at four in the morning all look the same and none of them sell anything you want. You may find yourself on a plane older than your parents, over mountains that have a reputation. You will not understand what anyone is saying, and the one person who does understand will not be the person you asked.
>
> And there is the small stuff, which is somehow worse. No torch, and the hotel corridor is impenetrably dark. Too much luggage, so every staircase becomes a negotiation. An out-of-date guidebook sending you to a restaurant that closed three years ago. And, if you are travelling with people you met on the way, the particular misery of not knowing whether your message was received or whether you have simply been left behind.
>
> **What to do about it**
>
> So here is my advice, and it is short enough to remember.
>
> Give yourself time. Plan, but plan loosely — one or two things a day, then let the rest happen, because the best parts of any trip are the parts nobody scheduled. That is the single most useful thing I can tell you: do not over-plan.
>
> Learn six words of the language before you go. Please do not treat a guidebook as a bible; it is a map of last year. Talk to people instead — other travellers are an information network, and they will look after you if you let them. Buy a cheap local phone so you can actually find the friends you made. Take more money than you think you need, because something always breaks. Pack less than you think you need, because you will carry it every day. Take the risk — jump off the boat, do the thing that scares you slightly — and then be sensible about the genuinely dangerous bits. Those are two different instructions and both matter.
>
> **One last thing**
>
> Travelling does not require bravery. It requires a little preparation and a willingness to be a beginner in public. Every person who has done it started exactly where you are now, nervous and badly packed. Go anyway.

*Word count: 697 words including the four subheadings.*

## Accepted alternatives recorded

The MS list is explicitly non-exhaustive.

- **Positive aspects:** Text One’s comedy about Colonel Percy Fawcett and the “wonderfully romantic place names like Zanzibar or Timbuktu” (p. 2, lines 8–11) is unused here and is a legitimate, highly usable positive: romance of place names, the pleasure of a story worth telling afterwards.
- **Problems:** “creepy-crawlies” and “being nervous” are MS points this talk only touches (the rainforest being “too ‘in your face’, in your hair, in your clothes and under your skin”, p. 3, lines 41–42, is the obvious source for the first); a candidate can develop either at length.
- **Advice:** “enjoy the culture” and “take advantage of the support offered by fellow travellers” are both used lightly and could carry a whole section; a candidate who builds the talk around one central rule (slow travel, or pack light) with the other bullets subordinated is fully within the scheme.
- **Register:** a talk to peers may be humorous, serious, or deliberately self-deprecating. Any of these is acceptable provided the audience is addressed consistently as peers and the three bullets are all covered.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO1:** all three bullets are covered with developed, textually grounded points, from *both* texts, with the source detail reworded rather than lifted wholesale; the advice section is prioritised, which is where the discriminating, “well-focused comments” live.
- **AO4:** the talk keeps a single recognisable voice — wry, direct, occasionally self-mocking — from greeting to closing appeal; it addresses an audience of peers explicitly and stays plainly spoken rather than written. The four subheadings are part of the delivered talk (they are spoken aloud as signposts and are short enough to be heard), not essay scaffolding.
- **AO5:** varied paragraph lengths matched to speech rhythm; a wide punctuation range including dashes, semicolons and a correctly punctuated short quotation; no comma splicing, no missing articles, consistent tense.
