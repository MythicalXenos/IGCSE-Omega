## [CONSTRUCTED GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed magazine article

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content and the November 2023 report guidance, using verified details from both texts. **Not** a Pearson script, **not** examiner-marked, **no score claimed.**

> **Time to learn something new?**
>
> *Six months from now you could be able to do something you cannot do today. Here is the honest version of how that happens — including the parts nobody puts on a poster.*
>
> There is a particular kind of evening that I suspect most of us recognise. Homework is done, or convincingly pretending to be done. Your phone is warm from overuse. You have watched a person on the internet make pasta from scratch in ninety seconds and you are thinking, I could do that. And then you think, I have no time, I would be terrible at it, and anyway where would I even start? This article is about that moment.
>
> **What could you learn?**
>
> Almost anything, which is part of the problem. The obvious answers are languages, cooking, knitting, surfing, rock climbing, swimming. The better answers are the ones that make your friends look up: chess, driving, scuba diving, parkour, martial arts, an instrument you have never held, painting badly on purpose, learning to fly. The point is not to choose something impressive. It is to choose something you are actually curious about, because a skill attached to an interest stops feeling like homework. If you love food, learn to bake. If you love being outside, learn to climb.
>
> **How to do it**
>
> Pick one thing. Not three. Trying to learn two languages at once leaves you tired and confused, and it is the same with anything else — the more you spread your attention, the less of it each thing gets.
>
> Then break it down. Nobody learns to surf by standing up in the sea. You start on the sand, learning where your feet go, and only then take it into the water. Whatever you choose, find the sub-skills and learn them in order; it turns a terrifying whole into a series of achievable bits.
>
> Practise more than you read. Research is comfortable and practising is uncomfortable, which is why we all do too much of the first. Alternate them. Then find the boring hour in your day — the scrolling, the fourth episode in a row — and spend it on the skill instead. Even one hour a night, used properly, gets you somewhere real.
>
> And take lessons from someone who can already do it. Not everything can be self-taught, and asking for help is faster than guessing.
>
> **Why it is hard**
>
> Time, first of all, and the fact that most of us spend it without noticing. Commitment, which is a different problem: starting is easy and continuing is not. Choosing, which sounds trivial until you have spent three weeks deciding between three hobbies and started none of them.
>
> Then there is fear. Fear of looking ridiculous, fear of being the worst person in the room, and underneath both of those, fear of failing in front of other people. It is worth knowing that the fear is often the largest obstacle, and that it does not disappear before you begin — you begin and it shrinks.
>
> There are practical barriers too. Facilities cost money; lessons cost more; some places have no pool, no gym, no teacher within reach. And some of us carry a bad early experience — a class we hated, a teacher who laughed, a summer of being the slowest in the group — that quietly convinced us we are simply not the sort of person who can do this thing.
>
> That last one is worth arguing with. One writer describes learning to swim as an adult after decades of avoiding water entirely: the first lessons were panic, flailing and a lot of splashing, and then, midway through the third lesson, something clicked. He could swim. Not beautifully, not fast, but he could do the thing he had believed he could not.
>
> That is the part worth taking away. Learning something new as an adult or a teenager is not about talent. It is about choosing one thing, breaking it into pieces, putting in the boring hour, and being willing to be bad at it in public for a while. Six months is not very long. What would you like to be able to do at the end of it?

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Different title treatment:** the title may be set as a headline with or without a standfirst; what matters is that it appears and that the piece reads as a magazine article for young adults rather than an essay.
- **First bullet alternatives:** the report's own list (driving, chess, scuba diving, flying a plane, martial arts, parkour, dancing, playing an instrument, snowboarding, painting) is explicitly praised; so are personal hobbies not in either text.
- **Third bullet alternatives:** lack of time and fear of failure are the two most obvious routes; a candidate may instead build the whole article around one barrier (money, facilities, or a bad past experience) and treat the others briefly, provided all three bullets are addressed.
- **Text Two material unused here:** the statistics (about one in five Americans cannot swim; 28% of non-swimming city-dwellers say they are not interested) and the “coping mechanisms” argument (that avoidance is more effort than learning) would support a more analytical article.
- **Register:** humorous, encouraging, confessional or mildly provocative all fit the report's description (“humorous, informal and friendly”); what it penalises is losing the article form or the young-adult audience.
