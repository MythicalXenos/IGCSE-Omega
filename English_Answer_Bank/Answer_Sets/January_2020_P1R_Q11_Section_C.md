## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed description

> **The net-mender**
>
> The most memorable person I have known is a woman called Halima who mends fishing nets on the concrete apron behind the harbour, and I have never learned her second name.
>
> She is small and square, and she sits on an upturned crate with her legs folded under her in a way that suggests she has been sitting like that for fifty years. Her hands are the part of her you remember. They are broad across the knuckle and pale at the palm, and the skin over the back of them has gone thin and shiny, and the right thumb has a flattened nail from a hammer in about 1979 that she will tell you about if you ask and will not tell you about if you do not. There is a permanent groove across the side of her right index finger where the twine sits.
>
> Her work has a sound: a wooden needle, about the length of a ruler, pushed through the mesh and pulled back, and a small click as the knot tightens. Click, pull, click, pull, about once a second, for six hours. When she is thinking she stops, and the stopping is louder than the clicking.
>
> She wears the same thing in every weather — a green overall with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, and a scarf tied at the back of her neck rather than under her chin, which I have always thought of as the mark of somebody who intends to work rather than to be seen. She keeps her twine in a tin that once held biscuits, sorted by thickness, and she will not let anyone else reach into it.
>
> She talks to everybody and remembers everything. She knows which boat is late, whose son has gone to Italy, which of the men is drinking again; she keeps the harbour in her head the way other people keep a diary. She once told me, in the middle of a knot, *A net is not for catching fish. It is for letting the water through* — which I did not understand at fourteen and have thought about most weeks since.
>
> What she changed in me is smaller and more useful than admiration. I was a boy who started things and left them, and I spent one summer sitting on the concrete beside her with a practice net and a blunt needle, being corrected in silence until the knot was right. She never praised me once. She simply handed the net back until it was correct, and in doing that she taught me the difference between trying something and finishing it, which no teacher had managed in nine years of school reports.
