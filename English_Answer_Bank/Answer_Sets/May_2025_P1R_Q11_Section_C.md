## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed description

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 20), the AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 21–22) and this paper's own report (pp. 58–60). **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **Ines, who cannot sit still**
>
> Ines is small, and always slightly damp: her hair at the temples after games, her palms before an exam, the back of her neck in July. She has a laugh that arrives a half-second before the joke lands, which is how you know she has already decided to be amused. She bites the skin at the side of her thumbnail when she is reading something difficult, and she reads difficult things on purpose.
>
> She is the least restful person I have ever met, and the most calming to be near. Both of those are true and I do not know how she manages it. In a waiting room she will have reorganised the magazines; on a bus she will be talking to a stranger about the stranger's dog by the third stop, and the stranger will be enjoying it.
>
> Her school bag is a small ongoing disaster: a geometry set with no compass, three half-pencils, a tennis ball, something that used to be a banana. She can find a plaster in there within four seconds. She has produced a plaster for me in the following places: a coach, a hospital corridor, and once, memorably, the middle of a piano exam.
>
> What makes her my best friend is harder to photograph. It is that she asks the second question. Anyone can ask the first one — how was it, are you okay — and she does ask those, but she waits, and then she asks the thing underneath, and she does not look away while you answer. When my parents separated, she did not say anything wise. She sat on the floor with her back against my bed and threw the tennis ball at the ceiling for forty minutes, and at some point I started talking, and she kept throwing.
>
> She is also the only person who tells me the truth before I have asked for it. Once, in the corridor outside the art room, she said, “You're doing that thing where you're funny at people instead of talking to them.” It was not kind. It was accurate, which she would say is better, and which I have come to think might be the same thing.
>
> People like Ines are easy to describe for a sentence and difficult to describe for a page, because the qualities are not in the surface. She is untidy, quick, nosy, generous with the last of anything. But what her friendship has actually done is quieter: it has made me someone who expects to be listened to, and that expectation follows me into rooms she has never entered.
>

*Word count: 430 words of body text (435 including the title line).*

## Craft map — how the description meets the descriptors

| Element | Where | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| Sensory imagery in the first paragraph | “slightly damp”, “hair at the temples”, “laugh that arrives a half-second before the joke” | Report: successful candidates “employed sensory imagery to create rich descriptions” |
| **No** “how we met” | Absent by design | The report's three-time complaint: meeting stories turn the piece into narrative |
| Qualities illustrated by moments, not labels | The plaster list; the tennis ball; the art-room sentence | Report: “well-chosen anecdotes used to illustrate the friend's qualities”; “listed traits without elaborating” is the named failure |
| All three MS elements present | Person ¶1–¶3; qualities ¶4–¶5; “what it meant” ¶6 | MS explicitly lists description of the person, qualities, and positive effects/what it meant |
| Specificity that invites the reader in | Named places (“a coach, a hospital corridor… a piano exam”), four-second plaster | Report: “specific and vivid… left the reader desiring to be friends with the person who was described” |
| Description stays dominant | Nothing is resolved; no plot; the anecdotes are single moments | Examiner note on the graded script: “maintains the focus on description throughout” |
| Voice in the friend's own words | ¶5 “You're doing that thing where you're funny at people…” | Dialogue used as characterisation, not as scene-building |
| Vocabulary precise, never inflated | “a small ongoing disaster”, “the thing underneath”, “funny at people” | The top band rewards “powers to evoke… using effective vocabulary”, not ornament |

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **People (MS):** “real (alive or dead) or imaginary” — a grandparent, a sibling, a cousin, a classmate, a teacher, a friend who moved away, an imaginary companion, or “pets described metaphorically” (the report notes this was done).
- **Structure:** qualities as headings in miniature (as here); a day with the friend, all description and no plot; the friend seen through three objects; a before/after of what the friendship changed.
- **How much anecdote:** the report wants anecdotes used **to illustrate qualities**, so one short moment per quality is the safe ratio. Anecdotes that run for a paragraph each start to rebuild the narrative problem.
- **What to avoid:** a chronological “we met in Year 7” opening; a list of adjectives; writing about what makes a good friend in general rather than *this* friend; two friends compared; a bereavement or accident used for unearned drama.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO4:** sustained descriptive mode of a single person, with the three MS components distributed and no narrative drift; the friendship's effect shown as a durable change in the narrator.
- **AO5:** deliberate sentence-length variation, a colon-and-semicolon list structure, concrete nouns over adjectives, and one line of dialogue that does structural work.
