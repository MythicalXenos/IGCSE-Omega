> **Art for all, exams for some**
>
> There are two claims hiding inside this sentence and they are not equally strong. That children should make art at school: obviously, and I would defend it in any argument. That everyone should *study* it — sit an exam in it, be graded on it, have it taking up a slot that chemistry wanted: that is a different proposition, and it is the one this answer is really about.
>
> Begin with what nobody sensible disputes. Art is the one timetabled subject where the answer is not already known. A student who has spent the morning copying formulas can spend an hour discovering that a line can be soft or hard, that a colour changes when it sits next to another colour, that a drawing can fail and still be worth keeping. That is not a soft benefit; it is training in a kind of thinking the other subjects do not supply. The skill also travels — the student who can draw a diagram understands the design brief, the biology drawing, the geometry of a bridge — and I have never met an engineer who regretted being able to sketch.
>
> And yet “everyone” is where the argument wobbles. The student who cannot draw and is told, twice a week for five years, that they cannot draw, does not become creative; they become a person who believes they are not. I have watched this happen. The same hour could have gone to the subject they were good at, and there is no shame in saying so, because time in a school timetable is the least elastic resource we have. There are not many jobs that require art. That is not an argument for abolishing it, but it is an argument against making it compulsory for the student who will never take one.
>
> So what would I actually defend? Art as a guaranteed part of every week, taught well, taken seriously, with the mess and the materials properly funded. Then art as an examined option for those who want it, at the same level of prestige as any other option. What I would not defend is the pretence that compulsory and valuable are the same word.
>
> I agree, then, to a considerable extent — but with the sentence quietly repaired. It is important that everyone should *make* art at school. It is not important that everyone should be examined in it, and confusing the two is how a good subject ends up resented by the students who need it most.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Positions that score:** agree fully on creativity and wellbeing; agree with a distinction between provision and examination (as here); disagree by arguing the timetable is finite and art is a hobby; agree only for younger year groups.
- **MS material to use:** creativity; drawing helps in other subjects such as design and technology; many forms of art; relaxation from academic subjects; boredom for those who cannot draw; time better spent studying or exercising; few jobs require art.
- **Support that works:** the transferable skill of visual thinking; the demoralising effect of compulsory failure; funding and materials; the difference between an option and a requirement.
- **What to avoid:** a personal story of a favourite art teacher in place of an argument; borrowing the Section A extracts; “art is subjective so no one can mark it” as a whole essay; an unqualified “art is pointless”.
