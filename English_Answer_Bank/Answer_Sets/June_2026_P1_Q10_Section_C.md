## Cross-paper examiner guidance — June 2025 report, Q10 (quoted; different title, method only)

- “Candidates interpreted this question in a variety of ways… Examiners commented that there were **fewer unnecessarily gruesome and gory stories**. Occasionally candidates were **over-ambitious, producing extremely long responses with complicated plot-lines**.”
- On the title: “Often candidates chose to reveal the link to the title… at the end of their writing and, whilst in some cases this appeared to be a deliberate strategy, examiners commented that **sometimes it seemed to be an afterthought**.” The June 2026 MS makes the same allowance — “Explicit reference to the title may not be mentioned until the end” — so a late title reveal is permitted but must be **engineered**, not bolted on.
- “Examiners saw a number of responses that were identical (or nearly identical)… Candidates should not be encouraged to learn essays which they then regurgitate.”
- Typical competent response: “a basic storyline with an identifiable beginning, middle and end; introduce a central character or situation… maintain a consistent narrative voice… used some descriptive detail to engage the reader, often around setting or appearance and attempted a resolution or twist.”
- More successful: “entertaining and engaging responses that were sharply focused on the task… a clear narrative structure and a range of writing techniques used to good effect. These responses had **fewer characters which were well-developed and a well-thought-out plot**… able to use figurative techniques and wide-ranging vocabulary deliberately… a range of structural techniques… **flashbacks, varied pacing, and well-placed dialogue**.”
- Less successful: “muddled storylines and **weak endings that were not closely related to the events that had unfolded**… often lengthy with repetitive and unfocused plot ideas… plots from films, television series and computer games which were barely adapted.”

**Design consequences:** two characters only, one location, one flashback, dialogue used twice for pace, a twist that grows out of the objects already established, and a final line that names the box so the title lands as a consequence rather than an announcement.

## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed story

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 20) and its AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 21–22), with craft method informed by the June 2025 report commentary quoted above. **No report or script exists for this paper.** Not a Pearson script, not examiner-marked, **no score claimed**.

> **The Box**
>
> The shed had been locked for eleven weeks, and it still smelled of him: engine oil, cold tea, the peppermint he sucked to hide the cigarettes. My mother stood in the doorway with a bin bag and did not come in.
>
> “Top shelf,” she said. “Leave the green one.”
>
> It was not a big shed. It took me four minutes to clear the shelf — paint tins, a jar of screws sorted by size, a radio with the dial snapped off — and the whole time the green box sat at the end of it like something waiting to be introduced. Flat tin, painted over so many times the corners had gone soft, and a padlock small enough to be a joke.
>
> He had shown it to me once, when I was nine, holding it up the way he held everything: at arm's length, as if it might bite. *Nobody opens that box.* He said it without turning round, which was how he said everything that mattered.
>
> “There's no key,” my mother said. “He lost it years ago. It's probably empty.”
>
> I got the bolt cutters from the car. The metal gave with a sound like a cough.
>
> I had expected money, or medals, or the deed to something. What was inside was paper, folded twice, soft as cloth. Pencil, in his small square capitals. A list.
>
> *Mrs Okonjo — back gate, 4 hinges.*
>
> *Kaur family — the tap, then the other tap.*
>
> *The boy at 14 — bike chain, twice, don't charge.*
>
> Page after page of other people's broken things, dated, costed at nothing. Forty years of Saturdays, entered in pencil like a ledger of debts owed to him.
>
> Underneath the list was a bundle of drawings. Mine. Crayon dinosaurs, a house with the sun in the corner, a rocket with my name spelled wrong — every one of them on the back of an invoice, flattened out and kept dry, kept flat, kept.
>
> My mother sat down hard on an upturned crate and put her hand over her mouth. The shed was very quiet. Somewhere behind the fence a dog was barking at nothing at all.
>
> “He never said,” she said, finally.
>
> “He did,” I said. “He just said it in pencil.”
>
> We took the box home. It is on my shelf now, unlocked, with the drawings back inside it and the list on top, and every so often I take it down and read the names and think about a man who kept a record of everything he ever gave away — and never once wrote down what he kept.

*Word count: 435 words, close to the QP's “approximately 400”.*

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Interpretations of the title:** a literal box (keepsake, toolbox, evidence box, moving crate); a metaphorical one (a box room, a box on a form, “thinking inside the box”, a penalty box); a box that should not be opened; a box that is empty. The MS says explicitly that no indicative content can be specified and examiners should be open to a wide range of interpretation.
- **True or imaginary:** the QP permits a memoir-style narrative, so a candidate may write about a real family object. The report's warning about **identical or learned responses** applies with force here — a rehearsed story bent onto this title is exactly what examiners criticised.
- **Genre:** ghost story, crime, war, science fiction, school story and family drama are all viable. The report notes examiners were pleased to see **fewer gruesome stories**, so gore is neither required nor rewarded.
- **Length:** approximately 400 words. The report criticises “extremely long responses with complicated plot-lines”, so a tight single-scene story with one twist outperforms a sprawling one.
- **Late title reveal:** permitted by the MS, but the model shows the safer construction — plant early, return at the end — because the report says a late link “sometimes seemed to be an afterthought”.
