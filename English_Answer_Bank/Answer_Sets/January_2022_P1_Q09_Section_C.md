## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed discursion

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 16) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 17–18), with method from this paper's own report. **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **Travel, and the holiday that isn't**
>
> Nearly everyone says yes to this question, and nearly everyone means something different by it. So let me fix the terms first. Travel, for the purposes of this answer, is not the same thing as a holiday. A holiday is a period in which nothing is required of you; travel is a period in which a great deal is required of you, most of it inconvenient. And “broadens the mind” cannot mean “provides pleasant memories”, or the question answers itself and is not worth asking. It must mean a change in how a person sees the world.
>
> On that definition, the answer is yes — but conditionally, and the condition is what matters.
>
> Travel broadens the mind when it forces you to be wrong. Stand in a market where you cannot read a single sign and the ordinary confidence of an adult collapses; you become, for a few days, functionally illiterate, and you learn what that feels like from the inside. Eat what is put in front of you. Be the only person in the room who does not know the custom. None of this is comfortable, and all of it is educational, because it moves you from the centre of your own picture. The tolerance the mark scheme mentions is not a mood; it is the residue of having been the outsider.
>
> But most of what the travel industry sells does the opposite. A resort is a portable version of home with better weather: the food is familiar, the language is yours, the staff have been trained to pretend the culture is a decoration. You can fly eight hours and never once be wrong about anything. That is not travel broadening the mind; that is a holiday, and the mind returns exactly as it left, with a tan.
>
> The objection that impresses me most is not the cost, or the risk of illness, or the fact that films and books can teach you about a place. It is the environment. A return long-haul flight produces a quantity of carbon that no individual act of recycling will ever offset, in exchange for a personal education. It is difficult to argue that the world is improved by a trip that damages the world. I think the honest response is not to deny the cost but to price the benefit properly: travel less, stay longer, go overland where you can, and be honest that a weekend in another continent is consumption, not education.
>
> So: does travel broaden the mind? Yes — sometimes, for some people, under conditions most tourists never meet. It is one of the most efficient ways ever invented of learning that you were wrong, and it is also one of the easiest ways of avoiding that lesson entirely. The mind is not broadened by distance travelled. It is broadened by how much a person is willing to be altered on the way.
