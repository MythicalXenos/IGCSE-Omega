### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[CONSTRUCTED MS-BASED RESPONSE]** *Original response, based on Text One and the Results MS; not Pearson-scored. No mark is assigned.*

Doll begins with a compact, confident promise: “Looking on the bright side doesn’t always come naturally, but you can train your mind to seek it out”. The concession that positivity “doesn’t always come naturally” acknowledges the reader’s difficulty, while “you can train your mind” reframes it as a learnable skill. The claim that it “will do wonders for your health” immediately gives that effort a worthwhile reward. She anticipates resistance by quoting familiar instructions such as “Turn that frown upside down!” and “Smile, you’ll feel better!”, then undercuts their simplistic tone with the comic exaggeration “enough to make you want to punch a wall”. Her aside, “look, no one wants to be told to smile, ever”, sounds conversational and recognises the reader’s scepticism rather than scolding them. The reference to an “Instagram meme” also meets readers on familiar online territory, but the claim that positivity extends beyond a meme raises the argument above a slogan. *(Text One, lines 1–9.)*

After asking “So what is it, exactly?”, she gives a definition through Noulas’s phrase “open, optimistic viewpoint” and the familiar metaphor of “seeing the silver lining on a bad day”. Doll is careful to distinguish optimism from pretending that problems do not exist: it does not mean sweeping “your actual thoughts under the rug” to make room for “unicorns and rainbows”. Instead, she calls it “viewing situations from a more well-rounded perspective”. Her example of someone stressed by a workload turns that abstract definition into a sequence of practical steps. The tricolon “take a deep breath, consider what you’ve already accomplished, and tell yourself you’ll get the job done in the best way you can” moves from calming down, to recognising progress, to a realistic assurance. The advice is encouraging but not a demand to deny reality. *(Text One, lines 10–17.)*

The article then supports its claims with named experts and research. Noulas’s explanation that positive thinking can improve “your mood, physical health, energy level, concentration, productivity and more” uses a cumulative list to suggest wide-ranging benefits. Lines 21–24 add that positive people are “better at problem-solving and dealing with setbacks”, “more resilient”, and able to “open up creativity”, “connect better with others” and “boost your overall well-being”. The sequence broadens the case beyond simply feeling happier, while “sunnier light” keeps the tone hopeful. Naming Noulas and citing research gives these claims authority rather than presenting them as the writer’s unsupported opinion. *(Text One, lines 18–24.)*

The subheading “Build the Skill” introduces advice in a manageable section. Davidson’s comparison—“You can’t just pick up a violin and play”—makes clear that a positive outlook, like an instrument, requires practice. Doll follows this with accessible examples: appreciate a view or artwork, list things to be grateful for, or meditate. Other subheadings, including “Spread Some Kindness”, “Flash a Grin” and “Nurture Your Relationships”, divide the article into practical options. “Flash a Grin” is followed by the colloquial imperative “try cracking a smile”, making one action sound quick and accessible. Direct address and imperatives make the reader an active participant. In “Spread Some Kindness”, the tricolon “Hold the elevator door for someone, send a handwritten note, pay for the person in line behind you at the coffee shop” makes generosity concrete; the 2016 study adds evidence for the claim that kindness can increase happiness, and “the ripple effect” suggests that one action can spread outward. *(Text One, lines 25–52.)*

Finally, “It’s OK to Get Mad, Too” prevents the article’s message from becoming an instruction to feel cheerful at all times. Doll distinguishes useful “functional emotions”, which can prompt action against injustice, from “unproductive reactions” such as road rage. The closing contrast between a “wonderful and terrible place” and “good and bad things” accepts that life contains both. Ending on “It’s what you choose to put in front of you” returns to personal agency: the writer recommends practising a positive outlook while recognising that difficult emotions and situations are real. *(Text One, lines 54–60.)*

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[CONSTRUCTED MS-BASED RESPONSE]** *Original response, based on Text Two and the Results MS; not Pearson-scored. No mark is assigned.*

Newcomer begins with an intensely specific account of hardship: she is “sleeping on my friend’s couch”, holding “a huge medical bill” and has pneumonia. The accumulation makes her distress concrete before the friend offers the familiar advice, “Look on the bright side.” Newcomer’s blunt response, “I want to punch her in the face”, makes the mismatch between the crisis and the cheerful platitude startling. She immediately adds that she “would never actually want to cause her any harm”; this qualification keeps the line as comic hyperbole while preserving the frank, informal voice. The anecdote encourages readers to understand why positivity can feel dismissive when it ignores someone’s actual circumstances. *(Text Two, lines 1–7.)*

The transition “there’s some science behind my feelings” moves the piece from personal experience to an argument supported by research. The idiom “isn’t always all it’s cracked up to be” challenges the popular claim without asserting that positive thinking is always harmful. The question “What if embracing so-called ‘negative’ states … actually has a positive outcome?” invites readers to reconsider the labels themselves. The subheading “It’s Not All Rainbows and Unicorns” repeats a familiar image of effortless happiness but turns it into a warning. The juxtaposition of “embracing” with supposedly “negative” states challenges conventional labels, while “entitled” and “headlong pursuit” present constant positivity as an excessive, almost addictive demand. The idiom “shooting ourselves in the feet” makes its harm seem self-inflicted. *(Text Two, lines 8–17.)*

The writer then explains specific risks and supports them with research. Constant positivity can mean that someone “can never relax”, because a negative thought might “squirm its way to the surface”; the metaphor makes the suppressed thought seem persistent and difficult to control. The phrase “no back-up plan” gives a practical consequence to insisting that “everything works out”. References to “One study” and “Another study” show a pattern of evidence: pressure not to feel negative can make people experience negative emotions more frequently, and repeating a positive self-statement left some people with low self-esteem feeling worse. *(Text Two, lines 18–24.)* The parenthetical examples of “a bad job”, “a looming deadline” and “an issue with a partner or friend” make the risk of avoidance concrete. *(Text Two, lines 31–33.)* Named experts, including psychiatrist Mark Banschick and psychologist Julie Norem, further support the argument. Newcomer also qualifies the discussion of mania: she says a typical person does not experience positivity at that level, but an ordinary person can still “lose their judgment” or avoid “necessary action”. The qualification prevents the warning from becoming an exaggerated claim about everyone. *(Text Two, lines 26–40.)*

The heading “The Benefits of Being a Pessimist” marks a clear turn from the costs of forced positivity to the possible value of negative thinking. Newcomer cites findings that negative moods can produce “better quality and more persuasive arguments”, improve “memory and mental accuracy”, and prompt more careful thought. The alliteration in “memory and mental” draws attention to the cognitive benefits, and the positive wording attached to pessimism challenges the assumption that a negative mood is useless. The suggestion that “preparing for the worst” may decrease future suffering shows how realistic caution can help, while the closing advice to acknowledge negative emotions gives the reader a constructive alternative to denying them. *(Text Two, lines 41–53.)*

Her final paragraph explicitly rejects the opposite extreme: “We’re certainly not recommending that everyone become a sourpuss for life.” The inclusive “we” in “We’re certainly not recommending” makes the conclusion sound shared and supportive, while the informal “sourpuss” softens the argument and reassures readers that the writer is not replacing forced optimism with permanent gloom. The final sentence balances “Stop and smell the roses” against the humorous image of “dog poo on the sidewalk beneath your feet”. The contrast makes her conclusion memorable: enjoy positive experiences, but stay alert to real risks. *(Text Two, lines 54–58.)*

Q6 is assessed by AO2; Level 5 is 9–10 and calls for perceptive analysis with discriminating references (MS p. 10). ### Q7 — comparison of ideas, perspectives and methods

**[CONSTRUCTED MS-BASED RESPONSE]** *Original AO3 comparison, based on both exact source texts and the Results MS; not Pearson-scored. No mark is assigned.*

Both writers question easy slogans about positivity, but they use that starting point to take different approaches. Text One quotes advice such as “Turn that frown upside down!” before admitting that being told to smile can make someone want to “punch a wall”. Text Two opens with a friend saying “Look on the bright side” while Newcomer is ill, unable to pay a medical bill and living on a friend’s couch; her reply, “I want to punch her in the face”, makes the cliché seem especially inadequate in a real crisis. The similar comic image of anger lets both writers acknowledge readers’ resistance, but Doll uses it to build rapport before offering practical guidance, while Newcomer uses a personal anecdote to question whether positive thinking is always helpful. *(Text One, lines 3–7; Text Two, lines 1–7.)*

The texts make a close connection through their “rainbows and unicorns” imagery. Doll says that positivity does not mean sweeping real thoughts aside to make room for “unicorns and rainbows”; she defines it instead as “viewing situations from a more well-rounded perspective”. Newcomer’s subheading “It’s Not All Rainbows and Unicorns” similarly challenges an unrealistically cheerful outlook, then describes the “headlong pursuit” of constant positivity as potentially harmful. Both reject pretending that everything is fine, but Doll’s central message is that optimism can be trained and used constructively, whereas Newcomer concentrates on the pressure and risks of trying to stay positive all the time. *(Text One, lines 10–14; Text Two, lines 12–17.)*

Their evidence also supports contrasting emphases. Doll cites Noulas and Davidson, research on health and happiness, and a 2016 kindness study to show why positive thinking and helpful actions can benefit people. Newcomer names Banschick and Norem and refers to “One study” and “Another study” to show how suppressing negative feelings may backfire. She then reverses the expected language by describing negative moods as producing “better quality and more persuasive arguments” and improving “memory and mental accuracy”. Both writers use experts and research, but Doll mainly uses them to support positive habits, while Newcomer uses them to complicate the case for positivity and establish possible benefits of pessimism. *(Text One, lines 18–24, 26–28 and 35–42; Text Two, lines 18–40 and 42–49.)*

Their structures make their purposes distinct. Doll’s subheadings—“Build the Skill”, “Spread Some Kindness” and “Nurture Your Relationships”—organise practical suggestions. Her direct address and imperatives invite readers to act: the tricolon “Hold the elevator door for someone, send a handwritten note, pay for the person in line behind you at the coffee shop” gives kindness concrete forms. Newcomer also uses subheadings, including “The Benefits of Being a Pessimist”, but her article moves from personal experience through research and counter-arguments to a more qualified conclusion. The inclusive “we” in phrases such as “shooting ourselves in the feet” makes her warning feel shared rather than directed at one individual. *(Text One, lines 25–52; Text Two, lines 12–17 and 41–42.)*

Neither writer ultimately recommends an extreme. Doll’s final section, “It’s OK to Get Mad, Too”, recognises that anger can prompt action against injustice; her description of the world as “both a wonderful and terrible place” accepts mixed experience. Newcomer says she is not asking everyone to become a “sourpuss for life” and explicitly recommends a balance between optimism and realism. Her final contrast—“Stop and smell the roses, but first check for dog poo on the sidewalk beneath your feet”—makes that caution humorous. Both writers therefore value a balanced outlook, although Doll emphasises practising positive habits while Newcomer stresses acknowledging negative emotions instead of denying them. *(Text One, lines 54–60; Text Two, lines 54–58.)*
