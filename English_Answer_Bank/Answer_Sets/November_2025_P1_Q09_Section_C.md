> **Kindness is ordinary, and that is the problem**
>
> The statement is true, and it is true in the boring way: people are not always kind because people are tired, busy, frightened and short of money, and kindness is the first thing to go when any of those four arrive. That is not a defence of unkindness. It is a description of where it comes from, and a description is more useful than a complaint.
>
> Start with the evidence on the other side, because there is a great deal of it. When the floods came through our town two years ago, the mosque opened its hall to everybody, the shop on the corner gave away its bottled water, and a boy I had never spoken to spent a whole night carrying sandbags for houses that were not his. Multiply that by every town and you have a fact about human beings that no headline will ever report: most help, most of the time, is given by people who gain nothing.
>
> So why does the other thing happen? Watch a queue. In a queue with enough time, people are courteous; in a queue with a bus coming, they are not. Scarcity is the engine. Add fear and you get worse: a man who is afraid for his job will throw a colleague overboard without ever thinking of himself as cruel, because from the inside it does not feel like cruelty, it feels like survival. And add distance — a screen, a road, a hundred metres — and kindness stops altogether, because it turns out to be a skill that needs a face.
>
> There is also the harder question the statement skips: is kindness always the priority? I do not think it is. A surgeon who stops to comfort a family in the corridor instead of scrubbing in has been kind to one person and unkind to another. A teacher who passes a weak essay out of pity has been kind for a term and unkind for a lifetime. Kindness that never competes with anything is not a virtue; it is a luxury.
>
> Which is where I end up. I agree with the statement almost entirely, but I would add the part it leaves out: we are not always kind, and we are not always wrong not to be. What matters is the reason. Haste is a reason; fear is a reason; the wish not to hurt someone's feelings is a reason. Contempt is not a reason. And the difference between a bad day and a bad person is whether they can still see, in the queue, that the person in front of them is also having one.
