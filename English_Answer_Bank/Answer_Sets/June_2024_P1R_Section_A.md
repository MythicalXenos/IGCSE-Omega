### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Theroux opens with Willy’s dependence on him: “The first moving creature Willy saw was me”, followed by “he snuggled in my hand”. The first-person account places the writer directly inside the experience, while “warm cage” and “plenty to eat” show the practical care he gives the young bird. The details that Willy “doubled in size in ten days” and grew feathers “within a month” compress time and emphasise how quickly the writer watches him develop. Theroux then reflects that his writing day was “brightened in unexpected ways”: the positive verb “brightened” conveys pleasure, while “unexpected” suggests that caring for Willy brought an unanticipated change to his routine.

The writer uses touch, movement and sound to make the relationship vivid. “Pulsing heart” and “warm body” let the reader imagine holding a living, vulnerable creature; later, the “pillow of warmth” metaphor makes the goose’s feathers seem comforting and luxurious. Lists such as “climb in and splash, diving, beating his wings” create a lively picture of Willy enjoying the trough. Another list—“working his beak, sniffing, lowering his head and lengthening his neck”—records the writer’s close attention to Willy’s behaviour and his protectiveness when another goose comes near. The range of sounds, from “the caw of contentment” to “the shriek” and “trumpeting”, makes Willy seem expressive and distinctive; the repeated “squawk of recognition” and the image of him flying to the writer convey attachment.

As the passage develops, the focus moves from early care to a lasting companionship and then to private reflection. Calling Willy “a fixture” and “a certainty” presents him as a steady presence; “a consolation” shows how much emotional comfort the relationship gives the writer. The simile “as true as a compass point” conveys his perception of Willy’s loyalty, while the image of the goose as “a graceful ornament” and the “luminous” shapes at night emphasise his beauty. In the final paragraph, the writer contrasts how an animal behaves before visitors with the “solitary pleasure” and “mutual understanding” known by its owner. This ending presents looking after Willy as more than a task: it has given Theroux a close bond that outsiders cannot fully see.

**MS alignment check:** This answer develops the writer’s care, Willy’s rapid growth and behaviour, the sensory and figurative descriptions, the writer’s emotional reliance on the goose, and the private knowledge of the owner–pet relationship (MS pp. 5–6). It is written to the AO2 Level 5 descriptor—perceptive analysis supported by discriminating references (MS p. 7)—but is not a checklist response and has not been awarded a mark.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** The writer organises the account under the headings “Rescue”, “Recovery”, “The Reintegration Process” and “Living Wild”. This sequence takes the reader from an emergency to the elephants’ gradual independence. In the opening, the promise to use “whatever transportation is necessary”, including an “aircraft or helicopter”, makes the rescue effort sound urgent and ambitious. The contrast between this capability and the “profoundly traumatic event” of being orphaned draws attention to the animals’ vulnerability. The list “starvation, dehydration or injuries” broadens the danger, while the repeated “our” and the list of “milk, stretchers” and medicines present the Trust as a prepared, collective team.

The “Recovery” section stresses that care is continuous: “round-the-clock, day-in-and-day-out commitment” uses repetition and time phrases to convey the Keepers’ dedication. “Secure base” and “loving environment” contrast with “greatest need” and a “difficult period”, presenting the Nursery as protection from trauma. The three-part phrase “unconditional love, emotional support and the learning experiences” combines emotional and developmental care. Comparing the elephants to “human children” and describing “toys and stimulation”, games and hugs encourages sympathy; the practical details of blankets, rainwear, sunscreen and an umbrella also show how carefully the carers respond to the calves’ needs.

The later headings present rehabilitation as a long process rather than a quick release. The verb “graduate” makes the move to a Reintegration Unit sound like an achievement, while references to Anti-Poaching Teams and protected areas reassure readers that the transition is managed. “Vast wilderness” contrasts with the secure Nursery, showing what the elephants are being prepared to enter. The statement that independence can take “8 to 12 years” emphasises the scale of the commitment. Finally, the idiom “flown the nest” frames independence warmly, and the play on “elephants never forget” leads into a positive ending: former orphans may return to visit and share the joy of their own young. The structure therefore presents care as both immediate protection and patient preparation for life in the wild.

**MS alignment check:** This answer analyses the rescue list, emotional and physical vulnerability, collective pronouns, subheadings, continuous commitment, nurturing/family imagery, practical care, protection and the long return to the wild (MS pp. 9–10). It is aligned with the AO2 Level 5 descriptor but has not been awarded a mark.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers present caring for orphaned animals as meaningful, but they show different kinds of experience. Theroux tells a personal story about one goose. His first-person “I” and “my” make the bond individual, and the tactile phrases “pulsing heart” and “warm body” place the reader close to his contact with Willy. The Trust’s article speaks from a group perspective—“When we receive a call”—and describes Keepers caring for many elephants. Its wider, organised response makes the experience seem like coordinated work rather than one person’s private relationship.

The texts also differ in how they present the care itself. Theroux’s account is reflective and descriptive: Willy’s “pillow of warmth” and “the singular beauty” of the geese convey the writer’s personal affection and admiration. The Trust’s headings—“Rescue”, “Recovery”, “The Reintegration Process” and “Living Wild”—organize a sequence of practical stages. Details such as feeding every three hours, sleeping near the elephants and protecting them from the weather explain the sustained work involved. Yet both writers emphasise warmth and attachment: Theroux describes Willy flying to him, while the Trust promises “unconditional love” and says carers are “always there for a hug”.

Their purposes and outcomes are different as well. Theroux’s ending celebrates the “solitary pleasure” and “mutual understanding” that a pet-owner experiences, so the benefit is partly personal comfort and companionship. The Trust’s article is more informative and advisory: it explains how elephant calves are eventually prepared for independent life in protected areas and the wild. The “8 to 12 years” needed for independence shows the scale of that work. Both texts present human care as valuable, but Text One focuses on a lasting bond with a particular animal, while Text Two stresses the group effort and long-term welfare that allow many animals to return to the wild.

**MS alignment check:** The comparison is integrated across both texts and covers scale, narrative perspective, purpose, description, care and outcomes. It draws on the MS’s contrasts between one person and a charity, first-person and group perspective, personal benefits and animal welfare, and entertaining versus advisory/informative presentation (MS pp. 11–12). The AO3 Level 5 descriptor requires varied, comprehensive comparisons with balanced, discriminating references (MS p. 12); this is an original worked answer, not a score prediction.
