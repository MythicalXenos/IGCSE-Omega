### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** The writer first makes her advice sound personal and relatable. She describes rediscovering the outdoors and contrasts an “unhealthy and unhappy” screen-based lifestyle with the “significant difference” and enjoyment that being outside brought her. Her first-person experience makes the benefits feel real rather than abstract. The conversational aside about “cat videos on Facebook” and Netflix shows that she understands habits her young readers may share; “Don’t get me wrong” keeps the tone friendly instead of scolding. The direct appeal “listen up” then signals that her advice matters.

The numbered reasons and subheadings make the article easy to follow, while the simile comparing smartphones to “another limb” vividly suggests dependence on them. Dunbar shifts from this familiar problem to commands such as “ditch the Wi-Fi” and “get yourself outdoors”. Her repeated “you” and “we” make the advice feel both directly addressed to the reader and shared by a wider community. Mentioning “psychological studies” gives the argument an evidence-based tone, although she does not name a particular study. Three-part lists—such as “the media, social platforms, and smartphones”—make the pressure feel pervasive; the later catalogue of vitamin-D symptoms and health risks gives readers concrete reasons to care. The movement through concentration, sleep and physical-health benefits broadens the case, while “I can vouch” returns it to personal experience. By contrast, examples such as “a stroll in the park” show that the solution need not be ambitious. The alliterative “fantastic form” makes outdoor activity sound appealing. She ends with the confident promise that readers will feel “much happier”, leaving the argument on an upbeat, encouraging note.

**MS alignment check:** This response draws on the personal anecdote, informal direct address, numbered structure, smartphone simile, reference to studies, imperatives, lists, accessible activity and positive ending identified in the indicative content (MS p. 6). The Level 5 descriptor requires perceptive analysis and discriminating references (MS p. 7); it is not a checklist requiring every indicative point.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Bryson makes the walk seem punishing despite its initially picturesque setting. The “chuckling stream” and “hearty blue” sky suggest a pleasant landscape, but “brittle ice”, the repeated “brown” and the “unyielding” cold undermine that impression. His list “bug-eyed, breathing hard, heart kabooming alarmingly” makes his physical distress vivid; the exaggerated sound is comic, but it also shows how badly the climb affects him. The blunt short sentence “It was hell” cuts off any suggestion that he is coping well.

The repeated “hopelessly” and “way too much” sound like frustrated thoughts returning as the climb continues; the echo between “weighed” and “way too much” also makes his complaint self-mocking. Bryson describes the hill through a “curtain of trees” and an “ever-receding” slope, then repeats “another” as each apparent summit reveals more climbing. This makes progress feel confusing and endless. The three-part build from trees, to the receding slope, to his own “plodding weariness” crowds the reader with the same obstacles; “ages and ages” stretches the climb in time. Calling the apparent summit a “pitiless deception” makes the landscape seem almost deliberately cruel. Even at the top, the cold air and tough, wind-bent vegetation are not comforting: he is “past caring” and sprawls under his pack. The view is “sensational” and “splendid”, but he cannot escape the fact that he still has to walk it. The later repetition of “It was hell” brings the account back to its opening complaint, while the repeated “seven miles” makes an apparently modest distance sound absurdly demanding. Finally, he compares the pack to something that “doesn’t want to be lifted”; direct address such as “Imagine” and “Don’t tell me” invites the reader to feel its weight. “a private little world of weariness and woe” sums up his comic misery.

**MS alignment check:** The answer covers contrasts, physical symptoms, repetition, sentence length, the seemingly endless hill, the summit/view contrast, direct address and the circular return to “It was hell” (MS p. 9). The Level 5 descriptor calls for perceptive analysis of language and structure with discriminating references (MS p. 10). These points are examples, not a mandatory checklist.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers connect the outdoors with personal experience and address readers directly, but they present very different overall perspectives. Dunbar’s article is advice for young people: her numbered reasons turn outdoor activity into a series of general benefits, and her first-person example presents it as something that can improve ordinary life. Bryson, by contrast, recounts one particular trek. His use of “you” draws readers into that experience, but the memoir-like account mainly entertains through his struggle.

The difference is especially clear in tone. Dunbar’s positive language presents outdoor time as beneficial and manageable: she refers to its “extremely positive impact” and says even “a stroll in the park” can count. Bryson begins with a “chuckling stream”, yet quickly shifts to the “unyielding” cold and the blunt “It was hell”. His repeated complaints and exaggerated descriptions make the trek seem exhausting, while also making the account humorous. So one writer encourages readers to go outside; the other shows how unpleasant a real walk can feel.

Neither view is completely one-sided. Dunbar’s list of health problems warns readers what inactivity may cost, whereas Bryson does reach a view he calls “sensational” and “splendid”. But the view cannot erase the effort: he still has to walk it, and the trail “ran on”. Their structures reinforce their purposes: the article moves through numbered benefits towards the promise of feeling “much happier”, while Bryson’s chronological journey circles back to “It was hell” and ends without a triumphant finish. Text One is broadly persuasive and upbeat; Text Two is more mixed, self-mocking and focused on the hardship of one trek.

**MS alignment check:** The comparison is sustained across purpose, audience, experience, tone, structure and endings, with references balanced between both texts. It reflects the MS’s contrasts between an advisory article and a humorous travel account, and between a general set of benefits and one difficult trek (MS p. 11). The Level 5 descriptor asks for varied, comprehensive comparison and balanced, discriminating evidence; a response considering only one text is capped at Level 2 (MS p. 12).
