## Cross-paper examiner guidance — June 2025 report, Q11 (quoted; different prompt, method only)

- The central failure the examiners identify: “some candidates **did not focus on the descriptive nature of the task and wrote narrative responses** focusing how or why they were in the beautiful place… some responses were **too narrative, with pockets of description**.”
- On listing: less successful responses “tended to **list features of a place without developing the description. They repeated adjectives like ‘nice,’ ‘beautiful,’ ‘amazing,’ without exploring what made the place beautiful**. Some wrote about beautiful places in general or included several examples.”
- More successful: “a **focused and sustained attempt at crafting a piece of description**… creating a vivid picture… **using the different senses**… These responses had flair and enthusiasm, with **effective imagery, creating mood and atmosphere**. They used an impressive range of vocabulary… able to **stay sharply focused on the place itself, describing it in detail and looking at the reasons why it was significant**.”
- AO5 note that recurs across all three Section C questions: “a **lack of variety in the use of punctuation**” was common, and “**over-ambitious vocabulary which was not effective or appropriate**” limited some responses.

**Design consequences (applied to an object rather than a place):** description dominates and every memory is attached to a physical detail already described; the five senses are used in sequence rather than scattered; no adjective is left unearned (“beautiful”, “amazing” and “nice” appear nowhere); the piece stays with **one object** rather than several; and the memories explain significance without turning into a plot. Vocabulary is chosen for precision, not size.

## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed description

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 20) and its AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 21–22), with method informed by the June 2025 report commentary quoted above. **No report or script exists for this paper.** Not a Pearson script, not examiner-marked, **no score claimed**.

> **The ball on the shelf**
>
> It is a football, or the shape a football keeps after twenty years of not being kicked. The leather has gone the colour of weak tea and cracked along every seam, so the panels look like a dry riverbed seen from above. One pentagon has worn through entirely and shows a grey lining underneath, like a wound that has stopped bothering to heal.
>
> It sits on the top shelf of my wardrobe, behind the shoebox of chargers, and it weighs almost nothing now. That is the thing that surprises me every time I take it down. A football should have weight. This one is light as a shell, and when I press my thumb into the worn patch it gives with a small dry sigh, the air of twenty summers having gone out of it one puncture at a time.
>
> The stitching is still there, six rows of it, whitened and furred, and there is a name on it in biro that has faded to the colour of a bruise.
>
> The smell is what brings the field back: leather and dust and, faintly, cut grass. Behind the flats there was a strip of ground with two goals made of jumpers and a hedge that ate every ball that went over it. June evenings, seven o'clock, the light going gold and long. My father lacing his boots on the step, one foot up, not talking.
>
> Then rain — the particular heavy rain of that summer — and the ball soaking it up until it was twice the weight and stung your forehead when you headed it. Mud to the ankles. His laugh, which I can hear, and his hands, which I can picture better than his face: broad, wet, chalked with mud, turning the ball over to find the valve.
>
> Then the last evening, when the field was empty and I was sixteen and too old for it, and he kicked it once, hard, into the hedge.
>
> It is quiet on the shelf. The dust settles on it the way dust settles on everything that has been put down carefully rather than thrown away, and I leave it there, because it is the only thing in this room that is still the exact weight and colour of a summer evening I cannot otherwise get back to.

*Word count: 390 words — close to the QP's “approximately 400”, and the shortest of the three June 2026 P1 Section C models on purpose, since description rewards density over length.*

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Objects:** the MS permits “a range of objects which may be real or imaginary” — a watch, a mug, a tool, a musical instrument, a garment, a letter, a photograph, a ticket, a car, a phone, a piece of jewellery, or something invented. Class photographs, sports kit and family furniture are equally valid.
- **Structure:** object-then-memories (as here); a single sustained memory explored in detail; or the object described in three different settings to show change. A candidate may also describe the object in the present tense and move between present and memory.
- **Balance:** the MS asks for “descriptions of the object **and** the memories it evokes”, so neither half should be abandoned. The June 2025 report's warning cuts both ways here: pure description without memory would ignore the second half of the task.
- **Imaginary object:** allowed explicitly by the MS. A candidate inventing an object should describe it as concretely as a real one, since the descriptor rewards evoking “what the object is”.
- **Avoid:** writing about several objects, listing features without development, or turning the memories into a full narrative with dialogue and a resolution.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO4:** the piece is description with memory folded into it, sustained from first to last sentence, with a consistent reflective voice and a controlled circular structure; the significance of the object is shown and only stated once, in the final clause.
- **AO5:** wide punctuation range used for effect, deliberate sentence rhythm, precise concrete nouns, and no reliance on inflated adjectives.
