### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Keegan begins by drawing readers into a familiar experience: “Think of the birthdays” and the second-person “you have spent” make the general subject of celebration personal. The list of “birthdays, weddings, anniversaries and national holidays” shows how many kinds of occasion recur in ordinary life. The tricolon “to congratulate, celebrate and show affection” moves from recognition to shared enjoyment and personal care, presenting celebrations as ways of expressing relationships. The capitalised, conversational phrase “A Thing” gives the historical account a light, accessible voice rather than making it sound remote or solemn.

The passage then widens from familiar occasions to a long history. The examples of Egyptian pharaohs, a birthday feast in the Bible, and Claudia Severa’s Roman invitation, followed by the dates “3,000 BCE” and “100 CE”, give the explanation a documentary feel. The detail that women’s birthdays were not generally celebrated until around the 12th century also shows that customs have changed over time. Keegan then shifts from when celebrations began to why people need them, asking, “where does our urge to rejoice collectively actually come from?” This question invites the reader to consider the significance of celebrating, while “collectively” prepares for the focus on community.

The expert voices make the explanation sound informed: Dr Sheri Jacobson links celebrations to people being “fundamentally social creatures” and to the fact that we “still need other people for survival”. The inclusive “we” makes this sound like a shared human need rather than a claim about other people. The movement from community to individual benefits broadens the case. During “times of uncertainty”, people may withdraw into “darker thoughts”, but surrounding themselves with comfortable people is presented as an antidote. The named chemicals—“adrenaline”, “serotonin”, “endorphins” and “oxytocin”—give a specific, physiological explanation for feeling better; the contrast with stress-causing “cortisol” also makes the benefits of a hug concrete.

A later question, “how do we approach it mindfully?”, moves the article from explaining celebration to advising readers. Pamela Jacobsen’s image of going through life on “automatic pilot” suggests that routine can prevent “in-the-moment joy”; her repeated emphasis on “gratitude” presents celebration as a way to notice and value relationships. In the conclusion, “deviation from normal social order” is set against “life-affirming”, reframing partying as a positive release. The contrast between “bringing structure” and a world that is “chaotic and frightening” presents celebration as reassuring as well as enjoyable. The final phrase, “about as uplifting as it gets”, leaves the reader with an emphatically positive view of its importance.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Sweeney opens with a historical frame, calling New Year’s one of the “oldest-celebrated” holidays and dating its celebration to “2000 BC”. The informal aside “if you’re struggling with one night of celebrating, imagine eleven” turns the history into a joke directed at the reader, creating a friendly blog voice. The question “But why is New Year’s Eve really worth celebrating?” then moves the passage from background to argument. Describing it as “a symbol for starting over” and a chance to leave things behind while “embracing change and challenges” presents the occasion as an opportunity, not simply a party.

The writer avoids claiming that midnight has magical power: the new year can bring a “mental reset”, not because the calendar “creates some kind of magic”, but because it gives people a point at which to reflect and act. This qualification makes the argument sound realistic. The inclusive phrase “most of us” and direct address to “you” invite readers to reflect on their own changes. Repetition of “new” and the recurring subheadings—such as “New Beginnings Mean You Can Try Something That Makes You Happy” and “New Beginnings Allow For Setting New, Attainable Goals”—organise the piece as a sequence of manageable possibilities. The parallel headings help readers navigate the advice and reinforce the central idea of a fresh start.

Sweeney makes the suggestions concrete through lists: hobbies might include “reading more, taking more photos, crafting, cooking”, while social options include joining a club. The list gives readers different routes rather than insisting on one resolution. The personal anecdote beginning “Last year, I made a resolution to eat out less” shows the writer discovering an unexpected enjoyment of cooking, making the advice sound lived-in rather than abstract. By contrast, the short imperative “Stop doing things that make you unhappy” is emphatic and direct. The phrase “fairly common and totally respectable resolutions” uses alliteration to give giving up bad habits an approving tone. Where an activity may be unappealing, Sweeney offers alternatives: hiking, a sport or an exercise class instead of the gym, and taking a break or limiting time on social media. “Unpacking a little emotional baggage is a great way to travel forward” then turns letting go into an image of moving more freely toward the future.

The later advice is deliberately reassuring: “Make goals that are measurable and attainable” is followed by the reminder that “you have 365 days”, challenging the idea that change must happen all at once. In the self-care section, the alternatives “an hour a day, a day of the week, or a few days a month” make the advice flexible and realistic. “Wake-up, work, eat, sleep, repeat” compresses daily life into a monotonous rhythm; the verb “escape” suggests that planned self-care can interrupt it. The final section recognises that some changes take longer than twelve months. The balanced phrases “a perfect place to start” and “the time to finish” avoid promising instant success, while the closing tricolon, “A plan, a support system and a desire for meaningful change”, gives readers a practical basis for continuing. The text therefore ends with encouragement that reaches beyond January rather than with a short-lived resolution.

It aims at the AO2 Level 5 descriptor (MS p. 11), but is an original, unscored worked answer.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers argue that celebration matters, but Keegan considers milestones broadly while Sweeney concentrates on New Year as one particular opportunity for change. Keegan invites readers to “Think of the birthdays, weddings, anniversaries and national holidays” in their calendars, so her examples encompass communal and personal occasions. Sweeney instead frames New Year as a “symbol for starting over”. Both refer to history—Keegan gives examples from Ancient Egypt, the Bible and Rome, while Sweeney calls the holiday “4,000-year-old”—but Keegan traces several changing customs whereas Sweeney quickly turns from the holiday’s past to what an individual might do next.

Their explanations of celebration also differ in emphasis. Keegan uses named experts and specific references to “feel-good chemicals” to explain how celebrations strengthen social ties and affect the body and mind. Her focus on community is explicit: people “still need other people for survival”, and a hug is linked to oxytocin and reduced stress. Sweeney is more practical and self-directed. Repeated “New Beginnings” headings guide readers through examples such as taking up a hobby, giving something up and setting attainable goals; her anecdote about discovering that she enjoys cooking illustrates how one change can improve an individual’s well-being. Both also use inclusive language and direct address—Keegan’s “we” and “you”, and Sweeney’s “most of us” and “you have 365 days”—to draw readers into their arguments. Both offer reassurance, but Keegan’s evidence chiefly explains why shared celebrations help people, while Sweeney’s examples show how a person can use the new year to make changes.

Both also respond to uncertainty, though they present different kinds of comfort. Keegan says that, when life feels uncertain, enjoying how far one has come is “about as uplifting as it gets”; she presents celebration as a collective tradition that creates “structure” in a “chaotic and frightening” world. Sweeney presents a fresh start as a “mental reset” and reassures readers that “you have 365 days” to work towards a goal. Her advice recognises that change may take longer than the first month, while Keegan emphasises the immediate emotional and social benefits of gathering. Overall, both are informative and persuasive about celebration’s value, but Text One stresses shared connection across life’s milestones, whereas Text Two treats New Year as a prompt for individual, continuing self-improvement.

It follows the MS’s comparison points while using balanced references to each passage (MS pp. 12–13).
