### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Gregory states his position early: he is “fully convinced of the importance of art and design” and wants to explain his “personal story”. The first-person account makes his argument personal rather than abstract: his own education and career become evidence for art’s value.

He recalls being introduced to an exhibition at eight and says he can still “vividly recall” it. The simile “like walking into another dimension” presents the encounter as astonishing and transformative, while “never imagined” emphasizes how new the experience felt. His claim that the visit “has never left me” and remains “a major influence” shows that studying art affected him well beyond childhood.

The metaphorical phrase “absolutely embraced” conveys eagerness, and the triplet “make, mould and create” suggests absorbed, hands-on activity. He credits a teacher who “inspired, encouraged and supported” him, and recalls that art was “prized” at secondary school. The later invitation to “experiment, adapt and explore” reinforces his belief that art education should offer varied materials and room for discovery.

The hospital episode creates a contrast. Gregory calls the teacher “well-meaning” and describes her attempt to teach him to reproduce simple fish-and-bird line drawings, yet he “felt cheated” because he had already experienced more imaginative making. He recalls, “In desperation, the teacher allowed me to play with some clay instead.” The “endless possibilities” that “now flowed from my imagination” make the contrast with restricted copying explicit. His sense of “the anxiety flowing from the teacher” about whether the work could be marked suggests he understands the pressures on her even while criticizing the narrow task. The contrast distinguishes controlled reproduction from exploratory art-making.

Finally, his teaching career supplies evidence of art’s effect on others. He observes reluctant students becoming “willing risk-takers”, growing in confidence and embracing new possibilities. He worries that student teachers have been “denied” rich experiences and hopes they will “ignite the interest” of their own pupils. The closing questions ask readers how they can support, rather than limit, human development. Together, the personal history, contrast and final challenge present art as a valuable, lifelong part of learning, not simply a route to academic grades.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Safah’s first-person, conversational opening makes her enthusiasm immediate. “Anyone who’s been within ten feet of me” is humorous hyperbole. She jokes that adults over 30 with a maths degree “turn up their nose” at art; the parenthetical “no stereotyping intended” adds self-aware humour. “Shove me down the ‘study what’s useful’ pit” is a forceful metaphor for being pushed towards supposedly practical subjects. Her description of art as “such a massive part of my life” is emphatic, and references to its popularity at GCSE and its connection with creative industries show that she sees it as both personally important and relevant to young people.

She broadens that case through the repeated triplet “expression, passion and emotion” and a list of art’s uses: presenting opinions, challenging authority, recording history and allowing ordinary people to express themselves. The accumulation presents art as socially and emotionally powerful; the claim that it is “as close to immortality as we’ll ever get” gives that view an expansive, almost celebratory finish.

The paired questions “What is art actually supposed to be? What is it supposed to do, exactly?” lead into her criticism of narrow art education. Safah is “quite firmly against” the idea that art should simply copy nature; “sadly” conveys disapproval, and “mindless repetition” makes the exam-syllabus approach sound mechanical. This contrasts with “more unusual and inventive artists”, through whom she came to “truly love the subject” and understand its “freedom of expression”. The contrast shows that her enthusiasm is for creativity and meaning, not just technical accuracy.

Her account of Picasso makes that change personal. She admits that she once saw “a load of odd shapes and lines”, but later admired the “courage” behind his work. The triplet “new, strange and occasionally mad” gives his style an energetic, unconventional quality, and “fascinating” conveys her curiosity about his portraits. After recounting Picasso’s words, she says, “I fell in love with art again”, making her renewed enthusiasm explicit.

The final paragraphs extend art beyond galleries: books can reveal what is “hidden in the cracks in their art”, and Safah concludes that art can be approached in many ways: “whether we read about it, create it ourselves or simply appreciate it”. Her final direct address—“so long as you remember what art really is”—makes the reader a participant and frames art as accessible. Across the colloquial voice, questions, contrasts and personal example, Safah presents art as expressive, accessible and worth defending.

The AO2 Level 5 descriptor is on MS p. 8. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers regard art as important, but their perspectives come from different stages of life. Gregory looks back as an adult teacher on how art shaped his education and career; Safah writes as a teenager defending a subject she loves. Both use first person, yet Gregory’s reflective, fairly formal essay is comparatively measured, while Safah’s colloquial opening—“Anyone who’s been within ten feet of me”—sounds immediate and argumentative.

Their accounts of education show a shared belief in art’s value, but they emphasize different problems. Gregory recalls an exhibition that was “like walking into another dimension” and says it remains “a major influence” on his career. He credits teachers who “inspired, encouraged and supported” him and describes art as “prized” at school. Safah, by contrast, challenges the fact that creative subjects are being sidelined and is “quite firmly against” an approach based on “mindless repetition”. Gregory’s memories demonstrate what supportive teaching can make possible; Safah more directly criticizes schooling that restricts artistic expression.

Both writers also present art as something that develops people, though they support this idea in different ways. Gregory draws on his teaching career: he has seen reluctant students become “willing risk-takers”, grow in confidence and embrace new possibilities. Safah uses a broader list of art’s purposes—expressing emotion, presenting opinions, challenging authority and recording history—and a personal account of learning to admire Picasso. Her claim that art is “as close to immortality as we’ll ever get” gives it a public and enduring significance, whereas Gregory focuses more on the effect of art-making and teaching on individual development.

Their structures reflect these purposes. Gregory moves chronologically from childhood to teaching and ends with questions about how people can support “human development”, inviting reflection and responsibility. Safah uses rhetorical questions to challenge assumptions, then moves through her views on school art, Picasso and books before directly including the reader: art can be read about, created or appreciated, and “it’s everywhere”. The first text is almost autobiographical and persuasive; the second is a discursive, strongly opinionated defence of art.

Overall, both writers use positive language and personal examples to argue that art matters beyond examination results. Gregory presents it as a formative experience that can shape careers and help students grow; Safah presents it as a widely accessible form of expression and a challenge to narrow definitions of what is useful.

**MS alignment check:** This comparison considers both writers’ ideas and perspectives, uses balanced references, and compares adult/teenage viewpoints, autobiographical/discursive purposes, positive language, education and the effects of art. It develops the MS’s indicative comparison points (MS p. 9) against the AO3 Level 5 descriptor (MS p. 10). No mark is assigned to this constructed response.
