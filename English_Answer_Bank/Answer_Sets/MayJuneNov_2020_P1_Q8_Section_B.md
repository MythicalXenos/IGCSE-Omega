Some candidates may interpret the task more widely and use other examples than those mentioned in the passages.” The page ends “Reward all valid points.”

- **Important items that may be lost:** passports; keys; wallets/purses/money; documents; notebooks/paperwork; phones; other technological items; other valuable items.
- **What you should not do:** panic; search without a plan; make a mess; rush about; look in the same place several times; look in silly places; waste time looking where there is no chance of the lost item being found.
- **What you should do:** try to replace the lost item (if you really cannot find it); look near where the lost item should be; organise your search; search thoroughly; look in untidy areas; try to think where you were / what was happening the last time you saw the item; prepare valuable/important items to make them easily visible.

## [CONSTRUCTED GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed talk

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content and the November 2020 report guidance, using verified details from both texts. It is **not** a Pearson script, has **not** been examiner-marked, and **no score is claimed**. It is written as speech, with no headings, a greeting, spoken signposting, and a closing address.

> Right — hands up if you have lost something this term. Keep them up if it was something that actually mattered. Yes. That is most of the room, and that is exactly why I am standing here for the next few minutes.
>
> My aim is simple: by the end of this talk you will know the three things that go missing most often, the four things you should never do when they do, and a routine that actually works. No lecture, no finger-wagging — I have lost all of these myself, more than once.
>
> So, first, what actually goes missing. Passports, obviously, and if you have ever turned a bedroom upside down three nights before a flight you will know that particular flavour of terror: drawers emptied, old rucksacks unzipped, sandy foreign coins and abandoned sweets turning up instead, and the horrible moment when you have to admit that it is simply not there. Keys and wallets are the everyday version — five minutes late, whole morning ruined. Then documents and paperwork, which are the worst because they are usually the one thing you cannot replace quickly — birth certificates, exam certificates, forms that took a fortnight to get signed. Phones and other tech, which now carry half our lives in them. And the quiet ones nobody mentions: a ring your grandmother gave you, a notebook with a term of notes in it, a memory stick with your coursework on it.
>
> Now, what not to do. This is the part where I have made every mistake available. Do not panic — this sounds like advice from a poster, but panic is genuinely the thing that breaks your search, because your brain starts racing and you end up looking in the same three places over and over. Do not search without a plan. Do not make a mess: if you empty a bag onto the floor, you have simply created a second problem, and now you cannot tell what you have already checked. Do not rush about between rooms. Do not look in the same place five times because you are hoping it will appear, and do not waste time in places you already know it cannot be — the fridge is not hiding your passport.
>
> So what should you do? Four things. One: stay calm and go back to where the item is supposed to live — that is usually where it is, or within about a foot and a half of it. Two: search one room at a time, and do not move on until you have genuinely finished that room. Three: start with the messy areas, because if it were somewhere obvious you would have found it already. Four: sit down and rebuild the last time you saw it — where you were, what you were doing, who was with you, how you were feeling. That last one sounds strange, but it works: memory is easier to reach when you rebuild the scene around it.
>
> And if it is truly gone? Replace it, quickly, rather than losing a week to hoping. Ring the right office, ask what you need, and take the photo before you are too stressed to smile. Then do the boring thing that stops it happening again: one place for important things, every time, no exceptions. A bright folder for documents. A ridiculous keyring so your keys stop camouflaging themselves. A label on the thing you cannot afford to lose.
>
> Last thing, and this is the one I would actually like you to remember. Forgetting is normal. It is not a sign that you are stupid or disorganised; it is what brains do. What you can control is whether you have a system for when it happens. So: pick one place for your keys tonight, put your passport somewhere you could describe to a stranger, and the next time you lose something, come and find me — because I will probably have lost it first.
>
> Thank you.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

The MS list is explicitly non-exhaustive (“there are other possibilities… Reward all valid points”), and the QP invites candidates’ own examples.

- **Items:** “notebooks/paperwork” as distinct from documents, and “other valuable items” beyond the named categories, are valid; a locally convincing example (a school bus pass, a football kit, a relative’s ring) is equally creditable provided it is developed.
- **What not to do:** “waste time looking where there is no chance of the lost item being found” is the MS point closest to the model’s “the fridge is not hiding your passport” line; a candidate could instead develop “look in silly places” at length.
- **What to do:** “search thoroughly” and “organise your search” are MS points the model expresses as “finish one room” and “four things”; either could carry a whole paragraph.
- **Form choices:** a candidate may legitimately include a rhetorical question to the audience, a show-of-hands moment, an anecdote about losing homework for a maths class (the report’s own example), a slide reference, or a “any questions?” close. What the report penalises is (i) drifting into an article or essay, (ii) subheadings that make the form ambiguous, and (iii) a lively opening followed by flat prose.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO1:** all three bullets are developed at paragraph length; the *items* bullet — the report’s named weakness — is carried by consequences and personal detail rather than a list; Text One supplies the lived story and Text Two the expert advice, so both texts are used and rewritten rather than quoted in whole sentences.
- **AO4:** spoken register from first word to last — direct address, a show of hands, spoken signposting, an aside of self-deprecation, a second direct address in the middle, and a closing thank-you. No letter furniture, no headline, no subheadings.
- **AO5:** varied sentence lengths with deliberate fragments for spoken rhythm, commas in lists, semicolons and dashes, apostrophes checked, and no one-sentence paragraphs.
- **What is not claimed:** no Pearson or examiner mark, no level or score. The AO5 ceiling on this grid is Level 4, 7–8.
