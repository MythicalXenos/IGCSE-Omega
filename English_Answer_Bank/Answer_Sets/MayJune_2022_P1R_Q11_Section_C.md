## What this paper's own report says about Q11

- **The failure mode, in the strongest wording in the series:** “most examiners observed that a **significant number of responses were narrative-based, containing little description**, and told a story about something that had happened unexpectedly, such as finding lost treasure whilst on holiday.”
- **What success looked like:** “Those candidates who understood the descriptive focus provided detailed and successful descriptions of the experience **and its impact** with effective technical choices, **particularly alliteration and the use of adjectives**. These responses were enthusiastic and fully focused.”
- **The two credited examples:** the Twin Towers described as “something most certainly unexpected… the scene, the smells, the horror and atmosphere”; and “breaking your arm unexpectedly”, where “the description revolved around the feelings, the hospital and the pain”.
- **Strongest answers:** “used very effective imagery and gave perceptive descriptions of the unexpected experience and sometimes **focused on something original** which was unexpected.”
- **Weaker:** “pedestrian, used a limited range of vocabulary and lacked detail… entirely narrative-based, losing the descriptive focus of the task.”
- **AO5:** “examiners did comment that **the range of punctuation used was quite limited**.”

**Consequences:** hold the clock to a few minutes, keep it descriptive rather than plotted, put the **impact** inside the description (not in a moralising final paragraph), choose something original, and use alliteration and adjectives as the report's own examples do.

## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed description

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 19) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 21–22), with method from this paper's own report. **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **Four minutes without electricity**
>
> It happened at 6:41 on a Thursday, in the middle of a supermarket, and it happened to about two hundred people at once.
>
> One moment the aisle is a normal aisle: fluorescent, humming, a woman ahead of me reading the back of a packet with her glasses pushed up on her head. The next, everything stops. Not slowly — there is no dimming, no flicker, no warning. The lights go out and the sound goes out with them, and the sound is the part nobody expects to miss. The freezers cut, the fans cut, the tills cut, and in the space those machines leave behind you can suddenly hear a shop: a trolley wheel ticking to a halt, a child saying *Mummy?* twice, a man somewhere near the fruit laughing once, nervously, because he does not know yet whether laughing is allowed.
>
> It is not dark. That is the first surprise. The emergency lights come on in a thin green line above the fire doors, and the big front windows, still full of four o'clock sun, keep the whole front of the shop in a warm amber half-light, so the aisle I am standing in is striped: bright at the windows, brown in the middle, black towards the bakery. Everything looks like a photograph of itself.
>
> The second surprise is the quiet. Two hundred people, and for about thirty seconds nobody speaks above a murmur, as though we have all been handed the same instruction. Then the phones come out. That is the strangest part, and it is the part I keep thinking about: two hundred small white rectangles, lifted at once, turning the dark side of the shop into a field of glow-worms. Faces underneath them are lit from below, blue and intent. The woman with the glasses has stopped reading the packet and is filming the ceiling.
>
> And then, strangely, the politeness. A man in a suit begins directing people towards the tills as though he works there. Someone holds the door. A girl of about six says, loudly and with enormous confidence, *It's not scary, it's just dark*, and the woman behind her laughs properly this time.
>
> The lights come back at 6:45. The freezers groan awake, the fans resume, the tills beep, and the shop becomes a shop again. Nobody looks at anybody. But I have stood in a building with two hundred strangers while the machinery that runs it stopped, and I have watched what people do in the four minutes when nothing is expected of them.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Experiences the report names:** weather events; accidents; unexpected celebrations; the Twin Towers; a broken arm; finding lost treasure.
- **Original choices that satisfy “totally unexpected”:** a power cut; an animal inside a building; a sudden hailstorm; a stranger's act of kindness; a building that is empty when it should be full.
- **Structures:** a narrow time window (as here); the moment of impact then its delay; three successive surprises in one scene.
- **What to avoid:** telling the whole day as a plot; describing only what happened and not its effect; an accident with a moral at the end; a narrative with a twist; a list of feelings without a single sense.
