### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Krause presents grammar as more than a set of technical rules: it affects whether people understand a message and how they judge its writer. Her opening question, “Why is good grammar important?”, directly invites the reader to consider the issue. She then contrasts widely accepted texting and slang with grammar that will “set you apart in professional settings”. The phrase suggests that accurate writing can distinguish someone positively in work contexts. Her direct address, “your grammar, spelling and punctuation represent you in the world”, makes that judgement feel personal; the heading “Good grammar is a brand ambassador” casts grammar as a representative that speaks for the writer, while “trust signal” links correctness to reliability and attention to detail. The statistic that four in ten job applications are rejected because of poor grammar and spelling adds apparent evidence and makes the professional consequences seem immediate.

The writer also makes the subject approachable by describing her own experience as a grammar specialist. She says people may fear that she judges them, then admits, “I get that” and that she makes mistakes too. This self-awareness prevents her from sounding infallible. Her numbered categories—“Purists”, “Rebels” and “Don’t-know-don’t-care”—organise the debate into memorable groups. The descriptions are lightly humorous: the purist risks believing that poor grammar “heralds the fall of civilization”, while the rebels are “far too hip” to be caught up in “irritating rules”. The headings and lively labels make different attitudes easy to follow without turning the article into a dry list of rules.

Krause’s practical case is that grammar helps a writer communicate clearly. She cites the parallel claim, “The better the grammar, the clearer the message”, to connect accuracy with understanding. She then makes the risk of poor writing concrete by imagining a reader having to “translate your poorly written sentences” and getting the writer’s intent wrong. The rhetorical question “don’t I?” draws the audience into that problem; the short ending, “They’ll move on to the guy who is”, gives the consequence a blunt, memorable finish.

At the end, the writer broadens the argument from professional usefulness to the value of language itself. The three-part description “beautiful and powerful and worthy of respect” gives the closing section an elevated, almost philosophical tone. Her list of what language helps people do—from describing reality to making peace—presents it as central to human life. The final assertion, “Good grammar honors the power and beauty of words”, therefore frames grammatical care as respect for communication, not merely conformity to rules.

**MS alignment check:** This response develops the MS’s points about the opening question, direct address, personal voice, labelled groups, humour, professional credibility, clear communication and the uplifting final section (indicative content, MS pp. 6–7). The AO2 Level 5 descriptor requires perceptive analysis with discriminating references (MS p. 8); the indicative list is not exhaustive. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Godwin initially presents technology’s effect on English through a deliberately extreme example of online language: “LOL D@T is teh Rox0rz!!! 111oneleven”. He imagines this as proof of “the end of civilisation as we know it”, using hyperbole to mimic alarm about changing language. The casual aside “Goodness knows” then keeps the voice conversational and hints that the fear may be exaggerated. This opening engages readers who worry about “proper” grammar while preparing to question that reaction.

The writer next introduces linguist Gretchen McCulloch as offering “a different view”. Labels such as “rule-fiends” and “uptight” make rigid rule-followers sound narrow, while “a golden age of eloquence” presents internet communication as a possible expansion of expression. Godwin quotes McCulloch’s claim that “There’s no moral virtue in using language in the most uptight way”; by identifying her as a Canadian linguist and referring to her book, he gives her perspective credibility. The attribution matters: the passage presents McCulloch’s words as her view, rather than suggesting every quoted claim is Godwin’s own wording.

A list of new forms—“irony, tone, gesture, slang, abbreviations, in-jokes, silly accents, etc.”—conveys the range of changes. The examples of signing off with “xoxoxoxo” or using asterisks for “*emphasis*” show how writers use text to replace cues once supplied by speech or gesture. The article also recognises a possible difficulty: younger people may find their parents’ messages sound aggressive. However, McCulloch’s observation that people use some acronyms “some of the time” and do not fill every phrase with them reassures readers that informal forms have not displaced all other writing.

The later comments emphasise communication rather than strict rules. McCulloch’s statements “That’s going to make you a better writer” and “Good writing is writing that is read the way you intended it to be read” repeat the idea of writing to be understood. The dramatic phrase “your social life depends on it” shows how important written communication has become for younger people. By closing on McCulloch’s reassurance that “language change isn’t one of them”, Godwin leaves the reader with a calm, positive view: technology is changing English, but the article presents that change as adaptation, not the end of the language.

**MS alignment check:** The response covers the humorous opening, informal tone, McCulloch’s expertise and viewpoint, examples/listing, the positive effects of informal written practice, and the reassuring conclusion (indicative content, MS p. 10). The AO2 Level 5 descriptor is on MS p. 11. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers consider how language affects communication, but Krause stresses the value of careful grammar while Godwin presents technology-driven change as a normal, potentially positive development. Krause argues that grammar helps people communicate clearly and make a good impression; Godwin challenges the idea that new online forms automatically damage English. Their perspectives differ, although neither treats communication as unimportant.

Krause makes the social consequences of grammar personal. Her direct address—“your grammar, spelling and punctuation represent you in the world”—presents writing as part of a person’s public and professional image. She calls good grammar a “trust signal” and cites job applications being rejected because of poor grammar and spelling. Godwin, by contrast, opens with the mock online sentence “LOL D@T is teh Rox0rz!!! 111oneleven” and the exaggerated “end of civilisation as we know it”. The hyperbole makes panic about internet language seem excessive. Where Krause urges readers to take grammar seriously, Godwin uses humour to question whether unfamiliar forms should be feared.

Both writers use examples and structured lists to make their arguments accessible, but for different purposes. Krause divides attitudes into “Purists”, “Rebels” and “Don’t-know-don’t-care”, using humorous descriptions to explore why people value or resist grammar rules. Godwin describes different internet-user groups and lists new forms of expression, including “irony, tone, gesture, slang, abbreviations, in-jokes, silly accents”. His examples show how technology has expanded informal written communication. Both use a conversational voice and humour, yet Krause’s labels help persuade readers that good grammar matters, while Godwin’s examples make language change appear varied and adaptable.

The writers’ treatment of clarity also creates a useful contrast. Krause quotes the idea that “the better the grammar, the clearer the message”, and imagines a reader getting the writer’s intent wrong. Godwin also values being understood: McCulloch says good writing is read “the way you intended it to be read”. However, he presents new digital forms as ways of communicating tone and gesture, not simply as careless errors. His reference to parents misreading messages as aggressive recognises that technology can create misunderstandings, but the discussion also shows how users adapt to those new conventions.

Their endings underline the difference in emphasis. Krause finishes by calling language “beautiful and powerful and worthy of respect” and concludes that “Good grammar honors the power and beauty of words”, giving her article an uplifting defence of grammatical care. Godwin ends by reassuring readers that language change is not something to fear. Both value communication, but Krause closes by urging respect for grammar, whereas Godwin asks readers to accept the evolution of English alongside technology.

**MS alignment check:** The comparison is sustained across both texts, addresses ideas and perspectives as well as method, and balances references to each. It develops the MS’s contrasts between Krause’s personal argument about grammar and Godwin/McCulloch’s account of technology’s effects, while noting shared concerns with clear communication and humour (indicative content, MS p. 12). The AO3 Level 5 descriptor is on MS p. 13. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.
