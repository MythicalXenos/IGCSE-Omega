### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** (original MS-based response; not Pearson-scored). Anderson opens with the direct question “Have you ever wanted to learn a new skill” and then the familiar obstacle of not having enough time. This invites readers to recognize their own experience before offering advice. The examples “speak Spanish, make fresh pasta, learn how to knit or master surfing” range across different interests and levels of challenge, making the advice feel relevant to a broad audience. The claim that successful people are “always learning and developing new skills” then presents learning as useful self-development, not just a hobby.

The numbered subheadings make the advice look like a manageable sequence. “Pick A Passion” uses alliteration and a direct instruction to make the first step memorable. The repeated conditional “if” personalizes the advice: readers are asked to connect a new skill to what they already enjoy. The reward that dedication can feel “part of the fun rather than a chore” is reassuring. Anderson then acknowledges the difficulty of learning: the tricolon “time, focus and concentration” stresses the effort required, while “tired and confused” warns against dividing attention. The contrasting outcome, “success and mastery”, gives the reader a reason to follow the advice.

The writer explains how to make progress in small steps, including a surfer first practising how to stand on a board on the sand. Saying the next stage “will be much easier” reassures the reader that a complex skill can be broken down. The short imperative “Alternate between researching and practicing” is clear and memorable, and the language-learning example shows what active practice looks like. In the final section, time becomes “your most valuable asset”; familiar distractions such as Instagram and Netflix make the advice concrete. The ending returns to the opening concern about time and closes positively: even an hour used wisely can produce “a lot of progress in a short amount of time”. The friendly, practical structure presents improvement as achievable through interest, focus and consistent practice.

**MS alignment check:** The response addresses the opening question and direct address, repeated conditionals and examples, numbered subheadings, alliteration, the tricolon, contrasting outcomes, the surfing example, the short imperative, modern distractions and positive ending (MS pp. 6–7). The Level 5 descriptor calls for perceptive analysis with discriminating references (MS p. 7); the indicative points are examples, not a checklist.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** (original MS-based response; not Pearson-scored). Willis begins by showing the social shame attached to not swimming: people ask how his parents could have “condemned” him to a “shameful existence”. The exaggerated language makes the judgment sound harsh, but he immediately rejects it with “not their fault”, then recalls his “strident objections”. The repeated “I hated them” and blunt description “I was awful” establish how unpleasant the childhood lessons felt. “loathed” and “dreaded” intensify that fear, while “all flailing limbs” makes his lack of coordination vivid and faintly comic.

He widens the account beyond himself with the Red Cross statistic that “About one in five Americans can’t swim” and the figure of “28%”. This makes the difficulty seem common rather than an isolated personal failing. At the same time, his description of an “instinctive, not-totally-irrational fear” uses dry humour to acknowledge why water can seem threatening. His decision to try as an adult is expressed through a three-part list—he “bought a pair of goggles, signed up for adult lessons, and prepared to confront” his “oldest, wettest nemesis”. The list shows resolve, while the mock-heroic “nemesis” makes the challenge sound both serious and amusing.

The first lesson is presented as chaotic. His limbs seem to have an “independent existence”; the personification in “none of which seem interested in what the others are doing” turns his poor coordination into a joke. The verbs “churn” and “chop” make his movements forceful but uncontrolled; the “sinking ship” metaphor shows the panic beneath the humour. He contrasts this with children “zipping up and down” their lanes, who make swimming look effortless. The movement from “three decades of unbridled terror” to “to my genuine astonishment, I can swim” makes his first success feel hard-won. Yet “panic-sprinting” and the simile of “a panicked trout” keep the achievement self-deprecating rather than triumphant.

The short sentence “I do it, though” marks persistence despite those problems. Temporal details—“Midway through the third lesson”, “the next class, and then the one after that”—show gradual improvement, not instant mastery. In the ending, Willis broadens his personal experience with “we” and “our”, arguing that adults can still try genuinely new things. “euphoria” and the idea that “anything is possible” give the conclusion hope, while the modest example of swimming “25 meters uninterrupted” keeps the optimism grounded. Humour, candid physical detail and the change over time present adult learning as frightening and difficult, but possible and rewarding.

**MS alignment check:** The response covers social judgment, his refusal to blame his parents, childhood dislike, physical disorganization, research and statistics, humour, determination, the children as a contrast, the long-standing fear, self-deprecation, temporal markers, inclusive pronouns and the positive ending (MS pp. 9–10). The Level 5 descriptor calls for perceptive analysis with discriminating references (MS p. 10); no score is assigned to this constructed response.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** (original MS-based response; not Pearson-scored). Both writers are interested in people learning new skills, but Anderson gives general advice while Willis recounts one difficult experience. Anderson uses direct address and the opening question “Have you ever wanted” to speak to the reader, then organizes her guidance under numbered headings such as “Pick A Passion” and “Learn By Doing”. Her examples range across skills, from languages to surfing. Willis instead tells the story of learning to swim in the first person, moving from childhood lessons he “hated” to his adult decision to try again. His focus stays on one skill and one learner.

The texts present the learning process differently. Anderson makes it seem manageable if the learner is interested, focused and patient: she recommends practising one skill at a time, breaking it into sub-skills and alternating research with practice. Her example of first learning to stand on a surfboard on sand makes progress sound planned and “much easier”. Willis describes the physical confusion of swimming through verbs such as “churn” and “chop” and the image of his limbs seeming to have an “independent existence”. His account therefore shows drawbacks and fear that the advice article does not dwell on. The contrast is not that Anderson says learning takes no effort—she mentions dedication—but that Willis lets readers experience how awkward the effort can feel.

Both texts nevertheless encourage the possibility of progress. Anderson’s tone is friendly and reassuring, promising that a skill can become “part of the fun” and that even limited practice can lead to progress. Willis uses self-deprecating humour, but his short statement “I do it, though” and his later sense of “euphoria” show persistence paying off. Their structures reinforce the difference: Anderson’s numbered guide offers steps in advance, whereas Willis’s chronological personal account reveals setbacks and improvement as they happen. Together, they suggest that learning takes time and practice, while showing both the usefulness of a plan and the difficulty an individual may have to overcome.

**MS alignment check:** The comparison is balanced across both texts and covers their purpose and form, direct address, range of skills versus one skill, the guide’s manageable steps versus the account’s obstacles, and their shared theme of overcoming barriers (MS pp. 11–12). The Level 5 descriptor requires varied, comprehensive comparison with balanced, discriminating references; a response considering only one text is capped at Level 2 (MS p. 12).
