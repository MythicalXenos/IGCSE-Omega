## What the January 2020 report adds

Quoted from the report’s Section B (Question 8) commentary:

- **AO1 — the second bullet is the weak spot here.** “The second bullet point ‘different types of art’ was sometimes less developed than the others with candidates tending to list the different types with no additional development.” Candidates “tended to concentrate on aspects such as learning art in school, the value of good teaching and visiting art exhibitions.” More able candidates “referred to a good range of ideas and made developed comment on all three bullets points… covered all three bullet points equally and produced clearly focused arguments.”
- **AO1 — lifting is penalised by name.** “Examiners commented that a significant number of candidates lifted information from the texts especially whole sentences. This affects the quality of the responses as candidates are expected to adapt the material and use their own words.” Less able candidates showed “lifting of large sections from the original texts without any attempt to re-work the material.”
- **AO4 — formal drift again.** Most understood the letter format, and “addresses are not necessary as it is the tone, style and register that should be appropriate for the task”, but “sometimes examiners commented that the letters were rather too formal for a letter to a friend.” The best responses “constantly reminded the reader that it was a letter to a friend by addressing their friend throughout”; the weakest “did not write in a style that resembled a letter and chose to write an article or essay.”
- **AO5 — recurring errors:** “comma splicing, missing or misused apostrophes, problems with homophones, misspelling of basic vocabulary, not capitalising ‘I’ for the personal pronoun, missing capital letters at the beginning of sentences and grammatical errors.” Better responses used “apostrophes, commas in lists and other devices”, paragraphing “for effect as could be the deliberate use of short sentences and rhetorical questions”.

**Design consequence:** this model develops the *types of art* bullet at paragraph length — explaining what each medium lets you do rather than listing it — keeps a named friend addressed throughout, and adapts the source detail into a new voice instead of quoting sentences.

## [CONSTRUCTED GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed model letter

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content and the January 2020 report guidance, using verified details from both texts. It is **not** a Pearson script, has **not** been examiner-marked, and **no score is claimed**.

> Hi Priya,
>
> You said last week that you “just don’t get” art — that it is one of those things other people understand and you nod along to. I thought that too, until about two years ago. Here is what changed my mind, and I promise there is no gallery snobbery in it.
>
> First, how you actually learn about art, because I did not learn it the way you would expect. My route was teachers. The one who mattered was a student teacher who took our class to a local exhibition when I was eight; I can still picture the birds and the fish on those walls, and I remember thinking that I had walked into another dimension. Later, a teacher let us build things out of papier-mâché — torn paper and glue, moulded into whatever shape you wanted — which was messy, slow and completely absorbing. In secondary school it was a different teacher: he painted, but he let us try whatever materials we liked, and he taught us art history, which is how I ended up in galleries on Saturdays without anyone forcing me. So: teachers, exhibitions, reading about artists, going to college, and mostly asking questions. You do not need talent to start. You need one person who says “try this” instead of “you’re doing it wrong”.
>
> Second, the types of art — and this is where people give up, because they think art means drawing a convincing apple. It does not. Painting and drawing are only the obvious two. There is design, which is everything from a poster to a phone; there are crafts, which is the whole world of making by hand; there is modelling and sculpture, where you build up a shape instead of flattening it onto paper; there is papier-mâché, which is basically junk and glue and is very forgiving if you are a beginner; and there is ceramics — clay, the wheel, glazes, the firing that turns a lump into a pot. That last one is what I ended up studying. I only found it because a teacher, stuck with me in hospital at eight, gave up on line drawings of fish and handed me clay instead, and the possibilities suddenly seemed endless. Each of these does a different job: clay is about form and patience, paint is about colour and decision, drawing is about looking properly.
>
> Third, why it matters, and this is the part I would argue with anyone about. Art is a way of saying something when words are not enough — it has been used to challenge authority, to record moments that mattered, and to let ordinary people pour out what they could not put into a sentence. It gives you freedom of expression, it develops your imagination, and it can genuinely change how someone sees the world. Practically, the creative industries are booming, so it is a real route to work. And it is not only in galleries: it is on your phone, on the bus, in the book you are reading. Picasso said, “The world does not make sense, so why should I paint pictures that do?” — and once I heard that, I stopped worrying about whether my work was “accurate” and started enjoying making it.
>
> Come to one workshop with me. It is two hours on a Thursday, it costs nothing, and if you hate it I will buy you a coffee and never mention it again. But bring an old shirt, because the clay gets everywhere.
>
> See you Thursday,
>
> Jonah

## Accepted alternatives recorded

The MS list is explicitly non-exhaustive, and the report confirms that candidates could “use their knowledge of art to provide further ideas for their letters”. Creditable alternatives not used in the model:

- **Ways to learn about art:** “by developing/growing up” and “from others (students)” — both MS-listed and untouched here. Any real local route (a family member, a YouTube tutorial, a museum’s free workshop) is also valid if tied to the bullet.
- **Types of art:** photography, printmaking, textiles, graffiti or digital art are all reasonable extensions of the MS categories; the report accepts candidates’ own knowledge.
- **Why art is important:** “you can learn new techniques” and “it can help people develop” are MS points not explicitly made in this letter. Note that the report also records that a number of candidates who chose Section C Q9 on this paper reused extract material and were limited by it — for Q8, adaptation (not quotation) is what earns AO1 credit.
- **The specific Picasso sale detail** (“Dora Maar with Cat”, $95 million, Sotheby’s, 4 May 2006) is available in Text Two but was deliberately left out to avoid the “list of lifted facts” effect the report criticises.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO1:** all three bullets are developed at paragraph length, with the *types of art* bullet given the most explanation — the exact weakness the report identifies — and with both texts used and reworded rather than quoted in bulk.
- **AO4:** named friend, contractions, direct questions, a concrete invitation and a wry closing detail; no addresses; the register never slips into an article or a leaflet.
- **AO5:** varied sentence structures, deliberate short sentences for emphasis, semicolons and dashes for control, correctly punctuated quotation and list commas, and none of the report’s listed errors.
- **What is not claimed:** no Pearson or examiner mark, no level or score. The AO5 ceiling on this grid is Level 4, 7–8.
