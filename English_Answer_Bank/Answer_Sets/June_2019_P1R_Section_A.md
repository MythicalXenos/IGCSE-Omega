### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Whittle presents social change as something young people can already achieve, rather than something reserved for adults. She calls it “a great mistake” to think otherwise and says young people are “transforming their world in ways that put adults to shame”. The emphatic opening challenges a common assumption; the comparison with adults makes young people’s capability sound not merely possible but impressive. Her direct address, “As a teacher, you can be the inspiration”, then gives the reader a constructive role: teachers should enable students rather than assume they alone must lead change.

The heading “Examples of social change are plentiful” introduces evidence for this advice. Whittle names three young people and briefly describes their actions: Grace Miner formed a group to discuss media portrayals of girls; Joshua Williams helped feed people in Miami; and seven-year-old Ryan Hreljac bought a well and continued raising money for wells in Africa. The names, ages and concrete actions make the examples specific and memorable. Calling them “inspiring” guides the teacher towards seeing students’ actions as models that can motivate others, not as exceptional cases to dismiss.

The subheadings then turn that broad message into practical guidance. “Offer them a choice and a voice” uses a balanced phrase to emphasize participation. Whittle concedes, “It is true that young people need rules and structure”, but follows with the rhetorical question “how can we expect them to emerge into adulthood fully capable of making their own decisions?” The concession makes her advice sound measured; the question invites teachers to recognize that too much control would undermine the independence they want students to develop. She recommends “Offering them small decisions” and says teachers should “actively seek their opinions”, so responsibility is presented as something that can be taught gradually.

“Use technology effectively” and “Connect real life to the classroom” broaden the advice beyond classroom discussion. The metaphor “in a bubble” warns that lessons can become detached from the problems students see around them. By contrast, bringing in “real world events” and asking students to devise solutions connects learning with action. Whittle also treats social media as a practical tool, using the Ice Bucket Challenge as a recognizable example of one person prompting a much wider campaign. The headings make the article easy to follow, while direct instructions and the suggestion “Why not, for example” keep the tone advisory rather than simply critical.

Finally, “Hold them accountable” adds follow-through to the earlier emphasis on choice. Whittle argues that students should take responsibility and see that “what they do does matter”. Her concluding summary gathers the advice into “choice, technology, real-world scenarios, and accountability”, leaving teachers with a clear set of methods. Across the article, positive examples establish that young people can act; the headings and specific recommendations show teachers how to help them do so.

**MS alignment check:** This response develops the MS’s non-exhaustive points on the positive opening/examples, direct address, subheadings, advice, rhetorical questions, the contrast between rules and independence, the “bubble” metaphor, technology and accountability (indicative content, MS p. 5). The AO2 Level 5 descriptor requires perceptive analysis with discriminating references (MS p. 6); no mark is assigned to this constructed response.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Jones’s belief is supported first by her personal involvement. The introduction identifies her with the “Diana Anti-bullying Champion Award”, and the speech then presents her own experience of working with projects. This gives her views a personal basis: she speaks as someone involved in community work, not as a distant observer. Her repetition that young people have “incredible, incredible potential” makes her confidence difficult to miss; repeating “incredible” intensifies the praise and signals how strongly she believes in them.

Jones also sets the negative assumptions about young people against evidence of what they can do. She says some people expect young people to be “destructive” and to “not contribute anything positive”, then contrasts this with the projects’ opportunity to show that young people can do positive things and take responsibility. The contrast makes the speech respond directly to the stereotype rather than merely ignoring it. Her metaphor of “shining a spotlight” on young people suggests that their constructive work deserves to be seen, helping to challenge that reputation.

The first-person voice makes the argument sound authentic, while the movement between “I”, “we” and “you” involves the audience. Jones shifts from describing what she believes to addressing young people directly: “My message to young people who want to get involved in society is: don’t be scared.” This signals a change from explanation to encouragement. Her advice draws on energetic language: “have courage”, “standing up and saying” what matters, “keep striving” and “persevere”. Even the image of “ramming your head into a brick wall” acknowledges how difficult change can feel, so her optimism does not depend on pretending that participation is effortless.

The structure builds towards reassurance. Jones repeats the idea that small actions can matter, then ends by saying that even “any change at all” can make a “massive difference to someone else’s life”. The contrast between “any change” and “massive difference” makes the impact of an individual effort sound worthwhile. The short closing commands, “So, stick with it” and “So have courage”, leave the audience with memorable instructions. Because the passage is shaped as a speech and speaks directly to its listeners, Jones’s belief becomes a personal call to action as well as a positive view of young people.

**MS alignment check:** This response develops the MS’s points on Jones’s award and personal voice, pronouns, repetition, positive language, the “spotlight” metaphor, inspirational wording, direct audience engagement and optimistic ending (indicative content, MS p. 8). The AO2 Level 5 descriptor is on MS p. 9. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers believe young people can contribute to social change, but they address different audiences and give them different roles. Text One is advice for teachers: Whittle says, “As a teacher, you can be the inspiration”, presenting adults as people who can make participation possible. Text Two is a talk that addresses young people themselves; Jones introduces “My message to young people” and tells them “don’t be scared”. The first writer concentrates on how adults can empower students, while the second encourages young people to act for themselves.

Whittle makes her case through examples of young people already changing their communities. The heading “Examples of social change are plentiful” introduces Grace Miner, Joshua Williams and seven-year-old Ryan Hreljac, whose distinct actions make the idea of youth involvement concrete. Jones also draws on real involvement, but her perspective is more personal: she tells listeners about her work with the Diana Award and Changing Faces. Her first-person account and the award mentioned at the beginning give her encouragement the feel of lived experience. Both therefore support their positive view with examples, although one surveys several students and the other speaks from the perspective of an involved young person.

The texts also differ in the kind of help they propose. Whittle gives teachers specific approaches under practical subheadings, including “Offer them a choice and a voice”, “Use technology effectively” and “Connect real life to the classroom”. She concedes that students need “rules and structure” but questions how they can learn to make decisions if they are never given any. Her advice is about creating opportunities, building confidence and helping students follow through. Jones focuses more on the barriers young people face, explaining that they may feel expected to be “destructive” or to “not contribute anything positive”. The metaphor of “shining a spotlight” presents the Diana Award as a way of making their constructive actions visible and challenging those assumptions. Thus Whittle emphasizes practical teaching methods, whereas Jones stresses recognition, reputation and young people’s confidence.

Their forms and tones reinforce those purposes. Whittle’s article is organized with subheadings and moves from examples to advice and a final summary. Its direct address and rhetorical questions make it engaging, but the guidance remains structured and explanatory. Jones’s speech uses first-person pronouns, repetition—“incredible, incredible”—and direct encouragement. The closing commands “So, stick with it” and “So have courage” sound personal and immediate. Whittle’s final summary gathers the teacher’s responsibilities into choice, technology, real-world scenarios and accountability; Jones ends by assuring young people that even “any change at all” can make a “massive difference”.

Overall, the writers share an optimistic belief that young people’s actions matter, but Whittle explains how teachers can develop that capacity, while Jones argues directly to young people that they already have the power and courage to use it. Their different audiences lead them to present social change as both an educational process and an opportunity for young people to challenge how society sees them.

**MS alignment check:** This comparison is sustained across both texts, balances references and explores audience, ideas, tone, structure and language. It develops the MS’s comparison points (Text One’s teacher audience and specific advice; Text Two’s young audience and supportive speech; both writers’ positive support for youth involvement) and the AO3 Level 5 criteria on MS p. 11. It remains a constructed, unmarked answer.
