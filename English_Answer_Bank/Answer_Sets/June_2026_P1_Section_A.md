### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** The writer presents his handwriting as a skill he once valued but has gradually lost as typing took over. The opening “I used to love writing with a pen—genuinely loved it” sounds sincere, while “used to” immediately places that affection in the past. Calling the nib’s sound “music to my ears” makes handwriting a sensory pleasure. The three-part description “clean, legible, and distinctly mine” establishes a positive starting point: the writing was readable, controlled and personal.

The short transition “Everything changed in January 2012” marks the moment technology begins to alter that ability. The computer is described through a list of faults—“old, bulky”, “faulty”, with a “dead battery”—yet the writer calls it “a marvel”. This contrast conveys how exciting the new technology seemed despite its defects. The concise claim that the “keyboard quickly replaced the pen” then makes the change sound rapid. Years later, the earlier “neat lines and carefully curved letters” have become an “unrecognizable mess—a chaotic scrawl”. The opposing descriptions make the deterioration unmistakable, while “visual representation of chaos” suggests that the page now displays his loss of control.

When the writer recounts the meeting, self-mockery makes the failure vivid but comic. The list “grown man, educated, supposedly intelligent” builds an expectation of competence before the admission that he is “completely unable to write legibly” overturns it. He calls himself “an absolute clown”, and the hyperbole “a crime committed in HD, with Dolby Surround Sound” turns poor handwriting into an absurdly dramatic spectacle. The mother-duck image—words waddling and tap-dancing through ink—extends the humour and gives the reader a clear picture of untidy marks. The later pattern “Sentences refused to align; some stretched diagonally, others spiraled downward” gives the writing movement of its own; the skydiving simile makes it seem uncontrolled rather than merely unattractive.

The observer’s response raises the personal cost of the episode. He “froze”, with “disbelief etched” on his face, then looks at the writer “like I was some strange species that had escaped from a lab”. His eyes are “narrowed, burning with colorful tongues of flame”, and his voice “thundered like a rogue storm”; the verbs in “dropped his glasses, slammed the table, and demanded” make his anger seem sudden and forceful. These exaggerated reactions make the narrator seem alien because of a basic skill he thought he possessed. The direct questions “Did you go to school?” and “Then why can’t you write?” expose the embarrassment more plainly than the comic comparisons do. Later, being seen as an “illiterate fool” and calling the event “public humiliation” show that the joking tone does not remove the sting.

The ending turns the failure into a Darwin joke: the writer imagines himself as a “human who once wrote neatly but now survives by typing”. That comparison suggests that repeated use of keyboards has displaced the practice handwriting needs. His resolve—“I need to practice writing again”—is direct, but the final irony is that he is typing the story on his phone, his “true comfort zone”. “Evolution really is no joke” closes with humour while preserving the underlying concern: a once-loved, legible skill can fade when technology makes handwriting unnecessary.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** The writer presents handwriting as a way to preserve a person’s identity, creativity and memories, not just a method of putting words on paper. She begins with her mother’s old letter. Although its paper is “thin and ratty”, the handwriting and “turn of phrase” are “unforgettably hers”. The contrast between the worn paper and the lasting impression makes the letter seem emotionally durable. Its “wondrous observations” “immediately” transport the writer to “another place and another time”, showing how a handwritten object can revive a person’s voice and experience. Her wish to read it “again and again and again” stresses its continuing value.

The letter is contrasted with an email that “might have been lost” as laptops and operating systems change. Instead, she “added it to a box in the cupboard”. This practical act of keeping it suggests that paper can become a lasting record. The explanation “Knowing that the person held a pen to write the words” focuses on the physical act behind the message; the verb “elevates” explicitly presents that contact as making correspondence more valuable. Handwriting is also individual: she remembers her mother’s distinctive rounded “N” and recalls trying to forge her signature. The failed imitation suggests that handwriting carries a personal identity that cannot easily be reproduced.

The writer then moves from family memory to changing habits in schools. The alliteration in “rarely receive” draws attention to how uncommon letters have become in her life. She admits that she now writes “almost entirely” on a laptop and that even a shopping list is often typed on her phone. This candid detail shows that she is part of the change she worries about. The contrast between her own school years—when pupils practised until they earned a pen licence—and children now doing much of their learning on keyboards makes the shift seem broad, not just personal. She acknowledges that keyboards make correction easy and that spelling and grammar apps can help, but the conjunction “but” turns the paragraph toward her concern: “I fear we may be losing something.” “Fear” signals genuine unease, while “may” keeps the claim measured rather than absolute.

The year 6 boy’s writing gives the argument a concrete example. The writer can read it easily, but what matters most is that she can see his crossings-out and corrections, evidence of thought developing on the page. His sentences are not in the “dictated form” of an app; they are “an expression of his own”. The sequence “Imperfect, incomplete, rambling and sometimes lyrical” accepts qualities that a grammar tool might remove, then ends on the more positive “lyrical”. This contrast suggests that handwriting can make room for an individual creative voice, not just polished correctness.

The repeated opening “We know” builds a cumulative, persuasive case: handwriting aids creativity, improves memory, helps people process information deeply and represents personality. The closing reflection on how “we will archive our lives” carries those ideas into the future. Emails have not been kept, but handwritten notes, love declarations and children’s cards are “treasured”; that verb makes their emotional value explicit. The list moves through stages of the writer’s life, and the phrase “trace my history through the handwriting of others” presents old writing as a personal archive. She values handwriting for physical connection, learning, individual expression and the recovery of memories, even while recognizing why people choose keyboards.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers present handwriting as important while recognizing that keyboards have changed how people write. However, Text One is a humorous first-person narrative of one man’s deteriorating handwriting, whereas Text Two is a reflective, persuasive article that moves from the writer’s family memories and school workshops to wider claims about education and memory. The first centers on one individual’s embarrassment; the second draws on pupils and keepsakes to argue for broader benefits. Their different forms shape what each writer wants the reader to notice.

Both texts link handwriting with personal identity. In Text One, the writer remembers that his work was “clean, legible, and distinctly mine”; the last phrase suggests that his handwriting expressed something individual. Text Two makes a similar point through the mother’s “unforgettably hers” writing and the writer’s memory of her distinctive rounded “N”. Yet the emphasis differs: Text One looks back at an ability the narrator has lost, while Text Two shows how a handwritten letter can preserve her mother’s distinctive voice across time.

Both writers trace that change to technology. Text One marks a turning point when the “keyboard quickly replaced the pen”, then contrasts “neat lines” with an “unrecognizable mess”. The change is told as a personal decline in skill. Text Two describes a wider shift: the writer now types “almost entirely” on a laptop, children do much of their learning on computers, and she rarely receives letters. She openly acknowledges that keyboards make editing easier and grammar apps can help. Text One also reveals the narrator’s dependence on technology—he says the only thing he writes by hand is his signature and admits that his phone is his “true comfort zone”. Neither writer pretends that keyboards are going to disappear, but Text Two is more explicit about their practical advantages.

The consequences each writer stresses are different. Text One turns poor handwriting into a public embarrassment. The hyperbole “a crime committed in HD” and the mother-duck simile make the scrawl comic, while the observer’s question, “Then why can’t you write?”, shows how quickly illegibility can undermine confidence and how others judge him. His resolution, “I need to practice writing again”, focuses on recovering a practical skill. Text Two is less concerned with legibility alone. The year 6 boy’s neat work matters, but the writer is especially interested in being able to see where he has crossed things out and how his writing became “an expression of his own”. The description “Imperfect, incomplete, rambling and sometimes lyrical” values the creative process and personal voice, even when the result is not polished.

The writers’ structures and tones reinforce this contrast. Text One moves chronologically from childhood enthusiasm to a humiliating meeting, then ends with a comic comparison to Darwin and the irony that the narrator is typing the story on his phone. The humour makes his warning self-deprecating rather than a simple attack on technology. Text Two starts with a specific, treasured letter, broadens to school practice and the repeated assertion “We know”, then returns to family keepsakes. That movement from personal memory to shared benefits and back to a life archive makes the argument more reflective and persuasive. The inclusive “we” invites readers to consider their own habits.

Ultimately, both writers suggest that handwriting should not be dismissed: it affects how people communicate and can carry a distinctive personal voice. Text One presents its loss as an individual problem of legibility, confidence and practice; Text Two sees a wider risk to creativity, memory and the way people keep their histories. One ends laughing at his dependence on typing, while the other warns that a convenient digital habit may cause valuable forms of expression and remembrance to disappear.
