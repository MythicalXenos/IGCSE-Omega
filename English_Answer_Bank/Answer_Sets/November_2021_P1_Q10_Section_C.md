## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed story

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 17) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 18–19), with method from this paper's own report. **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **Seventy rupees**
>
> The flour came at six, and by seven my father knew he had been robbed by a man he had shaken hands with for nineteen years.
>
> The sack had been the same sack, the weight was right, but the price had gone up by a third and the money in the tin had not. He stood behind the counter doing arithmetic on the back of a delivery slip, and I stood at the oven with the paddle in my hands, and neither of us said anything, because there was a queue.
>
> The queue is the thing I remember. Eleven people, most of them regulars, most of them knowing that the bread would be a little smaller this week. Mrs Rahman was third in line with her purse open already. Mr Iqbal was fifth, and he was the one I was watching, because Mr Iqbal had been buying four loaves on credit since his son's accident in June, and my mother had said what everybody said, which was *poor man*, and my father had said nothing at all.
>
> At about half past seven my father put the pencil down. He did not make a speech. He picked up the smaller loaves, the ones he had been shaping since five, and he slid the tray onto the counter, and then he took the little card out of the drawer — the card we use for wedding orders, with the shop's name at the top — and he wrote on it in his awful capitals: TODAY, FOUR FOR THE PRICE OF THREE.
>
> I said, *Baba, we can't.*
>
> He said, *We can for today.*
>
> I said, *Mr Iqbal takes four.*
>
> He looked at me then, properly, the way he looks at dough when it has not proved. He said, *Then today he pays for three, and next week he pays for three, and in a month he has paid for the whole thing or he has not, and we will know.*
>
> That was the whole argument. It took eleven seconds. Then he went back to the oven, and Mrs Rahman bought her two, and Mr Iqbal came to the counter and read the card twice and said nothing and counted out three from a purse that had been holding four, and my father put the fourth loaf in the bag anyway, and said, *For the boy.*
>
> I have thought about it for years, mostly because it was not generous in the way that people are generous in stories. He had done the sums. He knew exactly what a month of three-for-four would cost him, and he did it with his eyes open, and he never mentioned it again — not when Mr Iqbal paid in full, not even at the wedding.
>
> And that was the choice: not whether to be kind, which was easy, but how much kindness he could actually afford, and then to spend precisely that.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Interpretations from the report:** a moral dilemma; a life-or-death decision; education, career, marriage or university; “doing the right thing”.
- **Structures that satisfy “the choice unfolding”:** a decision taken in stages across one scene (as here); two characters who disagree and a third who forces the issue; a choice whose cost is revealed after it is made.
- **Reveal options:** title confirmed in the final clause while the decision is already visible; an explicit naming mid-story; a closing line that re-reads the earlier detail.
- **What to avoid:** an afterthought title line (“…and that was my choice”) dangling off an unrelated plot; gore; life-support clichés; a film plot; a plot spanning years; a saintly protagonist with no cost.
