## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed discursive answer

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 17) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 18–19), with method taken from this paper's own report (pp. 42–45). **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **Group work, and the myth of the lone genius**
>
> Every school puts a poster on the wall of somebody working alone. Einstein at a desk, a writer in a shed, a scientist with one light on at three in the morning. Nobody puts up a poster of a committee. And yet almost everything those people are famous for was argued into shape by somebody else first — a colleague, an editor, a rival, a friend who said the second sentence was rubbish.
>
> Still, I do not quite agree with the statement, because it is built on a false choice. Working in a group and working on your own are not two opinions about the same activity; they are two tools, and the sensible question is which one the job needs.
>
> Take the strongest case for the group, because it is strong. A group pools what nobody individually has: the person who can draw, the person who can count, the person who will actually ring the charity and ask. Work gets shared, and a shared workload is not a small mercy in a week with three deadlines in it. A group also shames you into finishing things; it is much harder to abandon a task when four people are expecting it at nine o'clock.
>
> But I have sat in enough groups to know the other side. A group can become a stage for one loud person and an audience for everybody else, and the quietest member, who had the best idea, will often not say it twice. Group work also has a way of absorbing time without producing anything: forty minutes of agreeing how to begin. And there is a particular kind of tiredness that comes from being pleasant in a room for two hours when you could have finished the thing in one.
>
> My own experience splits cleanly down the middle. The best mark I have had came from four of us on a fund-raising stall: one did the posters, one did the money, one did the talking, and I did the thing nobody wanted, which was the spreadsheet. The best piece of writing I have done came from a Sunday afternoon with the door shut. Neither would have worked the other way round.
>
> So the statement is half right, and half right is not enough for a preference. Groups are better for problems that need more than one kind of knowledge, and for anything that requires nerve. Solitude is better for anything that requires a single voice, and for the first messy hour of any idea. What a person actually needs is not a preference but a sense of which is which — and the honesty to admit that the answer changes as the task does.
>

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Stance:** agree (the majority), disagree, or reject the dichotomy (as here). All are inside the MS, which explicitly allows positive discussion of solo work.
- **Groups the MS names:** school councils, sports teams, action or charity groups, fund-raising committees; large or small groups.
- **Advantages to use:** cooperation, pooling ideas and expertise, shared workload, increased motivation.
- **Disadvantages to use:** strong characters dominate, conflict or disagreement, individuals may work harder or be more creative alone.
- **What to avoid:** the four stock phrases named in the report; opening with a quotation; a two-sided list with no verdict; a rant that ignores the other side; a narrative about one group project stretched to 400 words.
