## [CONSTRUCTED, 30/30-TARGET] Constructed talk to peers

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content (MS p. 16), the verified band descriptors (pp. 17–19) and the P1R report's Q8 commentary. Aligned with the characteristics of the **verified 30/30 script at printed pp. 37–42**, which was not readable. **Not** a Pearson script, **not** examiner-marked, **no score claimed.**

> **THE FRIEND AUDIT**
>
> Right, hands up if you have a best friend. Keep your hand up if that person is in this room.
>
> Most of you have just put your hands down, and that is the whole point of what I want to say. The person you would ring at two in the morning is probably not sitting next to you. In my case she is fifty-one, she is my next-door neighbour's mother, and she has never once used the phrase “back in my day”.
>
> So this is a talk about friends — not friendship in the abstract, the greeting-card version, but the actual people. Because most of us have more of them than we think, and we sort them into a league table that they never agreed to.
>
> **The types of friends we actually have**
>
> Start with the obvious one: the friend you have had since you were six. You learned to ride a bike together, badly, and one of you still cannot do it without hands. You have nothing in common any more except eleven years of shared history, and that turns out to be enough.
>
> Then the friends who are nothing like you. In my case that is Yusuf, who is a different religion, goes to a different school, and thinks my taste in music is a medical condition. We met building a go-kart out of a pallet and two broken wheels, and we have been arguing about engineering ever since.
>
> Then there are the ones who are older. Not teachers — actual adults who chose to spend time with you. My neighbour's mother taught me to make bread and told me, without a flicker of pity, that the thing I was panicking about would be funny in a year. She was right.
>
> And the ones from somewhere else: a cousin in another country you speak to in three-minute bursts, a friend from a summer job, the person you met on a course and now text about nothing. Different areas, different countries, different lives. They do not fit together. That is the point.
>
> **What we actually do with them**
>
> Look at what friendship consists of, in practice. We make things — cakes, go-karts, terrible art. We play games, and some of us take them far too seriously. We learn skills off each other: how to fix a puncture, how to do a magic trick, how to make bread that is not a brick.
>
> We go places. We read the same book and argue about the ending. We tell stories, some of which are even true. We turn up when something has gone wrong and sit there saying nothing useful, which is often the most useful thing available.
>
> And we do the small, unglamorous things. We bring presents that nobody asked for. We make people feel welcome in a room they have never been in before. We spend time together doing absolutely nothing of consequence, and it turns out that is not nothing — it is the whole thing.
>
> **Why having different friends improves your life**
>
> Here is where it matters, and here is the bullet point most people forget.
>
> You can work together. Revision with someone who thinks differently is not the same as revision alone; you get their brain as well as yours. You have someone to be with, which sounds small until you have spent a Saturday without it. They look after you — and you look after them, which is the part that changes you.
>
> You learn from them. I have learned to cook from a seventy-year-old, to argue properly from a boy with a go-kart, and to listen from a friend who does not say much. None of that was on a syllabus.
>
> They sort out problems. Not by fixing them — by being the person you say the problem out loud to, at which point it shrinks to its actual size. They lighten the mood when the mood needs lightening, and they laugh with you rather than at you, which is a distinction worth defending. And underneath all of it, they care about you. That is not a small thing to be able to say about more than one person.
>
> Our own survey of Year 11 — forty-one of us, in the common room, one lunchtime — found that students who named three or more distinct groups of friends were twice as likely to say they had someone to talk to when things went badly. Small sample, one school, no professor involved. But it matches what I think you already know.
>
> **One last thing**
>
> So: hands up if you have a best friend. Now look around. The people with their hands up are not the lucky ones and the rest of you are not the unlucky ones — because a life is not built out of one perfect person. It is built out of a bike-riding friend, an arguing friend, an older friend who feeds you, and a friend in another country who replies in three-minute bursts.
>
> Go home tonight and count yours. I think you will find you have more than you thought, and that at least one of them has not heard from you in a while. Tell them. That is all I am asking.
>
> Thank you.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Types:** the MS list is explicitly open — religion, background, education, age, lifestyle, area or country. Online friends, friends from sport or work, siblings, cousins, and “the friend you only see at one activity” are all valid; so is a talk that argues there are only two or three real types.
- **Things:** the MS allows almost any shared activity — creating, competing, gaming, travelling, reading, helping, giving, hosting, “having fun”, or simply “spending time together”. A candidate may argue that the best thing friends do is nothing at all.
- **Improvement:** the MS's eight points can be argued singly or combined. A candidate may also take a more critical line — that some friendships are not improving and that learning to leave one is a skill — provided the bullet is still addressed.
- **Unused Text One material:** the “history isn't easy to overcome… neither is religion” idea of a friendship crossing class and ethnicity; the carved pomegranate tree and “the sultans of Kabul”; the weekly ten-Afghani allowance spent on warm cola and rosewater ice cream; Hassan's illiteracy and the narrator reading to him; the narrator's own quiet admission that he stopped reading riddles when Hassan proved better at them.
- **Unused Text Two material:** the “friendships are the real love stories of our lives” framing; the pyjamas-and-doorstep lockout; the six-hour drive to Santa Barbara and the car-sick toddler; “I no longer believe you can search for new friends”; the spare set of keys as the final proof of trust.
- **Register:** warm, funny, self-aware and equal-to-equal. The report's warning is against sounding **patronising** and against letting the talk collapse into an article.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO1:** all three bullets are developed with multiple MS points and text-derived detail, the third bullet is the longest and most argued section, both texts are reworked into the speaker's own examples, and no phrase is borrowed.
- **AO4:** unmistakably a talk — direct address, hands-up interaction, rhetorical questions, an anecdote, a joke at the speaker's expense, a statistic with a caveat, and a closing return to the opening image. The register is sustained and never patronising.
- **AO5:** controlled punctuation range (colons, semicolons, dashes, parentheses, a correctly punctuated list), deliberate fragments for rhythm, consistent tense, and paragraphing that follows the three bullets.
- **Not claimed:** this is not the 30/30 script from report pp. 37–42. That script exists and was examined; it was not readable through any accessible route, so no wording from it is reproduced and no mark is claimed for this model.
