## [CONSTRUCTED GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed website contribution

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content and the November 2020 report guidance, using verified details from both texts. It is **not** a Pearson script, has **not** been examiner-marked, and **no score is claimed**.

> **Contribution: “Weather doesn’t just happen to us — it redraws the place we live in”**
>
> I want to argue one thing on this page: extreme weather is not only something we shelter from. In the space of a day it can rewrite a landscape and reorganise a whole community’s day, and the two effects are usually discussed separately. They should not be.
>
> Take the types first, because they are not interchangeable. Thunder and lightning are the spectacular ones — the kind that keep you indoors as a child, counting the gap between flash and crash. Thunderstorms bring wind and rain together and do the real damage. Monsoon rain is a different animal again: not an event but a season, arriving “from nowhere” at first and then settling in for weeks. Wind on its own can strip a roof; rain on its own can drown a road. When people say “bad weather”, they are usually lumping four quite different things into one word.
>
> The landscape is where you see the difference most clearly. Lightning starts fires — I have read an account of a strike that set a hillside alight at one in the morning, put out quickly, but only just. Wind and water take property apart: docks disconnected, boats lifted onto the shore, jet-skis deposited where no jet-skis belong. And then there is the slower transformation, the one that never makes a photograph. Paths disintegrate into mud. The yard outside a house becomes, in the writer Katy Gardner’s phrase, “a treacherous pit of the most slippery mud imaginable”. Roads effectively become rivers, and fields become swamps that will still be swamps next week. A landscape can be changed so completely that you need a boat to recognise it.
>
> The effect on people is where I think we underestimate weather most. It frightens — that is the obvious one, and it is real. But it also teaches, and it fascinates: one contributor to this subject describes a childhood fear of thunder that turned into a degree in meteorology, because a school weather project made the thing she was scared of interesting. That is worth sitting with. Weather can decide what someone does with their life.
>
> It also decides what everyone does with their week. When the rains are at their worst, work thins out to almost nothing — the labouring, the fishing, the trading that a village runs on. Money for food becomes harder to obtain, and hunger and debt follow. Movement stops: tracks flood, motorbikes and rickshaws vanish from the roads, and social life narrows to whoever is within reach of a boat. Getting to a latrine becomes an expedition. Ordinary tasks turn into physical ordeals, and people get ill from the water.
>
> But here is the part I would ask readers to notice: the same weather that isolates people also makes them extraordinarily capable. Everyone else walks confidently through the mud that defeats a visitor. Children go out before dawn with spears and pots and come back with fish from flooded fields. Neighbours shout and pick leeches off each other’s ankles. Communities develop a working knowledge of water that no textbook gives them.
>
> So, if you are studying weather this term, do not stop at the diagram of a cumulonimbus. Ask what the storm did to the road, the field, the fishing, the family. If you have your own example — a flood, a heatwave, a night of lightning — post it below; this page is better with real places in it.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

The MS list is explicitly non-exhaustive, and the report allows candidates to extend and personalise the material.

- **Types of weather:** hurricanes, tornadoes, blizzards, heatwaves or droughts are all valid extensions of the MS categories; so is a single developed local example, provided the other bullets still get real treatment.
- **Effects on the landscape:** “the landscape can be changed unrecognisably” is the MS umbrella point the model distributes across mud, floods and coastal damage; “damage to infrastructure” could carry a paragraph of its own.
- **Effects on people:** “can see amazing events” and “can be thrilling” are MS points this contribution only gestures at (through the “spectacular” framing), so a candidate who writes about the thrill of a storm rather than its cost is fully within the mark scheme. “Community spirit” is used; “makes people clever at coping” is used in ¶6. The personal-experience route is also open and is exactly what the report praises as “extending and personalising”.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO1:** all three bullets are separate, developed sections of roughly equal weight; both texts are used and reworded rather than quoted in bulk (with one short, attributed quotation); the contribution keeps to weather and never slides into climate change.
- **AO4:** the form is a contribution, not an essay — a title, an argument stated in the first paragraph, signposted sections, “I want to argue”, and a closing invitation to readers. That combination is precisely the report’s description of effective contributions (“persuasive and assertive”, features used “in the structure, tone and purpose”) and avoids its named failure (“more essay-like and formal”).
- **AO5:** tight paragraphing with one idea each, a wide punctuation range (dashes, semicolons, a colon, a correctly punctuated quotation), no missing articles, no comma splicing, and full capitalisation.
- **What is not claimed:** no Pearson or examiner mark, no level or score. The AO5 ceiling on this grid is Level 4, 7–8.
