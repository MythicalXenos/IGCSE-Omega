## What this paper's own report says about Q10

- “**Question 10 was the most popular question.**”
- **Interpretations listed:** challenges, holidays, bullying, sick parents/relatives/friends, education results, selection for a top football or sports team; messages “came in bottles, letters and painted on doors, written on beaches and hoardings, **signed and anonymous**”.
- **The pattern, in the report's own words:** “if there was a common denominator, it was that **the message was nearly always bad news for someone**.”
- **The late reveal, judged precisely:** “Often candidates chose to reveal the message at the end of their writing and, whilst in some cases **this appeared to be a deliberate strategy, examiners commented that sometimes it seemed to be an afterthought**.” — the same distinction, in a different metaphor, that the November 2023 report makes about Q11 inspiration.
- **Warnings:** “over-ambitious, producing extremely long responses with complicated plot-lines”; “a number of unnecessarily gruesome and gory stories… influenced by themes on contemporary television, films and computer games. These unpleasant plots sometimes struggled to maintain focus on the title”; and endings “premised on an impossible conclusion: ‘… and then I died’ or endings with it all being a dream.”
- **More successful:** “fewer characters which were well-developed and a well-thought-out plot… **The best responses were tightly plotted and covered a limited timescale.**”
- **Less successful:** “over-complicated or muddled storylines and weak endings that were not closely related to the events that had unfolded… Sometimes the message was added at the end of the story with little sign-posting, making the response to the title tenuous.”

**Consequences:** keep the cast to two, keep the clock to one afternoon, make the message a **physical object that is present from the first paragraph and repeatedly deferred**, and give it **good news** — the opposite of the “nearly always bad news” default this report names, which also honours the November 2023 Q10 lesson that a positive outcome is rare and therefore useful.

## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed story

> **The Message**
>
> The letter said the plot had to be clear by Saturday, which gave us one afternoon and a wheelbarrow, and Uncle Danny had already decided that everything in the shed was rubbish before he had opened the door.
>
> It smelled of creosote and wet earth and, underneath that, of my grandfather, who had been dead since March. There were forty-one seed tins, a kettle with no handle, a fork whose shaft had been mended with tape and wire, and a biscuit tin of receipts going back to 1988. Danny worked fast and cheerfully, saying *tip, tip, tip* as though the word itself were a tool.
>
> The envelope was on the shelf above the window, standing upright against a jar, and it had my grandfather's handwriting on it: FOR WHOEVER TAKES THIS ON — READ THIS BEFORE YOU DIG. The ink had gone the colour of tea. I put it in my coat pocket because my hands were full, and then I forgot it for two hours, which is the kind of thing you only recognise as a mistake afterwards.
>
> We cleared the glass, we stacked the canes, we argued about the water butt. At half past four Danny looked at the empty half of the shed and said we were nearly done, and I said, *nearly*, and felt the corner of the envelope against my leg.
>
> So I opened it in the doorway, in the last of the light, while Danny hosed the concrete. One sheet, both sides, in that small forward-leaning hand. It was not a letter. There was no *I am proud of you*, no *look after your mother*, nothing that a person says when they know they are being read after the fact. It was a list.
>
> *The pipe runs from the tap to the third bed, not the second — do not dig there. The apple on the left is a graft and will fruit this year; the one on the right never will. The robin has been here longer than you. Feed him.*
>
> Danny read it over my shoulder with the hose still running, and then he laughed, once, in a way that was not quite a laugh, and said, *typical*, and went back to the concrete.
>
> I folded it along its own creases and put it in my pocket, and I have it still. He had written it months before he died, knowing exactly who would be standing in that doorway with muddy hands, and he had not wasted a single word on goodbye. That was the message: not that he was going, but that the ground would still need digging, and that the robin would still need feeding, and that somebody — anybody — would have to know where the pipe was.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Media and vehicles (report's list):** text, social media post, letter, bottle, door, beach, hoarding, anonymous note — plus a recorded message, a will, a code, a sign.
- **Themes (report's list):** challenge, holiday, bullying, illness, exam results, team selection — all permitted; the report only warns when the plot serves the gore or the twist rather than the title.
- **Structures that fit the "deliberate strategy" test:** object present early and deferred (as here); message quoted in fragments before it is understood; two characters who disagree about whether to open it.
- **What to avoid:** an afterthought message in the final three lines; a long cast; a timescale of years; murder, kidnapping, gore; “and then I died”; “it was all a dream”; message left unexplained.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO4:** one place, one afternoon, two developed characters, and a message that is *in the reader's hands* from the second paragraph, so the ending is a fulfilment rather than a trick.
- **AO5:** control of the semicolon and the dash inside the quoted list, varied sentence length, and a closing sentence that carries the title's meaning without repeating the word.
