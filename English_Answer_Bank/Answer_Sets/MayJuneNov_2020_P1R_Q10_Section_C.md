## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed story

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 15) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 16–17), with method from this paper's own report. **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **Time for a Change**
>
> The letter was on the table when I got in, and my mother had put it under the sugar bowl so that it would look casual, which is how I knew it was not casual at all.
>
> She was at the sink with her back to me and the radio on, and she said, without turning round, *I have handed in my notice.*
>
> I said, *Notice for what?*
>
> She said, *The shop. Twelve years. I have handed in my notice and I start at the college in September.*
>
> My mother has worked at the fabric shop on the corner since I was six. She can tell you the price of a metre of anything by touching it, and she can tell you whether a customer will buy before they have said a word. It is the only job she has ever had, and I had assumed, without ever thinking about it, that it was the only job she would ever have.
>
> I said the wrong thing. I said, *But you're good at it.*
>
> She turned round then, and dried her hands on the towel, and looked at me for a moment. She said, *Being good at something is not a reason to do it for ever.* And then, because she is my mother, she added, *Also, I am going to be a nurse, and I am going to be bad at it for a while, and you will have to be all right about that.*
>
> We ate, and she told me the details in the way she does — the course, the hours, the money, which was less, the bus, which was two, the fact that she would be in a classroom with people my age and would need to buy a bag. She talked about the bag twice. I think the bag was the part she was frightened of.
>
> I asked her whether she was scared, and she said, *Of course I'm scared*, and got up to put the plates in the sink, and that was the end of the conversation for about ten minutes, until she said, from the sink, *Your father would have said I was mad.*
>
> I said, *He'd have said you were mad and then he'd have bought you the bag.*
>
> She laughed at that, properly, with her hands in the water, and something in the room changed shape. I have thought about it since and I think that was the moment I understood that she had been waiting for permission from somebody who was no longer there, and had decided to give it to herself.
>
> She qualified four years later. She was fifty-one, and she was not the best nurse on the ward for the first two, and she says she was the best in the third. The bag is still in the cupboard under the stairs, and the college letter is still under the sugar bowl, and it has been on that table long enough now to have gone soft at the folds — which is, I suppose, the whole point: that after a certain number of years, the only sensible thing to do with a life is to change it.
