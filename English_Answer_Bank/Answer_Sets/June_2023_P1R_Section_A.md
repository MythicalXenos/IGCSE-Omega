### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Renwick first presents Francis’s early work experience as something he wanted to escape. The image of a “future he would rather avoid”, followed by “12-hour days”, “hard work and sweat” and a “permanently aching back”, encourages readers to understand why he rejected that path. The later contrast with a “£1billion-plus valuation” makes the scale of his change striking. Francis’s direct quotations explain his own motives and make the profile feel grounded in his account rather than in the writer’s speculation.

The time markers move the reader from Francis at 14 to Gymshark’s founding and later growth. Lists and figures show the company’s expansion, while the tricolon “founder, majority shareholder and chief marketing officer” compresses several achievements into one phrase. His ambition is explicit in “By hook or by crook” and the alliterative aim to be “biggest and best”. The campus is described as a “branded theme park in glistening black and chrome”, making the company seem modern and impressive; the wall message “Be a Pioneer” also fits the idea of building something new.

The writer then complicates a simple billionaire-success image. The phrase “Resolutely down-to-earth” introduces Francis’s ordinary habits: ready meals, weekends with his parents, his dog and football. The writer’s humorous aside about where Francis gets his hair cut reminds us of his wealth, while the unexpectedly cheap domain name and “totally random decision” make the company’s origin seem less carefully planned than its later success. Overall, Renwick presents Francis as ambitious and highly successful, but still recognisably ordinary in parts of his personal life.

**MS alignment check:** The response uses the negative opening, direct speech, time markers, achievement list, ambition, campus description, personal details, humour and surprising domain-name answer identified in the indicative content (MS p. 6). The Level 5 descriptor requires perceptive analysis and discriminating references (MS p. 7); not every indicative point is required.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Saner presents her work as challenging but rewarding. In the opening, “striving” and “forced” suggest effort and pressure, but she balances them with “enjoyed”, “a great feeling” and “satisfying”. The reassurance “which everyone has” makes difficulty sound normal rather than a reason to avoid the sector. Her first-person voice keeps these claims personal.

She also challenges the idea that a technology career requires someone to have always been a technical expert. She recalls being “fascinated” by technology as a child, but says she did not begin her career in the sector and initially lacked technical knowledge. The three-part advice that experiences “will shape you, build your skills and help your career” presents transferable experience as valuable. Her list “innovation, creativity and problem solving” makes IT sound varied; examples such as “cyber security, robotics and Artificial Intelligence” make the range of fields concrete. The emphasis that technology is about “people” and making daily life “easier, better and safer” counters a narrow image of the work.

Saner names the barriers women face in a list—“stereotypes, education, confidence and lack of role models”—so the problem appears broad and embedded, not simply an individual failure. She then offers solutions: build confidence and belonging, increase female leaders and teachers, improve the syllabus and offer flexibility. The final imperatives, “Be confident” and “go after it”, turn the interview into direct encouragement. Her acknowledgment that the environment can be challenging, alongside “change is well under way”, keeps the optimism from denying the obstacles.

**MS alignment check:** The response covers challenge and reward, reassurance, her childhood interest, transferable skills, the variety and human purpose of IT, the barriers list, proposed solutions and the imperative ending (MS pp. 9–10). The Level 5 descriptor requires perceptive analysis and discriminating references (MS p. 10); the indicative points are examples, not a checklist.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both texts present people succeeding in fields linked to technology, but they have different purposes. Text One is a third-person article telling the story of one businessman; Text Two is a first-person interview that encourages more women to consider technology careers. Renwick begins with Francis’s unpleasant work experience and later contrasts it with Gymshark’s “£1billion-plus valuation”. Saner, by contrast, begins with the personal satisfaction she gets from helping people “achieve their full potential”. One text builds a single success story; the other argues that success should be open to more people.

The texts also define success differently. Text One emphasizes business growth through figures such as “£30,000 a day” and “£260 million”, direct quotations about building a major brand, and the description of a modern campus. Yet Francis is also shown as down-to-earth, so the profile balances financial achievement with an ordinary personal life. Text Two focuses less on money and more on personal and social benefits: teamwork, overcoming barriers and making people’s daily lives “easier, better and safer”. Its list of barriers—stereotypes, education, confidence and role models—shows why women may be excluded, while the solutions explain how more people could enter the sector.

Their forms reinforce these aims. Renwick’s article uses a chronological profile and Francis’s direct speech to inform readers about his rise. Saner’s interview uses first-person explanations and direct advice, ending with “Be confident” and “go after it”. Both provide background and show technology as important, but Text One reports what one entrepreneur achieved, whereas Text Two uses one woman’s experience to persuade readers that others can belong and succeed too.

**MS alignment check:** The comparison is sustained across form, purpose, gender, individual success versus wider access, financial versus personal benefits, and both texts’ links to technology. Evidence is drawn from both texts (MS pp. 11–12). The Level 5 descriptor requires a varied, comprehensive comparison with balanced, discriminating references; a response considering only one text is capped at Level 2 (MS p. 12).
