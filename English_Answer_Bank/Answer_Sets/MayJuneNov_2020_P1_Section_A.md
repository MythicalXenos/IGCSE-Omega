### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Keyes begins with an apparently secure plan: she is going to spend Christmas with her sister, and her passport should be in the “‘official things’ drawer”. The sudden “there it was – gone!” overturns that expectation; the exclamation makes the discovery feel immediate, while the short follow-up, “Except it couldn’t be”, captures her disbelief. She first “rummaged” through bills, then “systematically” checked each item; the contrast suggests an effort to impose order, but the search still ends with “nothing”. The emphatic capitals in “Was Not There” mark the point when she can no longer talk herself out of the truth.

The account then makes panic physical. Her “mouth went a little dry”, her “heart-rate increased”, and later she has “sweaty hands”. The movement from “rummaged” to “tear my room apart” and “launched an attack” makes the search sound increasingly forceful and uncontrolled; “whirling” carries that frantic motion into the next morning. At the same time, Keyes uses self-mocking humour—the mother’s saying about not finding “the water in the river”, the abandoned sweets and her “bomb-site bedroom”—so the episode is comic as well as frightening. The stakes prevent the humour from making the problem seem trivial: the ticket has made a “huge dent” in her “meagre finances”, is “non-changeable and non-refundable”, and she has only two days before departure.

The chronology accelerates from a sleepless night to the journey to the passport office. The active verbs “gasping”, “running”, “counting” and “panted” create a breathless pace, while Charlotte’s “Go, go, go!” and the simile “like I was a man parachuting into enemy territory” turn an ordinary errand into a mock military mission. The mismatch between ticket 792 and the displayed 23 briefly magnifies her fear—“My heart almost leapt out of my chest with panic”—before the young man’s casual “Oh, we don’t bother with that” punctures the tension. His promise, “We’d better organise you a new passport”, turns the emotional trajectory towards practical hope; the final paragraph then shows Keyes submitting the application form, photos, birth certificate and photocopies of her plane tickets to support the urgency of her case. The movement from an optimistic Christmas plan through comic crisis to this hopeful practical step gives the anecdote a clear emotional arc.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Mele begins with a direct instruction not to search places where the item is already known not to be, attributing it to “experts” to give the advice authority from the outset. He then moves to a familiar scenario: “You were sure you left the keys right there on the counter, and now they are nowhere to be found.” The direct question “Where could they be?” lets the reader recognize the frustration of misplacing something. The second-person address (“you were sure”) makes the advice feel relevant, while the simile “like going on a treasure hunt without a map” gives a vivid, lightly humorous image of an unplanned search. Calling misplacing objects “an everyday occurrence” reassures readers that the problem is ordinary; the aside “Consider printing this out” adds a conversational joke about the article’s practical value.

The subheadings make the advice easy to follow and give it a purposeful sequence: stay calm, search systematically, focus on clutter, retrace your steps, prevent future losses, and remember that forgetting is normal. Mele bolsters the advice by naming people and their expertise—Michael Solomon, a PhD student, a researcher, a professor, a doctor and a writer—rather than presenting every suggestion as unsupported opinion. Solomon’s compact assertion, “There are no missing objects. Only unsystematic searchers”, is reassuring but also gives the reader a clear principle: change the method, not simply search more frantically. The researcher’s “Our results suggest” presents the clutter advice as evidence-informed.

Many instructions use imperatives and direct address: “Go from one room to another”, “Don’t go round in circles”, “Try to recreate as rich an experience as possible” and “Think about the location”. These short, specific directions offer a calm alternative to panicked searching. The article also varies the advice: it moves from practical room-by-room searching to mentally recreating what the reader was doing when the item was last seen, then to low- and high-tech prevention. Pairing simple habits such as bright key chains or phone cases with trackers and apps makes the recommendations feel accessible across different situations.

The ending returns to reassurance. Dr Devi explains that multitasking can challenge memory and that “faulty memory is not a sign of lower intelligence”. By ending on this point, Mele removes blame from the reader and frames the advice as helpful support for a common difficulty, not a criticism of the person who lost something.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers treat losing things as a recognizable problem, but Keyes makes one passport search into a comic personal crisis, whereas Mele turns misplacing objects into a general problem that readers can manage. Keyes starts with a specific event—she is packing to visit her sister for Christmas—then shocks the reader with “there it was – gone!” and the abrupt “Except it couldn’t be”. Mele instead addresses the reader directly: “You were sure you left the keys right there”, followed by “Where could they be?” His opening simile, “like going on a treasure hunt without a map”, makes the uncertainty familiar and less alarming.

The difference in perspective shapes the tone. Keyes lets us experience her escalating anxiety through “my mouth went a little dry”, “sweaty hands” and “My heart almost leapt out of my chest with panic”. Her increasingly frantic actions—“tear my room apart”, “launched an attack” and “whirling”—make the search seem chaotic. Mele’s advice is calmer and more controlled: “Go from one room to another” and “Don’t go round in circles”. His short, direct instructions contrast with Keyes’s rushed sequence of events. Yet both writers use humour to keep the subject engaging: Keyes calls it a “tragic tale” and describes being hurried out “like I was a man parachuting into enemy territory”; Mele compares searching with a “treasure hunt” and adds the aside “Consider printing this out”.

Their sources of support also differ. Keyes’s account depends on people she meets: Charlotte advises her to contact the Irish Embassy, helps her through the application requirements and pushes her into action; the young man at the counter listens and agrees to arrange a new passport. His practical, reassuring “We’d better organise you a new passport” marks the emotional turn towards hope, after which Keyes describes submitting documents to support the urgency of her case. Mele takes a broader, advisory perspective, quoting named figures—including Solomon, Cunningham, Nowakowska, Khan and Dr Devi—to establish credibility. His subheadings organize a range of solutions, from searching systematically to retracing steps and using trackers. Unlike Keyes, he also explicitly explains that forgetting is “normal” and not a sign of “lower intelligence”.

Both texts therefore suggest that a lost item need not remain a disaster, but they reach that point differently. Keyes entertains through one escalating, first-person anecdote whose immediate problem appears close to being resolved; Mele informs and reassures through general advice supported by experts. The first makes readers feel the panic of a particular search; the second gives them methods for preventing or coping with similar searches.

**MS alignment check:** This comparison stays focused on both writers’ ideas and perspectives, compares their purposes and styles throughout, and balances references from each text. It develops the MS’s points about Text One as an entertaining personal account and Text Two as an informative advisory article, their different sources of support, and their shared suggestion that losing things can be coped with (indicative content, MS p. 9). Q7 is assessed by AO3; Level 5 requires a varied, comprehensive comparison, analysis across both texts and balanced, discriminating references (MS p. 10).
