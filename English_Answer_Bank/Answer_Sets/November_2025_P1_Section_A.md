### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** The writer presents digital communication as a powerful convenience that has made good online manners increasingly important. The opening tricolon, “modern technology, social media and instant communication”, quickly establishes how broad the change is. She then defines “netiquette” by combining “network” and “etiquette”, making the new term sound like a practical extension of familiar good manners rather than an entirely separate code.

In “A Communications Revolution”, the emphatic claim that the Internet has “revolutionised our everyday communication” is reinforced by the active verbs “removed” and “opened up”. The list moves from “ordering a takeaway” to “sharing the first pictures of your newborn child”, showing that online communication reaches into ordinary shopping and intimate family life. The upbeat examples make the Internet seem effortless—“all it takes is just the click of a button”—before the pivot “However” qualifies that optimism. The balanced parallel phrase “with new opportunities come new responsibilities” crystallises the article’s central argument. The short afterthought “if not more so” intensifies the claim that online conduct matters at least as much as behaviour offline.

The section “Set Clear Boundaries” then makes the costs of constant connection concrete. The informal shorthand “24/7” and the description of people expected to be always “on” suggest a world with no natural stopping point. This is contrasted with the older convention of not telephoning after “7 or 8pm”: now people swipe through WhatsApp notifications “late into the night”. The comparison makes the change feel immediate and recognisable, while the words “stress levels” and “work-family balance” show its consequences. The paired verbs “switch off and recuperate” imply that people need recovery from this pressure, not simply better manners for appearance’s sake.

The writer also uses evidence to show how online habits interrupt in-person relationships. The noun “distractions” gives social media a negative force, and the poll finding that many people check their platforms “at least ten times” while out with friends makes the scale of the problem striking. Yet her advice is not only critical: the positive adjective “deliberate” presents controlled use as achievable. By warning that posts are in the “public domain” and can “never” be retrieved, she shifts from distraction to permanence. The emphatic “never” and “vital” make selectivity sound essential, while the phrase “who we really are” appeals to readers’ wish to create a truthful, favourable online identity.

The later advice is practical and directed at both adults and young people. Imperatives such as “Try putting your mobile phone in a different room” turn the article’s concerns into realistic actions. The list of switching off notifications, putting phones away during homework and using airplane mode before bed gives readers specific alternatives to constant checking. Capitalising “Golden Rule” gives familiar moral weight to the instruction to treat people online as one would face-to-face. The final warning about a “digital footprint” that may be seen by teachers, friends’ parents and potential employers broadens the consequences beyond the immediate audience. Across the passage, direct address and inclusive “we” make the issue feel shared; the structure moves from the Internet’s benefits to its pressures and then to practical guidance.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** The writer presents modern manners as changing in form rather than simply disappearing. She opens with direct questions about familiar situations: whether party guests reply and whether someone holds a door. These examples invite readers to test the issue against their own behaviour. The larger question—“Do manners still matter, or have they become relics of the past?”—sets up a debate, while “isn’t a simple yes or no” immediately resists an oversimplified answer. Her position is therefore measured from the start.

The first subheading, “How have manners changed over time?”, introduces a broad historical comparison. The adjective “significant” and noun “transformation”, reinforced by “fundamentally altered”, stress the scale of change. The tricolon “texts, tweets and emojis” compresses several modern forms of communication into a quick list, while “diminishing face-to-face interactions” suggests that convenience may have displaced personal contact. Although the writer singles out Generation Z “particularly”, she qualifies this by adding that it “isn’t just those in Gen Z” who behave differently. That qualification keeps the discussion from turning into a simple accusation against young people.

The description of older customs as “pillars of social etiquette” presents them as the established foundation of polite society, but the following list—thank-you notes, napkins and formal grammar—also helps explain why some younger readers may experience the old rules as demanding. The contrast between a handwritten note and a thank-you text shows etiquette adapting to communication habits, not necessarily to a loss of gratitude. The phrase “more appropriate for the informal nature” of Gen Z’s communication acknowledges their reasoning rather than dismissing it.

The interrogative subheadings guide the reader through possible causes and examples, so the article develops its case in stages. Under “Why have the expectations around manners changed?”, smartphones and brief messages are only part of a wider explanation: the writer also mentions two-income homes, the isolation of COVID-19, increased mobility and social change. The verb “eroded” suggests that fewer face-to-face opportunities gradually weaken the practice of etiquette. Her description of mobility as lowering “the cost of bad manners” is especially pointed: when strangers are unlikely to know or correct one another, familiar neighbourhood pressure is reduced. At the same time, “more accepting and equal” presents some changes positively, and global travel is linked to greater exposure to different customs.

The punctuation section makes the generational disagreement explicit. Older readers may judge informal messages “sloppy or disrespectful”, whereas millennials and Gen Z may find them “faster and more natural”. Setting these opposing descriptions side by side shows that the same style can be interpreted in sharply different ways. The writer then counters the assumption that young people communicate carelessly: digital natives use their own rules to “share exactly what they mean”. The adverb “exactly” suggests intention and precision, and the concluding point that older generations have “yet to learn” this new language gently reverses the usual criticism.

The final section gives the writer’s answer—“Absolutely”—in a concise, confident sentence, but the rest of the conclusion preserves her nuance. She rejects the idea that changed etiquette necessarily means less “respect or kindness”, and praises “authenticity and directness”. The concluding tricolon “Respect, consideration and kindness” returns to enduring values; the contrast between these foundations and “rigid adherence to outdated rules” makes her final perspective reassuring but not dismissive of manners. She argues that the principles remain important even as their expression evolves.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers recognise that digital communication has changed how people behave, but they approach good manners from different angles. Text One, *Netiquette: A Guide to Digital Manners*, is a practical advisory article focused on conduct online. Text Two, *Would Grandma approve? The new rules of etiquette*, takes a wider, generational view of traditional manners and asks how their meaning has shifted. Their structures signal this difference: Text One uses topic-based headings such as “Set Clear Boundaries” and “Be a Role Model”, while Text Two’s question-form headings invite readers to consider a debate.

Both texts acknowledge the scale and convenience of technological change. Text One describes the Internet as having “revolutionised” everyday communication, “removed all physical barriers” and made tasks as varied as ordering food and sharing a newborn’s first pictures possible with a click. Text Two likewise says smartphones and social media have “fundamentally altered” communication, replacing some face-to-face contact with “texts, tweets and emojis”. However, Text One quickly turns from opportunity to duty: “with new opportunities come new responsibilities”. Text Two begins by asking whether manners have become “relics of the past”, then answers that the issue “isn’t a simple yes or no”. The first writer stresses the need for rules in a new digital environment; the second frames the question as a more complex change in social expectations.

Their concerns about technology overlap, but Text One is more cautionary. It warns that people are expected to be “always ‘on’” and contrasts the old restraint of not telephoning after “7 or 8pm” with WhatsApp messages arriving “late into the night”. Its references to stress and disrupted work-family balance present constant connection as potentially harmful. It also uses a poll about checking social media while out with friends to show how digital habits can intrude on face-to-face interaction. Text Two also notices reduced face-to-face communication, but explains this through a wider set of causes—including smartphones, two-income homes, COVID-19 isolation and increased mobility. Rather than presenting only a personal technology problem, it places manners in a changing social and historical context.

The writers also differ in how they treat generational behaviour. Text One directly advises parents to model acceptable conduct and gives children practical rules, including turning off notifications and treating others online as they would face-to-face. Its capitalised “Golden Rule” presents this as a stable principle that applies in cyberspace as well as offline. Text Two compares older customs—such as handwritten thank-you notes and formal punctuation—with newer habits. It acknowledges that older generations may see informal texts as “sloppy or disrespectful”, but contrasts this with Gen Z’s view of them as “faster and more natural”. By arguing that digital natives use their own conventions to “share exactly what they mean”, the writer is more openly sympathetic to the younger generation. Text One emphasises guidance and boundaries; Text Two emphasises understanding why conventions have changed.

Their endings reinforce these perspectives. Text One closes with a warning that online posts leave a “digital footprint” visible to teachers, parents and potential employers, so its final emphasis is on responsibility and lasting consequences. Text Two ends by arguing that “mutual respect and understanding” matter more than “rigid adherence to outdated rules”. Both writers value respect, and neither treats manners as irrelevant; however, Text One is more advisory and cautionary, whereas Text Two is more explanatory and reassuring about flexibility. Together, the texts suggest that good manners still matter, even though the settings and forms in which they are expressed have changed.
