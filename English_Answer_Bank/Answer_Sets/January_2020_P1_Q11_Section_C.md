- **Stronger responses:** “detailed and lively with fully developed ideas… a sustained focus on description… described their chosen picture… in detail, using an imaginative vocabulary and crafted the description using a wide range of sentences, paragraphs and descriptive devices… **clearly trying to convey a ‘verbal’ picture**.”
- **Less able:** “did not focus on the descriptive nature of the task and wrote narrative responses **focusing on re-telling the events that were shown** in the picture, painting or photograph rather than describing it.”

**Consequences:** describe the image as an object — its surface, framing, light, what is at the edge — rather than retelling what happens inside it; keep the emotional effect direct and unadorned, as the report credits; and choose something specific enough to be unmistakable.

## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed description

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 15) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 16–17), with method from this paper's own report. **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **The dog in the empty space**
>
> It is a small painting — smaller than a sheet of A4, which is the first surprise, because the space inside it feels enormous — and it is almost entirely empty.
>
> The emptiness is not blank. It is a sloping band of ochre at the bottom, painted thickly enough that you can see where the brush turned, and above it a wall of pale, dirty gold that gets darker and heavier towards the top of the canvas, until at the very top it is nearly brown. There is no horizon in any useful sense. There is no sky, no building, no ground you could stand on. The painting is a space with nothing in it, and it has been painted with such care that the nothing has weather in it.
>
> And then, at the bottom left, low down, almost at the edge, there is a head.
>
> Just a head. A dog's head, tilted slightly up, ears back, eyes open, looking at something above and outside the frame — and because the frame is everything we have, we cannot see what it is looking at. The head is small. If you hold the painting at arm's length, the head is about the size of a thumbprint, and everything above it is empty gold, and the dog is rising or sinking into that gold, and the painting does not tell you which.
>
> The brushwork on the head is loose — it is barely a head at all, three or four strokes and a dark smudge for the eyes — and that looseness is what makes it unbearable. A finished dog would be a portrait. This is a shape that has been left behind.
>
> The impression it made on me was not sadness exactly, although that is the word people use for this painting. It was recognition of a particular kind of aloneness: the moment when you understand that the thing you are looking at is above your head, and there is nothing to stand on, and no one in the frame to explain it. The dog is not frightened. That is the part I cannot get past. It is just looking.
>
> I saw it first as a reproduction pinned to the wall of a school corridor, between a fire notice and a timetable, and it stopped me in a way that the fire notice did not. In a corridor full of people, a thumb-sized head in a waste of gold was looking at something none of us could see, and it has been looking at it ever since, in every room I have hung it in.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Subjects:** a family photograph (common but workable if written “simply and directly”), a painting seen in a gallery or a book, a newspaper photograph, a school photograph, a portrait of a stranger, a childhood snapshot.
- **Structures:** composition described centre-outwards (as here); the image described in three passes at different distances; the image contrasted with what it does not show.
- **How to handle “the impression”:** name one specific recognition rather than a general mood; tie the effect to a technical feature (scale, looseness, framing, light).
- **What to avoid:** retelling the events inside the picture; a biography of the artist; describing the day you saw it rather than the image; “it is very beautiful and means a lot to me”; a family photograph whose significance is asserted but never shown.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO4:** the description is organised by the painting's composition (emptiness, then the small head), so the form of the answer mirrors the form of the image; the effect is stated as a recognition and tied to the brushwork.
