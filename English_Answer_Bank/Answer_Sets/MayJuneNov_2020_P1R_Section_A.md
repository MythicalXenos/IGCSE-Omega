### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Knowles begins by presenting weather as something frightening. The broad phrase “scared of almost anything that could cause damage” is followed by a list—“volcanoes, fire, earthquakes” and “thunder and lightning”—before she recalls being “terrified of thunderstorms” and “hiding inside the house”. This makes the fear feel like a genuine part of her childhood, not a passing dislike. The second-grade weather unit changes that perspective: she says the “weather play and weather study unit sparked my interest in weather”. The metaphor “sparked” suggests curiosity catching suddenly, and the contrast that her fear “decreased” while her “interest” began to grow marks the turning point.

She then traces that interest becoming a serious ambition. The repeated “more” in “learning more and more” and “the more I learned, the more I loved it all” makes her enthusiasm seem to build with knowledge. The phrase “further cemented my love” suggests that later experiences make her decision to become a meteorologist firmer. The passage therefore moves chronologically from childhood, through school, to the 2013 storms that confirm her choice.

The first storm is described through a quick change in the setting: “clouds began to form”, the wind “began to pick up”, and “the daylight fled”. The personification makes darkness seem to move in as the storm gathers. The pace then sharpens with “Then the lightning started. And it did not stop.” The abrupt short sentence stresses its persistence; “every few seconds for many, many hours” extends that sense of duration. Although the lightning starts a fire, the writer is “mesmerized”, falls asleep “reluctantly” and is “amazed” by what she has seen. Her response is fascination rather than renewed fear.

The next storm is “even stronger”, with “very strong wind and rain” and sand blown into her face. The next morning’s disconnected dock and boats washed ashore show the weather’s force; she calls the sight “astonishing”. Yet the key change is in her reaction: she is “no longer that scared little kid” but “that weather fanatic who couldn’t get enough”. The closing simile “like a kid in a candy store” and the repeated “want more”, “see more” and “want to know more” present excitement turning into a desire to learn. The final hope for “awesome storms” leaves her future-facing and positive.

**MS alignment check:** This response develops the MS’s non-exhaustive points on childhood fear, “sparked”, repeated “more”, “further cemented”, the storm’s changing pace, “many, many hours”, “reluctantly”, the stronger second storm, the “scared little kid”/“weather fanatic” contrast, the candy-store simile and the positive ending (indicative content, MS p. 4). Q3 is assessed by AO2; Level 5 is 9–10 marks and requires perceptive analysis with discriminating references (MS p. 5). This constructed response has not been marked.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Gardner first establishes that the monsoon is prolonged: by August, sudden storms “which came from nowhere and ended quickly” have given way to the “monsoon proper”. Rain then invades the narrator’s home, dripping through the “rough plaster ceiling” to “muddy my floor and dampen my saris”. The comparison “as chilly as winter” makes the morning feel unusually cold, while the people are “huddled”, “shivering” and “predicting fever”. The compound is “completely still” and “strangely quiet”; the usual noise of the boys has disappeared. The short sentence “Rain, rain, rain.” repeats the central word three times, making the rain feel relentless, while “cool morning sunlight” recalls a brighter routine that now seems distant.

The account then moves from the village’s changed landscape to the narrator’s daily difficulties. “The paths had disintegrated into impassable mud” and “the river was full again”, so travel depends on boats. In her own yard, the ground becomes a “treacherous pit of the most slippery mud imaginable”. The repeated “every day” makes losing a flip-flop and nearly falling sound routine; coming back “muddier than before my wash” adds an ironic, lightly comic touch. The list “my water pot, my umbrella and, at night, my lantern” shows what she must carry when it rains, and the alliteration in “muddy morass” makes the route sound slow and difficult. Reaching the latrine “exhausted and victorious” turns a basic task into a small struggle. She “cursed” her sari and “prayed fervently” that dysentery would not strike, showing how the mud affects both her comfort and her sense of safety. Her slipping and leech-covered feet are contrasted with the villagers’ “sure and confident feet”.

Gardner widens the focus from one person to the community. Life seems to “slow down” until it “came almost to a standstill”; there is “little work”, and obtaining food money becomes a struggle ending in “hunger and debt”. Calls and work stop, tracks are submerged and familiar rickshaws and motorbikes disappear. The description therefore presents the monsoon as more than inconvenience: it restricts movement and threatens livelihoods.

The later boat images provide a limited but attractive contrast. The “engine boat” carries villagers to town, and the narrator now spots bamboo boats with “painted cabins” or “great cloth sails” moving through the water lilies. These details suggest ways people continue to travel despite the floods. The ending offers a small moment of comfort rather than claiming the rain is over: it has “subsided a little”, and Amma brings “hot, sweet tea” and fried cakes. The structure moves from rain entering the home, to personal and communal hardship, and finally to a brief act of care.

**MS alignment check:** This response develops the MS’s non-exhaustive points on the unpleasant waking conditions, people’s reactions, the quiet compound, repetition and contrast, muddy descriptions, the narrator’s reactions, village-wide disruption, and the contrasting boats (indicative content, MS p. 7). Q6 is assessed by AO2; Level 5 is 9–10 marks and requires perceptive analysis with discriminating references (MS p. 8). The source’s final tea/cakes detail is also used as a valid, source-based point. This constructed response has not been marked.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers use first-person accounts to show how powerful weather can be, but Knowles traces how an early interest grows into a personal passion, while Gardner shows a monsoon disrupting everyday life. The difference is clear in the time scale: Knowles looks back across childhood, school and later storms, whereas Gardner describes a rainy season that has continued for “at least a month”. Knowles traces her interest towards a career in meteorology, with the later storms confirming her choice; Gardner stays close to the effects of the monsoon on one Bangladeshi village.

Both passages show weather changing the physical world. Knowles recalls lightning starting a fire and, after the stronger storm, says “One of the boat docks had been disconnected” and some boats were “washed up on the shore”. She calls the sight “astonishing” and treats it as evidence of what the weather can do. Gardner’s rain enters her home to “muddy my floor”, while the paths become “impassable” and the river fills. For Knowles, damage makes the storm more fascinating; for Gardner, the prolonged water makes movement and ordinary tasks difficult.

Their perspectives and tones are also different. Knowles begins as “terrified” but ends as a “weather fanatic” who is “like a kid in a candy store”. Informal expressions such as “insane lightning” and the repeated “want more” make her enthusiasm sound immediate and personal. Gardner’s opening is colder and more unsettled: people are “shivering and predicting fever”, and the compound is “strangely quiet”. Her “treacherous pit” and “muddy morass” convey daily discomfort, while the wider village faces little work and difficulty obtaining money for food. Her account is more descriptive and extends beyond her own inconvenience to the community.

Neither ending is simply a repeat of the opening. Knowles finishes excited about her studies and hopes to see “awesome storms” in the future. Gardner offers a smaller relief: the rain has “subsided a little”, and Amma brings tea and cakes. Some villagers also continue travelling by boat. Thus Knowles presents extreme weather as an inspiration that overcomes her childhood fear, while Gardner presents a persistent monsoon as a hardship that restricts life, though people find ways to cope and moments of comfort remain.

**MS alignment check:** This comparison considers a range of similarities and differences, compares both writers throughout, and balances references. It develops the MS’s points about the positive, inspirational perspective in Text One; the longer, disruptive monsoon in Text Two; their personal accounts; the different scales and settings; and the limited positivity near the end of Text Two (indicative content, MS p. 9). Q7 is assessed by AO3; Level 5 is 13–15 marks and requires comprehensive comparison, analysis across both texts and balanced, discriminating references. A response considering only one text is capped at the top of Level 2 (MS p. 10). No mark is assigned to this constructed answer.
