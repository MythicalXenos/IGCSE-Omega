### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** The narrator presents the relationship as genuinely close but complicated by divisions that make it difficult to call an ordinary friendship. His opening—“The curious thing was, I never thought of Hassan and me as friends either”—sounds reflective and unsettled. “Curious” signals that this is an unusual way to view their bond, especially after the shared experiences he immediately recalls. He repeats “Never mind” as he lists learning to ride a bicycle, making a homemade camera and flying kites. The refrain seems to brush aside evidence of closeness, so the reader senses a tension between the boys’ friendship-like actions and the narrator’s hesitation to name the relationship. The short sentences “Because history isn’t easy to overcome. Neither is religion” abruptly interrupt the nostalgic memories and identify serious barriers. The following “But” turns back towards childhood: their shared bond, he insists, was not going to be changed by “history, ethnicity, society, or religion”.

He recalls their childhood as joyful and familiar. The metaphor “one long lazy summer day” makes the first twelve years seem extended and carefree, while the tricolon “hide-and-seek, cops and robbers, cowboys and Indians” gives a lively list of ordinary boys’ games. Their adventures with the nomads are energetic and mischievous: they hear the animals approaching, hurl pebbles at goats and squirt water at mules. The repeated first-person plural “we” makes the activities feel shared. Yet Amir’s wording that “I’d make Hassan fire pebbles” also places him in control of what Hassan does, hinting that even the play is not entirely free of the unequal roles between them.

That inequality becomes clearer when the narrator contrasts their school-day routine. In the bazaars, he describes them strolling and talking together, and his father gives each boy an allowance, suggesting a brother-like closeness. The phrase “During the school year, we had a daily routine” then introduces a more unequal pattern. Amir “dragged” himself out of bed, while Hassan had “already washed up, prayed, and prepared my breakfast”. The compact list of Hassan’s duties, followed by the longer list of chores he performs at home, emphasises how much work falls to him. Amir admits that “while I ate and complained about homework, Hassan made my bed” and did other tasks. This contrast exposes the narrator’s comfort and self-centredness, as well as Hassan’s position as a servant in the household.

The passage then returns to the boys’ shared time after school. The three verbs “met up, grabbed a book, and trotted” make their trip up the hill sound eager and childlike. Carving “Amir and Hassan, the sultans of Kabul” on the pomegranate tree gives their friendship a shared, almost regal world of its own; the simple declaration “the tree was ours” makes the place feel like a private bond between them. Reading under the tree is another act of closeness, but it also exposes their different opportunities. Hassan cannot read because, the narrator says, his future illiteracy “had been decided the minute he had been born”—“what use did a servant have for the written word?” The descriptions “drawn to the mystery” and “seduced by a secret world” make books seem powerful and alluring, while “forbidden” stresses the unfairness of Hassan’s exclusion.

Their reading also complicates the balance between them. Hassan is “far better at solving” riddles than Amir, revealing an intelligence that his lack of schooling does not erase. Amir responds by stopping the riddles and choosing “unchallenging things”, which suggests he patronises Hassan and may feel threatened by his ability. Even so, Hassan “insisted” on “one more story, one more chapter”. Ending on his eagerness to keep listening leaves the reader with the pleasure they share, while the earlier details of class and literacy show why the narrator’s remembered friendship is affectionate but not straightforward.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** The writer immediately presents Claudia as an unusually important friend. The opening metaphor, “If friendships are the real love stories of our lives”, gives friendship the intensity of romance; calling Claudia “the one” makes her sound like the person the writer has been waiting to find. The personal anecdote that follows creates tension: she is in her pyjamas, locked out, with her two-year-old daughter alone inside. Although her phone app shows Maggie safely asleep, the situation still makes the writer sound vulnerable and distressed.

The repeated “grateful” and the qualifier “mainly” show that Claudia’s support matters more to the writer than the phone or dog. Their emotional states are contrasted: the writer is “mortified and distraught”, while Claudia is “calm and collected”. The repeated “Because” introduces a series of practical acts—making calls, helping find keys, offering to drive, feeding the dog and comforting the writer—so Claudia’s care seems both decisive and generous. The small detail that Claudia is also in her dressing gown, ready for bed, makes her help seem even more thoughtful because she gives up her own comfort to assist.

The writer shows that their bond also survives inconvenient experiences. Earlier that day Claudia had driven them to Santa Barbara; the six-hour journey includes Maggie being sick and is called “stressful”. Yet it soon “morphed into a funny story”, and the women joke about it later. The contrast between an unpleasant event and its later humorous retelling suggests ease and shared resilience. The writer then stacks short, confident statements—“We make each other laugh. We text constantly. She’s everything I want in a friend.” Only after this list does she reveal, “She’s also 72. Three decades older than me.” The delayed age detail surprises the reader and shows that their connection crosses a generational gap without weakening their closeness.

A flashback explains how the friendship began by chance. The writer is initially “frazzled” and “not in the mood”, and looks at Claudia “blankly”, so she is not searching for a new friend. Claudia’s warmth changes this first encounter: the writer says she “warm[s] to her immediately”. After a gap of “a few months”, they meet again; the writer’s worry about whether they will have “anything in common” briefly creates suspense. Claudia’s “thoughtful gifts” and the smile that “radiates such warmth” present her as welcoming. At Claudia’s house, paired descriptions—“stylish but cosy, lived-in but immaculate”—make the home seem both cared for and comfortable. The sensory image of the writer’s feet sinking into the “plush carpet” accompanies her feeling “relaxed and comfortable”. She is “completely unselfconscious”, “the way you do with a family member, not a new friend”, showing how quickly the relationship feels intimate and secure.

Near the end, the writer moves from describing individual moments to explaining Claudia’s emotional importance. The tricolon “unconditional love, protection and emotional support” presents the friendship as deep, dependable and almost familial; the writer says it recalls what she once received from her mother. Her claim that friends “simply appear in your life when you most need them” turns Claudia’s story into a broader perspective on friendship. The final reference to Claudia having “a spare set of keys to my house” circles back to the opening lockout. This practical callback is also a sign of trust: the friendship that began with a crisis has become part of the writer’s everyday life.

It aims at the AO2 Level 5 descriptor (MS p. 12), but is an original, unscored worked answer.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers present friendship as powerful, but they explore different barriers and different kinds of support. Text One is a fictional first-person memory of two boys, while Text Two is a first-person article about two women; the first is nostalgic but qualified, whereas the second uses a personal anecdote to celebrate an unexpected friendship. In Text One, the narrator hesitates to call Hassan a friend “in the usual sense” and names “history”, “ethnicity”, “society” and “religion” as forces that divide them. Text Two foregrounds a different difference: Claudia is “72”, “Three decades older” than the writer. Both friendships cross expected boundaries, but Text One connects its boundary to the boys’ social backgrounds and the servant relationship, while Text Two shows age does not prevent two neighbours from becoming close.

The writers also show the relationships forming in different ways. Text One looks back over “the first twelve years” of the narrator’s life and compresses his childhood into “one long lazy summer day”. The tricolon of games and repeated “we” make the bond seem longstanding and built from shared play. Text Two instead opens in the middle of a crisis: the writer is locked out while her daughter is inside, and Claudia immediately helps. The later flashback to their first meeting shows that the friendship grew through chance encounters and took “a few months” to develop. Thus one text presents a bond rooted in years of childhood routines, while the other first proves its strength through an act of kindness and then traces its gradual beginning.

Both texts show friends helping one another, but the balance of their relationships is different. In Text One the boys share an allowance and play together, and Amir reads stories to Hassan; the tree marked “the sultans of Kabul” makes them seem equal in their imagined private world. However, the narrator’s routine reveals that Hassan prepares his breakfast and performs household chores while Amir eats and complains. His inability to read is linked to being a servant, so the friendship remains entangled with class inequality. In Text Two, Claudia’s phone calls, search for keys, offer to drive and care for the dog make her practical generosity central. The writer also stresses reciprocity in their enjoyment—they “make each other laugh” and “text constantly”—and describes feeling “unselfconscious” with her. The texts therefore both value care, but Text One is more troubled by unequal roles, whereas Text Two presents the age gap as compatible with closeness and emotional security.

Their tones also differ. The repeated “Never mind” in Text One seems to dismiss evidence of the boys’ closeness, while the short sentences “Because history isn’t easy to overcome. Neither is religion” interrupt the happy memories with serious social barriers. The writer’s recollection is affectionate, but not uncomplicated. Text Two is much more openly celebratory: the “real love stories” metaphor and the phrase “the one” convey certainty about Claudia’s importance. Even difficult events become part of the friendship: the stressful car trip “morphed into a funny story”. The writer ultimately describes Claudia’s support as “unconditional love, protection and emotional support”, while Text One’s strongest simple statement of belonging is that “the tree was ours”.

The endings reinforce these perspectives in different ways. Text One closes on Hassan insisting there is time for “one more story, one more chapter”, returning to a shared childhood pleasure and leaving the relationship rooted in their companionship. Text Two ends with the writer’s general belief that friends appear when needed, then circles back to the opening incident through Claudia’s “spare set of keys”. That callback turns practical help into a lasting sign of trust. Overall, both texts emphasize the importance of friendship, but Text One presents a warm bond complicated by social inequality, while Text Two offers a more direct celebration of a friendship that overcomes an age difference and becomes a source of family-like support.
