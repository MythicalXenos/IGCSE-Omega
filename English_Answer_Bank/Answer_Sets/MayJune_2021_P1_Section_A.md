### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Mallalieu makes the trip sound doomed from its opening sentence. The repetition of “good journey” is not praise: it is immediately reframed by “it was never going to be a good journey” and juxtaposed with “bad ideas”. This makes the narrator sound rueful and self-mocking about the optimism of his plan. His wish to be “somewhere else, anywhere else” makes the journey seem like an impulsive escape from the “unpleasant office”, while the imagined return “all bright and eager” is undercut by the isolated sentence “I should have known better.” The contrast foreshadows how far the actual trip will be from the refreshing adventure he expected.

The choice of Rurrenabaque first sounds romantic because of its “wonderfully romantic” name, but the description quickly darkens: it is a “final jumping-off place into the heart of darkness” and Fawcett called it “a dismal heap”. Fawcett’s disappearance adds an ominous warning about following his route. The narrator’s late planning then creates a sense of accumulating difficulty: he must “zigzag” through a “disturbingly large number” of airports, words that make the route seem confusing and needlessly complicated. On arriving in La Paz, the tricolon “hot, humid and very black” makes the night feel oppressive. The description of altitude sickness as having “all the disadvantages of being drunk with none of the benefits” is comic, but the “headache, nausea, disorientation…” and trailing ellipsis also suggest that the symptoms could continue.

The short paragraph “In the morning, I got up too early” interrupts the account and makes his timing mistake stand out just before the hurried departure. His hotel is “impenetrably dark”; he has no torch, no booked taxi appears, and it is “dangerously late”. The problems compound at the airport: it is “not the one” he expected, the officials cannot speak English, and there is “sudden panic”. The rushed verbs “rushed” and “bundled” make him seem swept onto an “ancient” plane before his ticket is checked. Its “ageing air force officer” and the plane that “dipped and swerved” between valleys make the flight sound genuinely unsafe; the dry aside that planes like it have a history of finding their “final resting place” turns that danger into dark humour.

The arrival brings relief rather than the transformation he hoped for: Mallalieu’s verdict, “A dump yes, but dismal no”, marks the change in his feelings. He enjoys the river trip but dislikes the rainforest, which is “too ‘in your face’, in your hair, in your clothes and under your skin”; the accumulating list makes the jungle feel impossible to escape. “And I didn’t have a torch” recalls an earlier mistake, while the bright lights on his return make the small town seem “just like a city”. The ending circles back to the original taxi driver at another deserted airport. The narrator is pleased to see him but never learns what he was doing there “as he didn’t speak any English”, leaving the story on the same language barrier and wry humour that troubled the journey.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Kepnes opens with three isolated, one-word sentences: “Hope. Fear. Excitement.” Their clipped rhythm presents first-time travel as a mixture of anticipation and anxiety before the writer explains that he “didn’t know what to expect”. His first-person account—he was “young and inexperienced” and made “basic travel mistakes”—makes the advice personal rather than abstract. The comic sign imagined above his head, “I HAVE NO IDEA WHAT I’M DOING”, lets him admit his earlier uncertainty without sounding superior. He now speaks from “10 years of travel”, framing the blog as the advice he would give his younger self.

The subheadings divide the post into practical stages, so a new traveller can follow it like a reassuring checklist. Many are direct imperatives: “Don’t be scared”, “Don’t live by your guidebook” and “Travel slow”. The repeated second-person “you” addresses the reader personally. Kepnes acknowledges that “taking the leap into the unknown is scary”, but counters the metaphor of a frightening leap with the reassuring image of a “well-worn travel trail”. His comparison with early explorers—today’s travellers are not “discovering new continents”—makes the unfamiliar seem manageable; the logic “If millions of people can make their way around the world each year, so can you” turns reassurance into a direct encouragement.

The heading “Travel slow” introduces a warning against treating travel as a list of destinations. The exaggerated figures “20 cities in 20 days” and “100 countries” make an overfilled itinerary sound almost absurd. Kepnes contrasts the apparent achievement of many stops with the actual result: “photos, stress and a whirlwind of experiences, but no real knowledge”. The “whirlwind” metaphor suggests activity without time to absorb a place. The inclusive “we” in “we are always trying to squeeze everything in” makes this a common temptation rather than a personal failing. In contrast, the short commands “Don’t rush your trip” and “Slow down” enact the calm pace he recommends, before the metaphor “drink deep from a culture” presents slower travel as more rewarding.

Other sections make the advice concrete. “You don’t need a lot of gear” and “Pack light” offer simple, memorable instructions; “Trust me” adds the voice of experience. Under “Get a phone”, Kepnes uses the quick questions “Did your friends get the message? Will they be there?! Who knows!” to recreate the uncertainty of arranging a meeting while people are travelling. The practical answer—“Invest in one”—connects the advice to the value of keeping in touch. Likewise, “Go with the flow” pairs a clear heading with the positive phrase “happy accidents”, showing that flexibility can create opportunities rather than merely prevent stress. Repeating “No matter how well you plan” before advising readers to “Have a financial cushion” acknowledges that unexpected costs cannot always be controlled.

The final sections move from practical preparation towards confidence and companionship. The contrast “You may have screamed, but you loved that canyon swing” reassures readers that fear and enjoyment can coexist; the brisk commands “Challenge yourself. Take risks. Try new things” encourage them to act on that idea. At the end, the tricolon “They will guide you, point you in the right direction and be your mentors” presents other travellers as a source of support. The short assurances “You will make friends. You will be OK” lead into the apparent contradiction “Though you are travelling alone, you will never be alone.” The final tricolon—“take a deep breath, relax and enjoy!”—leaves the reader with calm, confident instructions rather than a warning.

**MS alignment check:** This develops the Results MS’s points on the opening fragments, first-person experience, subheadings, direct/inclusive pronouns, reassuring tone, the “leap” and “well-worn trail” images, travel statistics, short sentences and imperatives, “happy accidents”, practical advice and the closing tricolons (indicative content, printed p. 9). Q6 is assessed by AO2; Level 5 is 9–10 marks and requires perceptive analysis with discriminating references (printed p. 10). This constructed response has not been marked.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers draw on personal experience, but Mallalieu turns one hurried journey into a humorous account of things going wrong, while Kepnes uses years of travel to advise newcomers how to avoid common mistakes. Mallalieu imagines getting “into the wilds” and returning to his desk refreshed in “seven days”; the short time scale helps explain why the trip becomes rushed. Kepnes, by contrast, warns against “20 cities in 20 days” and “100 countries”, arguing that speed leaves travellers with “photos, stress and a whirlwind of experiences, but no real knowledge”. The first writer’s compressed itinerary illustrates the problem that the second explicitly advises readers to avoid.

The writers also shape their experiences differently. Text One moves chronologically through Mallalieu’s trip: the “disturbingly large number” of airports, a pitch-dark hotel, the wrong airport and an “ancient” plane build a sequence of mishaps. His short sentence “I should have known better” and the wry description of planes finding their “final resting place” make the narrative entertaining even when the flight sounds dangerous. Text Two is instead broken into subheadings such as “Don’t be scared” and “Travel slow”. Its imperatives and direct address—“you” and “your”—turn the writer’s experiences into an organised set of practical instructions for first-time travellers.

Their overall attitudes contrast, although neither is entirely one-sided. Mallalieu calls the rainforest “too ‘in your face’, in your hair, in your clothes and under your skin”, so travel brings discomfort as well as adventure. His return to Rurrenabaque is a “relief”, and the bright lights seem “just like a city”; he can enjoy the river journey even while disliking the jungle. Kepnes is more consistently encouraging: the “well-worn travel trail” and “happy accidents” present travel as manageable and potentially rewarding. However, he acknowledges fear, stress, unexpected expenses and the possibility of disliking an activity, so his positive advice does not pretend that every experience is easy.

Both texts use personal, informal voices, but for different purposes. Mallalieu’s first-person account is an entertaining story for a general travel-reading audience, ending with the taxi driver he cannot question because “he didn’t speak any English”. Kepnes writes a blog for people travelling for the first time, combining humour about his younger self with direct reassurance: “You will make friends. You will be OK.” The first text lets readers share the unpredictability of one specific journey; the second draws on accumulated experience to help readers make their own journeys more considered and confident.

**MS alignment check:** This compares both writers throughout and develops the Results MS’s contrasts in purpose, audience, structure, pace and attitude, while also recognizing that both texts draw on personal experience and that Mallalieu’s account includes relief as well as difficulty. The references are balanced across Text One and Text Two.
