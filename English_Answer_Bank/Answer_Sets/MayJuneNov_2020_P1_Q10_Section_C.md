## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed story

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 15) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 16–17), with method from this paper's own report. **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **Just in Time**
>
> The chemist on Mirpur Road closed at seven, and the prescription was in my pocket at ten past six with four kilometres and one river between me and it.
>
> It was for my sister, who is four, and it was the second time that week that the pharmacy near our flat had been out of it. My mother had written the name of the medicine on the back of an electricity bill in block capitals, and I had folded it into my shirt pocket like a document. I want to be clear that nobody was dying. She had a fever that would not come down and a cough that kept her awake, and the medicine was ordinary and necessary, and the chemist closed at seven.
>
> The first obstacle was a wedding. A whole street of one, with a drum group and a generator and three hundred chairs, and the road narrowed to the width of one rickshaw, and I stood in that crowd for six minutes with my hand on the folded bill.
>
> The second was my own fault. I took the short way through the market, which was packed because it always is at that hour, and I lost four more minutes against a woman arguing about the price of fish. I know it was four because I looked at the clock on the bank, and then I ran, and running in that heat is not a heroic thing; it is a hot, stupid, hiccupping thing, and I had to stop at the bridge and hold the rail.
>
> On the bridge a man selling water from a cooler asked me if I was all right. I said, *Chemist. Seven o'clock,* and he gave me a plastic cup without asking for money, and I drank it, and I have thought about that cup more often than about the medicine.
>
> I got to the shop at three minutes to seven. The shutter was half down and the owner was inside with his keys, and he saw me coming and he did not close it. He lifted it another foot, and I went under it on my knees like a man entering a tent, and I handed him the electricity bill, and he read the writing, and he said, *This one. Yes. Two hundred and forty.*
>
> I was out on the pavement with the box in a paper bag at four minutes past seven, with the shutter down behind me and the street lights coming on, and I walked the whole way home because I could not run any more, and I was not late for anything that mattered by then.
>
> My sister took the medicine and slept for eleven hours and woke up hungry. My mother put the box in the cupboard with the others. Nobody said *well done*, and there was nothing to say it for — but I have kept the electricity bill, with her block capitals on the back, and every so often I take it out and look at the time I wrote under the address, because it is the only record of the evening I got to the chemist just in time.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Interpretations:** being late for an exam, flight, appointment, interview, audition, wedding, birth; rescuing someone; a deadline at work; a piece of news delivered at the last moment.
- **Structures:** one journey against a clock (as here); two near-misses and a final success; a story where “just in time” turns out to be too late by seconds and the narrator must live with it.
- **Building tension:** a visible clock, an obstacle that removes an option, a helper who costs the narrator nothing and therefore matters.
- **What to avoid:** the alarm clock and the traffic jam; a deathbed; childbirth; gore; a rescue from a burning building; ornate vocabulary used for its own sake; a title line dropped in without preparation; a plot that needs more time than the story has.
