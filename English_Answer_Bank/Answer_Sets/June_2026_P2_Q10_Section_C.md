## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed story

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 8) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (printed pp. 9–10; PDF pp. 21–22), with craft method informed by the June 2025 report. **No report or script exists for this paper.** Not a Pearson script, not examiner-marked, **no score claimed**.

> **Lost and found**
>
> The charity shop smelled of other people's wardrobes. My job, two afternoons a week, was to sort the bags that came over the counter and decide what was worth a shelf. Most of it was not: single earrings, novels with cracked spines, a kettle with a note taped to it that said SORRY.
>
> The bag on the last Thursday in March was heavier than it looked. A man had put it down without waiting for a receipt, which people do when they want the object out of the house before they change their mind. Inside were three jumpers, a tin of buttons, and something wrapped in a tea towel.
>
> It was a rabbit. Not a real one — a cloth rabbit, grey where it had once been white, one ear shorter than the other, and a face worn so smooth by thumbs that it had the look of a stone in a river. The stitching at the neck had been repaired twice, badly, by somebody who loved it.
>
> I did not cry in the stockroom, but only because I was holding my breath.
>
> Because I knew the ear. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting: the way the seam ran wonky, because my grandmother had turned it inside out at the kitchen table and let me hold the needle while she counted to four. I knew the smell, which was not a smell any more but a temperature, the particular coolness of something that has been slept against.
>
> I was six when I lost it. We had been at the market, and there were pigeons, and a man selling figs out of crates, and my mother's hand was full of carrier bags instead of mine. I remember the search: the two of us going back along the pavement, past the figs and the pigeons and the man with the crates shaking his head, saying nothing, the way people say nothing when they think a child should be told off.
>
> For twenty years I assumed I had dropped it in the street. What had actually happened is now obvious. Somebody picked it up, took it home, washed it, sewed its neck twice, and kept it on a bed for two decades — and then, on a Thursday in March, put it in a bag with three jumpers and a tin of buttons and walked away without waiting for a receipt.
>
> I rang the number on the donor's form that evening. She was eighty-one. She said she had found it, and that she was glad, and that she had always wondered whose it was.
>
> It is on my windowsill now, losing its greyness in the light. Some things are lost so that other people can look after them for a while. The rabbit is both halves of the title at once, and it took me twenty years to find that out.

*Word count: 485 words.*
