> **The Spark and the Fire**
>
> I agree with this statement, but only about the beginning of things, and the difference between the beginning and the end is the whole of the argument.
>
> Consider what one person actually does. In 1955 a woman in Montgomery, Alabama, refused to move from her seat on a bus. Rosa Parks did not end segregation, and she never claimed to; what she did was make an already unbearable arrangement visible, in a place and at a moment where it could not be ignored. The difference she made was real, and it was also a beginning. What turned a single refusal into a year-long boycott, and the boycott into a change in law, was thousands of people walking to work for three hundred and eighty-one days. One person lit it. Nobody would say one person burned it.
>
> This is not a quibble. It is the point at which most celebrations of individual greatness go wrong. We like the story of the lone figure because it is neat, and because it lets the rest of us off. But look closely at any campaign that worked — a village saving its school, a town fighting a bypass, a group of nurses reforming a ward — and you find the same three-part structure. One person names the problem. A handful of others believe them. Then an organisation forms, with the dull apparatus that organisations need: someone who keeps the accounts, someone who writes the letters, someone who can speak to a council and someone who can fix the microphone. You need people with different skills, and you need enough of them to be heard at all.
>
> So where does that leave the claim? I think it leaves it true in the way that matters most, and false in the way that matters practically. It is true that almost nothing good starts without one person being willing to be inconvenient — to raise the issue in a meeting where it is unwelcome, to be the first name on a letter. Ideas do not arrive in committees; they arrive in individuals, usually at an awkward moment. But the statement says one person can *make* a difference, and the making is collective. A single person can raise awareness. A single person cannot usually sustain pressure, and pressure is what changes things.
>
> There is a second correction, more uncomfortable. One person can make a *negative* difference just as easily, and often with less effort, because it is easier to stop a thing than to build it. The statement's optimism is not guaranteed by anything except our preference for good news.
>
> So: to a large extent I agree, provided we read the statement as being about ignition rather than completion. One person can make a difference begin. That is a smaller sentence than the one I was given, and it is the one the evidence supports.
