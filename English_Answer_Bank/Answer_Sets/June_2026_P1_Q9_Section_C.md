## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed discursive response

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS indicative content (printed p. 20) and its verified AO4/AO5 grids (pp. 21–22), with method informed by the June 2025 report commentary quoted above. **No report or script exists for this paper.** Not a Pearson script, not examiner-marked, **no score claimed**.

> **‘We rely on modern technology too much.’ To what extent do you agree with this statement?**
>
> Last summer my phone died on the way to an airport, and for three days I could not board a plane, pay for anything, find an address or tell the time without asking a stranger. I did not feel liberated. I felt like a man who had turned up to a card game without cards. That week is why I think the statement is true — but only in a narrower sense than it is usually meant.
>
> Begin with the obvious: the reliance is real and it is not a personality flaw. It is infrastructure. Trains are booked through it, money moves through it, hospitals run on it, and a school timetable is now a thing on a screen. When a system is built around a tool, using the tool is not weakness; it is membership. A person who wears glasses is not "too reliant" on glass.
>
> And the benefits are not trivial. Technology has transformed our lives in ways we would not give back: a scan that finds a tumour early, a translator that lets two people who share no language buy bread, a video call that puts a grandparent in a room she cannot travel to. Anyone nostalgic for a simpler age should be asked which decade they would like the medicine from.
>
> Where I do agree is in the cost column, and there are three costs I take seriously.
>
> The first is skill. I cannot read a paper map properly. I can no longer do long division without checking. My handwriting, five years after school, is a rumour. None of this is tragic on its own; collectively it means that when the tool is removed, a generation of otherwise capable people stops functioning.
>
> The second is attention. We have built an economy in which the most profitable use of your time is the one that takes it from you, and we have put it in everyone's pocket before anyone asked whether that was a good idea. That is not a side effect. It is the business model.
>
> The third is trust. A machine that answers every question fluently teaches you to stop checking, and a system that is right ninety-five per cent of the time is the hardest kind to argue with. We are outsourcing judgement to things that cannot be held responsible.
>
> The strongest objection is that the panic is old: Socrates feared writing, parents feared the calculator, and both are still with us. That is fair, and it is why I do not think the answer is to use less. It is to use it deliberately and keep the fallbacks alive — know one route without the map, one sum without the phone, one phone number in your head. Reliance becomes excessive at exactly the point where failure leaves you helpless rather than inconvenienced.
>
> So: to a considerable extent, yes. We do not rely on modern technology too much because we use it; we rely on it too much because we have stopped being able to do without it, and we did not decide that on purpose. The remedy is not abstinence. It is a little redundancy, kept deliberately, like a spare key under a stone.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Agree entirely:** a candidate may argue without qualification and still reach the top band, provided the argument is developed and the concession is at least recognised. Evidence: constant availability, lost skills, engineered addiction, misinformation, environmental cost.
- **Disagree entirely:** an equally viable line — that “too much” is a moral panic repeated at every technology, that reliance on tools is what humans do, and that the real problem is corporate design and unequal access, not quantity of use. The MS explicitly says “Candidates are free to agree or disagree”.
- **Middle positions:** distinguish reliance from dependence; distinguish personal use from infrastructural necessity; argue that reliance is fine but the *single point of failure* is not; argue about who profits from the reliance.
- **MS points not used by this model:** “the power it consumes” (energy and resource cost) and a fuller treatment of “how reliable/unreliable it is” as a separate section. Both are legitimate routes.
- **Form:** a discursive response may be a continuous argument, a slightly personal essay, or a piece with a rhetorical opening. What the June 2025 report penalises is narrative writing, heavy scaffolding and pre-taught phrases.
