## Exact QP prompt — Q8 (printed p. 15)

Section B rubric as printed: “Answer the question in this section.” / “You should spend 1 hour on this section.” / “Use ideas from BOTH Text One and Text Two in the Source Booklet to answer this question.”

> 8 You have been asked to give a talk to encourage your peers to visit new places.
>
> You should include:
> - what might be seen and experienced
> - what might be enjoyable
> - what challenges there might be.
>
> Write the text of your talk.
>
> Think carefully about the purpose of your talk and the audience for whom it is intended.
>
> (30)

*Note:* the form is fixed twice over — “give a talk” in the stem and “Write the text of your talk” in the instruction line. The report shows that candidates who wrote a travel review, an article or a story instead lost AO4 marks even when their English was sound.

**AO4:**

- Techniques named in successful responses: “writing in the first person, using rhetorical questions and direct address and anecdotes”; “humour, self-deprecating comments, informal language, direct address and rhetorical questions”; “personal experiences confidently and appropriately to relate to the audience”; “register was sustained throughout”.

- Less successful: “Some did not adopt a suitable register for a talk: some simply **recounted a travel experience or wrote a review of a destination**; others **wrote articles or stories**. Some did not convey much sense that this was supposed to be a talk to a peer group often only acknowledging it in the greeting and conclusion. Some candidates wrote in an **overly informal way (as if it was a transcription of a social media video)**.”

**AO5:** successful responses: “a varied range of correctly spelt vocabulary… a range of appropriate punctuation and different sentence structures to help them create particular effects… accurate paragraphing… evidence of proofreading.” Common errors: missing basic sentence punctuation; very long unstructured sentences; comma splicing; missing or misused apostrophes; homophones; misspelling of basic vocabulary; not capitalising ‘I’; missing or random capitals; verb tenses.

**Design consequences:** (1) the **third bullet gets the most space**, because the report says that is the one candidates drop; (2) the model is a **talk from the first word to the last** — greeting, direct address, audience-facing questions, a closing call to action — and never becomes a destination review; (3) it uses **self-deprecating humour and a personal failure** as the report's own example of strong AO4 does; (4) it stays **informal but not social-media-informal**, since the report penalises transcription-style writing; (5) it uses the report's own three real-world challenges (language, street crime, needing a guide) alongside the texts' challenges; and (6) **nothing is quoted or closely reworded** from either text.

## [CONSTRUCTED, 30/30-TARGET] Constructed talk to peers

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content (MS p. 17), the verified band descriptors (pp. 18–20) and the report's Q8 commentary. It is aligned with the characteristics of the **verified 30/30 script at report pp. 32–35**, which was not readable. **Not** a Pearson script, **not** examiner-marked, **no score claimed.**

> **GO SOMEWHERE. ANYWHERE. (Ten minutes, I promise.)**
>
> Morning, everyone. Sit down, this won't take long, and I only want one thing from you by the end of it.
>
> Last summer I went somewhere I could not point to on a map before I booked it, and I came back unable to shut up about it. Not the flight, not the food poisoning — the bit where I stood at the top of a hill and genuinely could not remember what I had been worried about in June.
>
> **What you would see**
>
> I'm not going to sell you a destination, because the best one I've been to was chosen for me by a cheap ticket and a bad sense of direction. What I want to sell you is the twenty minutes after you arrive, when nothing works the way it does at home.
>
> Landscapes that do not look like anywhere else, for a start. Cliffs that turn gold in the sun and almost purple in the shade by two o'clock. Valleys so deep and so narrow that the track down them has been cut through solid rock, with a thin green thread of water at the bottom and palm trees growing out of it. Flat white salt-pans that look solid and absolutely are not — we found that out the hard way. Desert that goes on until the horizon stops being a thing you can find, where the wind erases your own tyre tracks while you are still looking at them.
>
> Towns, too, and this is the bit nobody tells you about. Driving into one out of the desert, the tower houses rise out of the haze and you would swear you were looking at a city skyline — until you get close and see they are plaster and mud brick and older than your country. Then you are inside, and it is noise and colour and drains and spices all at once, and then you turn a corner and there is a garden behind a wall with water running through it.
>
> And small places. Villages jammed under a cliff in permanent shade, where the children stare at you from doorways and the chickens get out of the way of your car. Boys leaping in and out of a pool that is an impossible blue-green. A village so high up that we drove into cloud to reach it, and came out of the mist in front of a walled town on a spike of rock that looked like something out of a story I read when I was seven.
>
> **What you would actually enjoy**
>
> Not the sights. The sights are the excuse.
>
> The contrast is what gets you. One hour you are in a market being overcharged in a language you don't speak, the next you are on a headland with a two-hundred-foot drop and the sea making a noise like applause below you, and the whole coastline is laid out like a blanket someone has just shaken out over a bed. The hills fold away in layers. Farmland, hedges, sheep dotted about like cream. A bay curled round like a horseshoe with a town tucked into it.
>
> Being alone on top of that is worth more than any of it. I sat on a bench that somebody had lugged up a hill for no reason except kindness, and for about ninety seconds the light came through the cloud in long slats and I thought, out loud, to nobody: this is perfect. Nothing happened. That was the whole event. It was the best afternoon of my year.
>
> And meeting people. Not tours — people. A man who drew us a map on the back of a receipt. A woman who fed us because we looked lost, which we were. You cannot plan those and you cannot buy them.
>
> **What will go wrong, and why you should go anyway**
>
> Here is the honest half, because you will find out anyway.
>
> Some of it is genuinely dangerous. There are roads that are gravel and dust with a drop on one side and no barrier on the other, and there are places where a car will sink if you drive across them, and there is weather that turns up out of nowhere and puts you in fog with twenty yards of visibility.
>
> Some of it is just uncomfortable. I have walked the longest mile of my life to see a monument that was not worth the walk, then walked another mile to see something I never found, and ended the day crawling under barbed wire with my bag on my head, bleeding slightly, and no idea where I was. I have been lost in three countries. I do not recommend it. I do not regret it.
>
> And some of it is the boring practical stuff, which is where people actually come unstuck: the language barrier, the risk of getting your phone nicked in a crowd, and the difference between hiring a good local guide and hiring a man with a car and an opinion. Check who you are getting in a vehicle with. Tell someone where you are going. Keep some cash in a different pocket from the rest of it.
>
> **So — one thing**
>
> You don't need a gap year and you don't need money. You need a Saturday and the willingness to be slightly uncomfortable for a few hours. Somewhere within an hour of here there is a place you have never been — a hill, a coastline, a town with a market and a church and absolutely nothing else. Go and look at it. Take the wrong turning. Get a bit lost, get a bit muddy, come back with one story that isn't about your phone.
>
> Because — and I mean this kindly — you have your whole life to be comfortable. You have got about six weeks before the summer is gone. Go somewhere.

*Word count: 989 words including title and subheadings. That is at the long end for a one-hour Section B answer; the report notes the “vast majority of candidates wrote long and detailed responses”, so length is not the aim — the third bullet's development is.*

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Topic:** the MS permits “other examples than those mentioned in the passages”, and the report confirms that a talk about **one destination** (Paris was common) can still reach all three bullets. A candidate may therefore argue for a single trip, a domestic trip, a city, a coast, or a range of places.
- **Seen and experienced:** anything from scenery, wildlife, food, architecture, festivals, transport, weather or people-watching — the MS list is explicitly not exhaustive.
- **Enjoyable:** the MS allows “being alone” as well as company, and “the effects of the weather” as well as sunshine; a candidate may argue that the enjoyment is in the planning, the food, the photography, or the relief of coming home.
- **Challenges:** the texts supply vehicle-swallowing sand, gravel tracks with a drop, mist, steep climbs, lost footpaths and barbed wire; the report adds language, street crime and guides; MS allows “dealing with the unexpected” — a catch-all that covers missed flights, illness, money and homesickness.
- **Unused Text One material:** the salt-flat crossing (“a vehicle might sink”); the Empty Quarter's size (a quarter of a million square miles of uninhabited desert); the “impossible from below that any vehicle could travel” winding roads; the Heraz terraces of millet and maize; the carved wooden gates opening inwards; “It is a magical place” as a closing judgement.
- **Unused Text Two material:** the collapsed land bridge that once joined Dorset to the Isle of Wight; Old Harry and Old Harry's Wife; the Ordnance Survey map and its absurd precision about where the writer is sitting; the obelisk and the Giant's Grave; Corfe Castle's jagged walls.
- **Register:** warm, funny, self-deprecating and informal is right; the report's caution is against **transcription-of-a-video** informality and against abandoning the talk form after the greeting, not against personality.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO1:** every bullet is developed with several distinct, text-derived points, and the third bullet (the one the report says is most often dropped) is the longest section, with four separate challenges plus practical mitigations; both texts are reworked into the speaker's own argument and no phrase is borrowed.
- **AO4:** the form is a talk throughout — greeting, audience address, direct questions, a personal anecdote that fails entertainingly, self-deprecating humour, informal but controlled register, and a closing call to action that names the audience's summer. It is not a review of a destination, an article or a story.
- **AO5:** varied punctuation (colons, dashes, semicolons, parentheses, correctly punctuated asides), deliberate short sentences for emphasis (“Nothing happened. That was the whole event.”), accurate paragraphing that follows the three bullets, and consistent tense control.
- **Not claimed:** this is not the 30/30 script. That script exists at report pp. 32–35 and could not be read; this model is written to the same descriptors by a different route.
