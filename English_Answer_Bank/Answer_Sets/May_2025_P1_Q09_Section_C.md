## Exact QP context — Q9 (printed p. 21)

*Form note:* the command word is **“Discuss”**, not “to what extent” and not “write an article”. There is **no audience named**, so — as the MS says — “the examiner is assumed to be the audience”. ## What this paper's own report says about Q9 (printed pp. 40–42)

The report is unusually specific, and its evidence changes what a strong answer should look like:

- The topic “was accessible and many candidates who chose this question had clear opinions. Almost without exception candidates argued that new opportunities do present new challenges.”
- Most “understood the nature of discursive writing and were able to use appropriate form, tone and register… put forward reasonable points and explain and justify them”. Ideas offered: “moving to a new school, starting university, trying something new, starting a new job or moving to a new country”.
- **More successful** candidates “adopted a persuasive and argumentative tone and had clearly been prepared to write this kind of response… a clear structure with a strong introduction and conclusion”. They “discussed a range of opportunities e.g. immigration, technological change, travel and the challenges these may bring”, gave “detailed examples… e.g. having to learn a new language and make new friends if you’re going overseas to a new college”, and “developed a balanced and mature argument, often acknowledging that challenges are not always negative but may foster personal development”. They used “well-integrated anecdotes or hypothetical examples”, an “engaging, confident voice”, and “a good range of rhetorical techniques… the use of questions, expert opinion, anecdotes and listing” designed “to convince the reader”, and “generally came to a more balanced judgement”.
- **Less successful** candidates “had problems with both maintaining a clear argument and structuring their responses”, were “one sided or poorly supported”, “repetitive… brief and undeveloped”, or vague; “Some wrote narrative responses.”
- The report names **scaffolded phrasing it penalises in effect**: “Some opened with a philosophical quotation or with their ‘most crucial reason.’ A few scripts used phrases that had been pre-taught… ‘I am going to be making strong and robust points on my stance’, ‘these points fall short of the mark because …’, ‘as the dust settles, where do we find ourselves?’ and the phrase ‘indubitably exigent’… These responses do not allow candidates to fully demonstrate their own writing skills.”

**Consequences for the model:** name a *range* of opportunities (not one anecdote stretched over 400 words); keep a visible argumentative spine; concede and then answer the concession; use rhetoric that does work rather than decoration; and **avoid every one of the quoted stock phrases above**.

## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed discursive answer

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 21) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 22–23), with method taken from this paper's own report (pp. 40–42). **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **New opportunities present new challenges**
>
> A place at a university four hundred miles from home, a promotion, a second chance at a subject you failed: each of these is handed to us wrapped in the same paper, and inside is a difficulty we did not order. The statement is true, then — but it is true in the way that “rain makes you wet” is true. The interesting question is not whether challenges follow opportunity, but who carries them, and whether we would be better off without them.
>
> Consider the most common version of the claim. A student wins a scholarship to a school where nobody knows her. She has gained a library, a laboratory, teachers who have published books; she has also lost the friend who used to walk home with her, the accent everyone found funny, the comfortable business of being the cleverest person in the room. Two challenges arrive with that one opportunity: the practical — a new curriculum, a longer journey, a different set of expectations — and the quieter one, the business of becoming someone slightly unfamiliar to herself. Both are real, and only one of them will ever show up on a timetable.
>
> The same doubling appears wherever opportunity is distributed. Immigration offers work and safety and asks, in return, for a new language learned in the evenings after a shift and a new set of friendships built from nothing. Technology offers a workshop in a pocket and asks for the patience to keep retraining as the tools change beneath us. Travel widens a person and then charges interest: the discomfort of being illiterate in a country you love, the small humiliations of ordering the wrong food and laughing about it.
>
> Does that mean we should refuse? A cautious life is not a stupid life. There are opportunities that are simply too expensive — too much money, too much of the wrong kind of risk, too great a cost to people who never agreed to pay it. A student who turns down a place she cannot afford is not timid; she is accurate.
>
> But notice what the cautious argument quietly assumes: that the safe option is stationary. It is not. A person who never leaves the town they grew up in still meets a changed economy, a changed street, a changed body. Challenges are not a fee charged only to the adventurous; they are the weather. Given that, the sensible question is not how to avoid difficulty but how to choose the difficulty that teaches you something. The scholarship student who is lonely in October is a more capable person by June, and not because loneliness is noble, but because she has learned how to build a life out of unfamiliar parts.
>
> So I agree with the statement, with one correction. New opportunities do present new challenges; more often, they present the same old challenge — that growth is uncomfortable and cannot be faked — in a new costume. The opportunity is not the reward for enduring the challenge. It is the door the challenge is standing in.
>

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Stance:** full agreement, full disagreement, or qualified (as here). MS: “free to agree or disagree”. The report notes near-universal agreement, so a well-argued **disagreement** is not penalised and may read as fresher.
- **Opportunities:** moving school; starting university; a first job; migration; technological change; travel; a family change; sporting selection; an unexpected responsibility (a caring role, a business started young).
- **Challenges:** practical (cost, language, commute, workload) and personal (identity, friendship, confidence, failure) — pairing one of each is the quickest route to a “balanced and mature” argument.
- **Register:** first person, examiner as audience (MS). A speech register is possible but must be *sustained*; a titled essay or a blog post is equally acceptable if consistent.
- **What to avoid:** narrative (“Some wrote narrative responses”); one anecdote inflated to 400 words; unsupported assertion; the four stock phrases the report names; a conclusion that merely repeats the introduction.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO4:** a stated position that develops rather than repeats, two registers of challenge (practical/personal), a real concession answered in the next paragraph, and a closing judgement that refines the thesis.
- **AO5:** deliberate sentence-length variation, a colon for definitional emphasis, semicolons for balance, one memorable metaphor sustained (“the door the challenge is standing in”), and no over-ambitious vocabulary used at half-strength.
