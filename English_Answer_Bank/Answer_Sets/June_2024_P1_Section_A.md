### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Reddy begins by asking whether humans are “living in harmony with nature”. This question makes readers examine their relationship with the environment. The self-critical phrase “path of self-destruction” and the active verb “destroying” attribute the damage to human choices; the list of emissions, deforestation and land destruction makes the problem seem broad, while “the very home that shelters us” makes the planet feel personal and vulnerable.

She then acknowledges that teenagers may feel “helpless and powerless” because governments and large businesses make important decisions. The contrast introduced by “While” turns that feeling into a reason for action: even if major change depends on institutions, “we as individuals also have the potential to make a difference”. The inclusive “we” and direct “you” share responsibility with the reader, while “potential” and “make a difference” offer reassurance rather than blame. The promise that there are “some ways” to “take control” presents action as possible.

The numbered subheadings organise the article into practical steps, making the advice easy to locate and follow. Reddy gives examples relevant to young people, such as beach clean-ups, fundraising and taking shorter showers. Repeatedly calling actions “simple” and describing how people can “effortlessly” change habits makes the response seem manageable. The statistic that “only 0.03% of the 70% of water available to us is fresh water” gives a precise, surprising reason to conserve it. Terms such as “eco-friendly”, “carbon footprint”, “habitats” and “ecosystems” signal environmental knowledge, but the writer also explains the issues plainly—for example, how plastic harms land and marine life. Overall, the article combines a serious account of human damage with accessible choices, so young readers are encouraged to see themselves as capable participants in protecting the environment.

**MS alignment check:** This answer uses the opening question and negative framing, the shift from institutional to individual agency, accessible examples, numbered subheadings, statistics, environmental vocabulary and clear explanations listed in the indicative content (MS p. 5). It is written to the AO2 Level 5 descriptor—perceptive analysis supported by discriminating references (MS p. 6)—but is not a checklist response and has not been awarded a mark.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Thunberg first shows how the phrase “climate change” can sound harmless. The clipped opening—“Climate change. It doesn’t sound that bad.”—breaks the phrase into short sentences, while “pleasantly” and “appealing possibility” echo the reassuring associations people may attach to the word “change”. The question “What’s not to like?” briefly voices that complacent response. The abrupt “But” then reverses it: “It is destabilising. It is breaking down.” These short declarations replace the earlier calm with a sense of crisis. Repeating and explaining “tipping points, points of no return” makes the risk clearer, while “awfully close” suggests urgency even though the exact timing is unknown.

Images of melting ice and the “doomsday glaciers” make the consequences seem severe; “doomsday” carries an apocalyptic implication. The “snowball” metaphor suggests that, once a process is moving, it may become difficult to stop. Thunberg then lists wildfires, hurricanes, heatwaves, floods, storms and droughts, making the effects feel widespread. “On steroids” is an informal, forceful way to suggest intensified weather, and the repeated “more” and “less” in the next sentences presents change as increasingly extreme and out of control. The rejection of the phrase “new normal” argues that these events are not a stable endpoint.

The contrast between a crisis imagined in the “faraway future” and one happening “right here and right now” removes the comfort of delay; repeating “right here” and “right now” makes the closing claim emphatic. The bathtub analogy turns an enormous climate problem into an everyday choice: when it is about to overflow, the writer says to turn off the tap rather than search for buckets or cover the floor with towels. Finally, “We are approaching a precipice” creates a vivid image of danger. Direct commands—“stand our ground” and “Do not let them drag us”—and the emphatic “Not one inch” make the ending sound like a call to resist further delay. The repeated “Right here, right now” leaves readers with an immediate demand for action.

**MS alignment check:** This answer analyses the opening sentence pattern, reassuring language, rhetorical question, destabilising diction, tipping-point and snowball imagery, disaster list, repetition, temporal contrast, bathtub metaphor and final precipice/imperatives identified in the indicative content (MS pp. 8–9). It is aligned with AO2’s Level 5 descriptor but has not been awarded a mark.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers present climate change as a serious human problem and try to influence their readers, but they differ in emphasis and tone. Reddy’s blog is directed at young people and concentrates on actions they can take. Its opening question, “are we living in harmony with nature?”, introduces the problem, then the numbered subheadings turn the rest of the article into practical advice. The assurance that “we as individuals” can “make a difference” and the description of recycling as “simple, yet effective” make the response sound accessible and generally optimistic.

Thunberg’s book extract instead concentrates on the scale and urgency of the crisis. At first, the phrase “What’s not to like?” represents a casual misunderstanding; it is quickly replaced by the stark words “destabilising” and “breaking down”. Her list of extreme weather and images of “doomsday glaciers” and a “precipice” make the threat sound more alarming than Reddy’s practical, explanatory approach. Thunberg does not provide a numbered list of everyday solutions; she uses the bathtub analogy and commands such as “Do not let them drag us” to argue that action is urgent. Reddy’s article is mainly advisory, whereas Thunberg’s is informative and persuasive through warning.

The difference is not absolute. Reddy gives detailed explanations of environmental damage, including plastic pollution, and Thunberg briefly allows “a small chance of avoiding” irreversible change. Both use direct address and inclusive language to involve readers, both rely on questions, and both stress the need to act. Their structures serve their distinct purposes: Reddy’s subheadings make individual actions easy to find, while Thunberg’s continuous argument moves from misleading reassurance to present danger and a forceful final call. Text One offers readers manageable steps; Text Two insists that delay is dangerous.

**MS alignment check:** The comparison is balanced across both texts and covers audience, purpose, focus, tone, structure and persuasive methods. It draws on the MS’s advisory/action-focused Text One, problem-focused and urgent Text Two, their different tones, and their shared emphasis on action (MS pp. 10–11). The AO3 Level 5 descriptor requires a varied, comprehensive range of comparisons and balanced, discriminating references (MS p. 12); this is an original worked answer, not a score prediction.
