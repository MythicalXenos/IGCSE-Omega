## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed letter to a friend

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content (printed p. 13) and the verified band descriptors (pp. 16–18). **No report or script exists in any accessible route for this paper**, so this is MS-guided only. **Not** a Pearson script, **not** examiner-marked, **no score claimed.**

> Dear Priya,
>
> You asked me — over voice notes, which is either the most modern thing about either of us or the most old-fashioned — whether manners are dead. I've been thinking about it all week. Short answer: no, but they've moved, and nobody gave the older half of the world the new address.
>
> **The good news first**
>
> Because there is a lot of it, and we forget that. I can talk to my cousin in Dhaka at eleven at night and it costs nothing and takes no planning. I can send you a photo of something stupid I saw on the bus and you can reply with an even stupider one within four seconds. That is not a small thing. Two hundred years ago a letter took three weeks and half the people involved couldn't read or write.
>
> And here's the part your grandmother might not believe: the rules have actually loosened in a good way. Nobody is going to faint because I wore trainers to something. Nobody's family name decides how they get spoken to. We're a more mixed, more tolerant bunch than the generation that thought a bare head in church was scandalous, and some of the etiquette that got dropped deserved to be dropped.
>
> **The bit I'm less sure about**
>
> We are permanently on call. That's the real change, isn't it, and it isn't really about politeness at all. Thirty years ago ringing someone at half nine at night was a mild scandal; now we answer messages at midnight under the duvet and think nothing of it, and then wonder why we're tired. My brother gets work messages on a Sunday. Nobody considers that rude. It is, though.
>
> And we're terrible company now. You know the poll — the one where most young women and a majority of young men admitted checking their feeds at least ten times while they were out with friends. Ten. I am not pretending I'm above it. I sat next to someone at a birthday dinner and we both spent the main course in silence, thumbing through other people's holidays.
>
> The other thing nobody says out loud is that the old rules weren't all silly. Writing a thank-you note by hand took twenty minutes and told someone you meant it. A text takes ten seconds and tells someone you meant it for ten seconds. Both are valid; one is warmer. And once you've posted something, you can't unpost it — it goes into the great permanent record and it sits there while you grow up in front of it.
>
> **What I actually do now, in case you want the list**
>
> Three things.
>
> Phone in the other room while I'm eating or doing anything with a human being in it. Out of sight, not face down on the table — face down still means I'm watching it.
>
> Nothing goes out after eleven at night, and nothing goes out that I wouldn't say to the person's face. This one rule has saved me twice.
>
> And when I'm with somebody older, I give them the benefit of the assumption that they mean well and I mean well, and we're just speaking different dialects. My aunt thought my full stops were aggressive. Apparently a full stop at the end of a one-line reply now reads as being annoyed. I've stopped using them with her. That's not surrender — that's manners, which was always just noticing what makes another person comfortable.
>
> Underneath all of it, though, the rule hasn't changed and it isn't going to. Turn up when you said you would. Reply when someone's waiting. Hold the door. Don't talk over people. Say thank you and mean it.
>
> So — manners aren't dead. They're just being rewritten by people who never got handed the old edition.
>
> Write back. Properly. With punctuation, even if it makes you look furious.
>
> Love from your (mostly) well-behaved friend,
>
> Sam

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Positive:** MS explicitly allows “it can be genuine”, “it can be honest”, “respect/thoughtfulness/kindness can all be shown” and “Gen Z's texts are as respectful as older generations' communication” — a candidate may therefore argue that modern manners are *better*, not merely different, and may defend emojis, lowercase and voice notes as polite forms in their own right.
- **Problems:** alongside the MS list, candidates may use the texts' own material — the Bookatable finding that a third of parents hand children devices at meals; the two-income household and the pandemic as causes of eroded practice; increased mobility reducing collective responsibility; the loss of the “I'll tell your mother” sanction.
- **Advice:** the MS's nine points are all short and combinable; “teach children how to behave online” and “be aware of digital footprint” support a paragraph addressed to a friend with younger siblings, which is a natural register move in a letter.
- **Unused Text One material:** “netiquette” as a portmanteau of network and etiquette; “with new opportunities come new responsibilities”; the suggestion of airplane mode in the evenings; modelling behaviour for children; the digital footprint visible to teachers, friends' parents and employers.
- **Unused Text Two material:** the opening questions about party RSVPs and holding doors; the argument that mobility and the loss of neighbourhood knowledge lower the “cost” of bad manners; the claim that punctuation-free texting is a *more* precise system invented by digital natives; “this generational shift… doesn't necessarily equate to a decline in respect or kindness”.
- **Form:** a letter to a friend may be warm, teasing, self-deprecating and personal; it should not become an article with a headline, or a speech with “Good morning, everyone”.
