## Exact QP context — Q11 (printed p. 21)

> **11** Describe a beautiful place.
> (Total for Question 11 = 30 marks)

Same rubric: one question; 1 hour; no re-telling of the source texts; approximately 400 words.

*Form note:* “**Describe**” is the only command. There is no “and explain why”, no “write about a visit”, and no audience. The temptation — which this paper's report says many candidates took — is to write the story of being in a beautiful place rather than the place itself.

>

>

**AO4 (MS printed p. 22):** L4 13–16; **L5 17–20** perceptive and subtle, sharply focused, sophisticated form/tone/register.
**AO5 (MS printed p. 23):** L5 9–10 — as above, with punctuation “to aid emphasis and precision”.

**Note what the MS asks for and does not ask for:** “a sense of **place and atmosphere**”. It does not require the place to be famous, exotic, or even pleasant-looking; it requires the writing to make a reader feel it.

## What this paper's own report says about Q11 (printed pp. 53–55)

Because this is the paper's own report, it is the most authoritative Q11 evidence in the bank and is quoted closely:

- “Some candidates produced well-written responses that were fully focused on the task of describing a beautiful place. Examiners commented that many candidates did focus on description, however examiners also commented that **some candidates did not focus on the descriptive nature of the task and wrote narrative responses** focusing [on] how or why they were in the beautiful place.”
- Range chosen: “beaches e.g. Cox’s Bazaar, mountains e.g. the Himalayas or forests… cities e.g. Dubai or Paris… personal settings e.g. a grandparent’s garden, a favourite park… exotic or unfamiliar places, often inspired by reading or travel.”
- Most candidates “offer[ed] some visual detail… organize writing into clear paragraphs, often moving from setting description to personal feeling or mood. They attempted to create atmosphere, although not always sustained.” Some responses “were **too narrative, with pockets of description**.”
- **More successful:** “a focused and sustained attempt at crafting a piece of description… employed a range of techniques appropriately, creating a vivid picture… using the different senses… These responses had flair and enthusiasm, with effective imagery, creating mood and atmosphere… an impressive range of vocabulary… **stay[ing] sharply focused on the place itself**, describing it in detail and looking at the reasons why it was significant. Some candidates chose a place that was beautiful in a way that was deeply personal to them and described it considering different aspects, interacting with the place in different ways and evoking a sense of how they felt when in the place.”
- **Less successful:** “often struggled to describe the beautiful place and were often narrative. They tended to **list features of a place without developing the description**. They **repeated adjectives like ‘nice,’ ‘beautiful,’ ‘amazing,’ without exploring what made the place beautiful**. Some wrote about beautiful places in general or included several examples of places, sometimes listing popular tourist destinations… without much description.”
- **AO5:** “there was a **lack of variety in the use of punctuation**” plus “over-ambitious vocabulary which was not effective or appropriate”, paragraphing problems and the standard error list.

**Consequences for the model:** one place, one vantage point, **no plot**; description developed by *returning* to the same details with new meaning rather than by adding new sights; all five senses; **not one** loose evaluative adjective left unexplained; and effects on the observer present but subordinate to the place, because the report both praises “how they felt” and rejects description subordinated to narrative.

## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed description

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 21), the AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 22–23) and this paper's own report (pp. 53–55). **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **A roof in Old Dhaka**
>
> The roof of my grandmother’s house is reached by a stair too narrow for two people to pass on, and it is less a roof than a field: patched concrete, a low parapet, a water tank, and a jasmine she planted in a paint tin thirty years ago which has since become a conspiracy of green over the pipe.
>
> At half past four it is still nearly dark. The city below is a sound before it is a sight — a ceiling fan starting somewhere, a tin shutter coming down, the first bus grinding its way up from the river — and the sky has the thin, dusty grey of a lamp left on in an empty room.
>
> Then the light arrives all at once, the way it does here: not a sunrise so much as someone lifting a lid. The parapet turns from grey to peach. The white dome of the mosque appears, then the pink one behind it, then the whole jumble of tin and concrete roofs, each with its own clothesline, its own caged plants, its own clutter of tanks, and for about ten minutes everything is the colour of tea with too much milk in it.
>
> Nothing happens. That is the point of it. A man in a lungi waters four pots on the roof opposite with the care of someone performing surgery. Two crows argue over a shrine of rubbish. Below, in the lane, the sweepers make their slow noise, and the dust they raise smells of old rain and marigolds and, faintly, of the drain.
>
> By seven the heat has arrived and the roof changes character: the concrete gives it back at your feet through your sandals, the jasmine smells stronger for being warm, and the calls from the mosque on the corner go out over the rooftops and come back off the tin, so that the same voice seems to be answered by a second, thinner one.
>
> My grandmother brings her tea up here at eight and sits in a plastic chair with her back to the dome. She has done it every morning since my grandfather died, and she does not look at the view while she drinks; she looks at the jasmine. I have asked her why. She says the roof is the only place in the house where nobody is allowed to want anything from her.
>
> That is what makes the place beautiful, in the end. Not the light, though the light is extraordinary at half past four. Not the domes, or the tea-coloured roofs, or the crows. It is that the roof is hers, and that she has given it to me for one hour a day — a place that asks nothing, where the whole loud, crowded, arguing city is laid out below like something that has finally been put down.
>

*Word count: 470 words of body text (475 including the title line).*

## Craft map — how the description meets the descriptors

| Element | Where | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| One place, one fixed vantage point | The roof throughout; the same three objects (parapet, water tank, jasmine) recur | Report: “stay sharply focused on the place itself”; warns against “beautiful places in general” and lists of several destinations |
| No plot | Time passes; nobody arrives; nothing is resolved | The central failure the report names: “did not focus on the descriptive nature of the task and wrote narrative responses” |
| All five senses | Sight (peach parapet, tea-coloured roofs); sound (fan, shutter, bus, crows, sweepers, the doubled call to prayer); smell (dust, old rain, marigolds, warm jasmine); touch (concrete through sandals); taste (the tea) | Report: successful responses used “the different senses” |
| Atmosphere developed, not asserted | The sky “like a lamp left on in an empty room”; the colour metaphor repeated and varied | Report: “effective imagery, creating mood and atmosphere”; rejects unsupported “amazing/beautiful” |
| No bare evaluative adjectives | “beautiful” appears twice, each time followed by the reason; the light is “extraordinary at half past four” — specific | Report: weaker answers “repeated adjectives like ‘nice,’ ‘beautiful,’ ‘amazing,’ without exploring what made the place beautiful” |
| Development by return, not by addition | Jasmine: planted in a paint tin → warm and stronger → the thing the grandmother actually looks at | The examiner note on the 18/30 script: “more detail would have improved this response”; depth beats breadth |

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Places (MS):** house, bedroom; “religious/ceremonial buildings, museums, parks, beaches or the countryside”; MS text also opens the door to imaginary places.
- **Structure:** one moment; a day; a year of seasons; arrival and departure with no events between (the safest shape for AO4).
- **How much feeling:** the report both praises “evoking a sense of how they felt” and criticises narrative dominance. The safe balance is a place-dominant piece with one short reflective movement, as here — not a feeling-dominant piece with scenery attached.
- **What to avoid:** a story with characters and events; several places compared; a travel-brochure list; “paradise”, “breathtaking”, “picture-perfect” without evidence; describing why you went there.
- **Q9/Q10 alternative readings of the same material:** the roof could equally be reworked for Q10 as the setting of a story, but that changes the form and would need a plot; it must not simply be reused.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO4:** sustained descriptive mode with a single, sharply focused subject; atmosphere built through recurring detail and light rather than through adjectives; the significance of the place established without turning into anecdote.
- **AO5:** punctuation used structurally (semicolon sense-lists, dash asides, colon), rhythm varied from four-word sentences to long accumulating ones, and a vocabulary that is concrete rather than ornamental.
- **Not claimed:** it is not the report's 18/30 script, and it cannot be compared with it; the report's Q11 exemplar is image-only. The 18/30 record is used here for its *ceiling* lesson, not as a language model.
