> **The word that gives it away**
>
> There is a version of this sentence that is true and a version that is nonsense, and the difference is one word.
>
> The true version is that we often regret the chances we do not take. That is easy to demonstrate. My uncle has kept a letter for twenty-six years — an offer of a job in another country, which he turned down because his father was ill — and he has never once opened the envelope again, and he has mentioned it perhaps four times in my hearing, always in the same flat tone, as though he were reading out a bus timetable. He does not regret staying. He regrets not knowing what staying cost him. That is the honest shape of the thing: the regret is usually about information we never got, not about a life we never lived.
>
> But the sentence says *only*. We only regret the chances we do not take. That is not true, and anybody who has taken a real chance knows why. People regret chances they took all the time — the business that failed and took a marriage with it, the move that turned out to be a mistake, the words said in a moment of courage that could not be unsaid. The statement confuses regret with cowardice, and they are not the same. Regret after action is expensive and specific; regret before action is cheap and vague.
>
> There is also the small matter of reasons. Some chances should not be taken, and the person who declines them is not timid but prudent. A student with a sick parent at home may be right to turn down a place abroad. A driver may be right not to overtake. The statement's optimism is generous, but it has no room in it for a person with responsibilities, and most of us become that person eventually.
>
> So, to what extent do I agree? Halfway, and on a condition. I agree that inaction leaves a particular kind of ache, because it is the ache of a story that never got written — and stories we invent about unchosen lives are always better than the lives themselves. I disagree that this is the only regret we have, and I disagree that taking chances is therefore always braver. Courage is not the same as motion.
>
> What I would actually tell a person deciding is this: count the cost, and then notice which way the cost lies. If the chance is refused out of fear alone, take it. If it is refused because someone else is standing behind you, the refusal is a choice too, and it deserves the same seriousness as the leap.
