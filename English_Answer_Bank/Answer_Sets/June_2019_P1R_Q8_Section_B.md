## What the June 2019 P1R report adds

Quoted from the report’s Section B (Question 8) commentary:

- **AO1 — the first bullet is where candidates fell down.** “Some candidates failed to address the first bullet point (different types of organisations or campaigns) and it was occasionally treated quite superficially although stronger candidates were able to integrate their own examples such as local charities, the Red Cross and UNICEF showing personal engagement.” The second bullet “was sometimes not covered in sufficient detail”, with points ranging from “the practical ‘fill a form in on the internet’ to more abstract comments about making decisions and developing independence and resilience”. For the third, candidates “commented on the negative perception of young people and how participation in such campaigns was character building… it was necessary to prove that young people were responsible individuals in their own right.” “Less able candidates wrote about just one bullet point, or only commented briefly on the second and/or third. Some lifted information directly from the texts or only considered only one text.”
- **AO4 — tone is the trap on this paper.** “Stronger candidates used rhetorical and persuasive techniques and established a sense of the friendly relationship with the recipient. Many candidates used an introductory paragraph devoted to establishing that this was a letter and most candidates sustained an appropriate register for a letter. **Sometimes the letters were too formal given the audience was a friend.**” Weaker responses “only acknowledged the register at the beginning and ending”, or “were more like recruitment leaflets or articles than a personal letter”.
- **AO5 — named errors:** “missing out definite and indefinite articles; missing out parts of verbs; incorrect subject/verb agreement; comma splicing; lack of capital letters, especially for ‘I’ and sometimes at the start of sentences.” Paragraphing was “generally handled well”.

## [CONSTRUCTED GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed model letter

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content and the 2019 P1R report guidance, using verified details from both texts. It is **not** a Pearson script, has **not** been examiner-marked, and **no score is claimed**. It opens by establishing the letter as a letter, uses “you” throughout, keeps contractions and asides natural for a friend, and still covers all three bullets at length.

> Hey Sam,
>
> You asked me last week why I keep disappearing on Saturday mornings, and I gave you a rubbish answer about “helping out”. Here it is properly. I’ve started volunteering with a small anti-bullying project that works with our local primary school, and I want you to come with me.
>
> First, because there is more out there than people think. The one I’m doing is tiny — six of us, mostly Year 10s — but it is part of something bigger: the charity behind it supports young people who have been bullied about how they look, which is where a lot of appearance-based discrimination starts. If that does not appeal, there is genuinely something else. There are national campaigns you can sign up to online in about ten minutes, and local ones that need people on a Saturday. The projects I keep coming back to are the ones that started small: a girl in Rhode Island set up a group to argue about how the media portrays girls, a boy in Florida began collecting food for hungry families in Miami when he was six, and a seven-year-old called Ryan raised the money for a well in Uganda and kept going for years. None of them waited to be asked.
>
> Second, how you actually take part, because this is the bit I got wrong at first. Pick something you already care about — if you don’t care, you won’t last three weeks, and that is the honest truth. Then start small. Read about it, follow a couple of organisations, and go to one meeting before you promise anything. Ask what the small decisions are, because being trusted with those is how you get trusted with the bigger ones. Use the internet properly rather than just scrolling: that is how the Ice Bucket Challenge raised more than a hundred million dollars, from one person’s idea. And then keep your word. Turning up when it is boring and following through is what makes adults take you seriously, which is exactly the point.
>
> Third — and I would rather tell you this than pretend otherwise — it is sometimes hard to take part. Some of it is practical. There is not always an opportunity near you, and half of us have no idea what is even possible, because nobody has ever shown us. Some of it is other people. There is a genuinely negative attitude about young people in this country: the assumption is that we are destructive, that we do not care, that we contribute nothing positive. Ellie Jones, who won a Diana Award for her work with a facial-disfigurement charity, calls that the main barrier, and she is right that it becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy — if everyone expects you to be a problem, it starts to feel like there is no point in trying. I have felt that. It is also, honestly, just embarrassing to say out loud that you want to change something, in case your friends laugh or in case you fail at it.
>
> But here is the thing she said that stayed with me: “young people have a lot more power than they realise”, and “don’t stop screaming until they hear you.” She also warned that it can feel like “you’re ramming your head into a brick wall” — and then you get there anyway. So come once. If you hate it, you never have to come again, and I will not mention it. If you don’t hate it, we have a Saturday and a stupid in-joke and something to show for it.
>
> Text me back before Friday, yeah? I’ll meet you by the gates at nine.
>
> Alfie
