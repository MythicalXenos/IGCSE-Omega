### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Andrews begins with successful finds: Derek McLennan’s Viking hoard, Roman coins in East Yorkshire and 50,000 Celtic coins worth £10 million. The precise sums and the claim that almost 1.3 million discoveries were made since the Treasure Act began make treasure hunting sound real and productive rather than merely legendary. The sentence “Buried treasure is not only real, but still being uncovered at a fast rate in Britain” states the article’s positive starting point. The paired questions “how do you find buried treasure and can you keep it if you do?” then turn the opening into a promise of practical advice and introduce the legal issue.

The numbered headings—“Step 1 – get equipped”, “Step 2 – get permission” and “Step 3 – look after what you find”—organise the article as a sequence the reader can follow. Andrews uses direct address and advice such as “you need a metal detector” and “You should probably pack a small shovel too”, making the guidance sound accessible and actionable. Technical details about detector depth, prices and using a GPS unit add specificity. The question “You’ve heard the tell-tale beep, dug up a purse of gold and then... What exactly?” uses a familiar sound, direct address and an ellipsis to let the reader imagine a successful find before shifting attention to how it should be handled.

The writer also presents treasure hunting as exciting but regulated. The heading “Step 4 – report it” is a blunt instruction, followed by the warning of an “unlimited fine” and up to three months in prison. The example of a former policeman jailed for 16 months makes the risk concrete. References to the Treasure Valuation Committee and the Treasure Act give the explanation an official, factual basis. The later subheading “What counts as treasure then?” uses a conversational question to introduce a list of legal definitions, breaking technical information into manageable points.

Finally, “Where to look for treasure” moves from rules to location advice. The contrast between city searches, described as “a bit of a lost cause”, and finds in the countryside narrows the reader’s practical options; the figure of 1,292 Norfolk discoveries adds a specific lead. Overall, numbered sections, questions, direct address, statistics and legal examples let Andrews present treasure hunting as both achievable and something that requires sensible, lawful preparation.

**MS alignment check:** This response develops the MS’s points about successful examples/statistics, the positive opening, direct address, questions, numbered subheadings, equipment advice, legal requirements and locations (indicative content, MS p. 6). The AO2 Level 5 descriptor requires perceptive analysis with discriminating references (MS p. 7); the indicative list is not exhaustive. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Seal begins with a sensational claim about a lost Nazi gold train, then immediately qualifies it: “At least, that’s what a couple of treasure hunters told the world last year.” The phrase “At least” signals doubt, and the contrast with searches that have gone on “for many years, usually without success” makes the story seem unreliable. Calling the claims “far-fetched stories” reveals the writer’s scepticism from the outset.

The account of the supposed train is followed by the treasure hunters’ claim that they have found it. Seal describes them as “the raiders of the lost Nazi treasure”, echoing the film *Raiders of the Lost Ark* and making the episode sound like an adventure story. The response “Enter the spoilsport scientists” uses an alliterative, theatrical entrance; the word “spoilsport” lightly mocks the hunters’ confidence. The italicised “Their” in “Their techno gadgets” contrasts with the hunters’ “latest techno gadgets”: the scientists’ equipment, rather than the treasure seekers’, delivers the decisive test. The short fragment “Just empty tunnels” abruptly punctures the dramatic claim and makes the failure sound final.

Seal then broadens his argument through examples such as the lost city of Z, Lasseter’s Reef and the Beale ciphers. Repeated references to maps, myths and unsuccessful searches make treasure hunting seem driven by stories that are difficult to verify. The Indiana Jones reference links these legends to popular adventure fiction, reinforcing the gap between romantic expectation and evidence. At the same time, the writer acknowledges why the myths persist: “people love a lost treasure story”, even after science has disproved a particular claim.

The closing paragraph briefly shifts direction. “Every now and then one of these expeditions actually strikes gold” introduces genuine recoveries, including a Spanish treasure ship and a 500-year-old shipwreck. The metaphor “stoke the fires of hope” shows how rare successes sustain belief. The final line, “It might just be that the legend is true…”, ends with an ellipsis that leaves the possibility open. Seal is mostly sceptical, but the ending acknowledges the small chance that keeps the search alive.

**MS alignment check:** This response covers the attention-grabbing opening, negative tone, doubtful language, the film allusion, the scientists’ evidence, sentence structure, myths/maps and the hopeful final contrast (indicative content, MS p. 9). The AO2 Level 5 descriptor is on MS p. 11. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Andrews presents treasure hunting as real and potentially rewarding, then explains how to pursue it responsibly. Seal is more sceptical: he describes the activity as commonly fuelled by legends and failed searches, although he admits that rare genuine discoveries keep people hopeful. Their perspectives differ, but both use concrete examples to make the subject vivid.

Their openings establish that contrast. Andrews starts with named discoveries and exact sums—such as the Viking hoard and the 50,000 Celtic coins worth £10 million—before saying that buried treasure is still being uncovered. The figures make success seem substantial and support his practical tone. Seal opens with the supposed Nazi gold train but hedges immediately: “At least, that’s what a couple of treasure hunters told the world last year.” Where Andrews treats discoveries as evidence that the search can pay off, Seal’s “far-fetched stories” and “usually without success” make such claims sound doubtful.

The structures reflect their purposes. Andrews divides his article into numbered advice steps, including “Step 1 – get equipped” and “Step 2 – get permission”. Direct address—“you need” and “You should probably pack”—turns the text into guidance for the reader. Seal instead develops an account of claims and failed searches, then introduces the “spoilsport scientists” who expose the empty tunnels. The allusion to *Raiders of the Lost Ark* makes treasure seeking seem like a popular adventure story, while “Just empty tunnels” sharply undercuts that romantic image. Andrews’s conversational questions invite the reader to imagine finding treasure; Seal uses ironic phrasing to question the stories behind it.

The writers also differ in how they handle evidence and outcomes. Andrews gives legal details, equipment prices and the location statistic “1,292 discoveries” in Norfolk to make his advice usable. Seal lists legends such as the lost city of Z and Lasseter’s Reef, but ends by acknowledging real recoveries, including a Spanish treasure ship. His metaphor “stoke the fires of hope” recognises why those rare successes keep treasure hunters searching. Both articles combine facts and examples, yet Andrews uses them to encourage informed participation while Seal uses them mainly to expose the myth-making around treasure hunting.

The endings preserve the difference in emphasis. Andrews leaves the reader with practical places to look and advice on reporting finds; Seal ends, “It might just be that the legend is true…”, an open-ended possibility rather than a promise. One writer is chiefly advisory and positive, the other predominantly sceptical but not entirely dismissive.

**MS alignment check:** The comparison is sustained across both texts, addresses ideas and perspectives as well as method, and balances references. It develops the MS’s contrasts between Text One’s practical/positive advice and Text Two’s scepticism and mocking tone, while recognising Text Two’s final successful finds (indicative content, MS pp. 12–13). The AO3 Level 5 descriptor is on MS p. 14. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.
