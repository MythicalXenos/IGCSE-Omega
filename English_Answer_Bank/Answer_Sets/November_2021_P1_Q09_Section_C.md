> **The difference between a habit and a slogan**
>
> There are two ways to look on the bright side, and only one of them is worth defending. The first is a habit of attention: you notice what is still working. The second is a refusal to notice what is not, and it is sold to us constantly, in songs and on mugs, as though the two were the same thing. They are not, and the distance between them is where this question actually lives.
>
> Take the habit first, because it has the better evidence. Seeing the positives in life does seem to make us happier, and that is not a small thing; a person who can find one good hour in a bad week is a person who can get through the bad week. Such people are also, frankly, more pleasant to be around. Pessimists drag a room down. I have sat at tables where one person's certainty that everything will fail quietly cancelled everybody else's plans, and I have watched a single cheerful person un-cancel them. That is a real power and it costs nothing to admire it.
>
> But now take the slogan. “Always look on the bright side” is not advice about attention; it is an instruction about *reporting*. It tells you not to say what you can see. And here the mark scheme's own reservation is exactly right: happy people do not always see the full picture. Sometimes they are happy because they are not looking. If your roof is leaking and you spend the evening admiring the moonlight coming through it, you are not an optimist, you are a person who will be wet by Friday.
>
> I have seen the cost of that up close. When my aunt was ill, the ward was full of people being brave at each other, and the one relative who said, plainly, *this is very bad and I am frightened*, was the one everybody was secretly grateful for. She was not being negative. She was being accurate, and accuracy is a form of care.
>
> So: to what extent do I agree? Largely, but not without conditions. I would keep the first half of the sentence and lose the word *always*, because *always* is where a useful habit becomes a demand. Look on the bright side when you can, and when you cannot, say so — and then, having said so, look again. That is not pessimism. It is optimism with its eyes open, which is the only kind that survives contact with a real week.
