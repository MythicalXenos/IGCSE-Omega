> **The week before the rains**
>
> My favourite time of year is not a season so much as a fortnight: the last two weeks of the hot months, when the city is waiting.
>
> Everything in that fortnight is a colour that has been left out too long. The leaves on the roadside trees are not green but a dusty grey-green, and each one carries a rim of grit, and the whole avenue looks as though it has been photographed in sepia and then had the colour put back badly. The sky is not blue; it is a white-hot nothing with the sun somewhere inside it, and by eleven in the morning the shadows have gone small and hard and there is nowhere to stand.
>
> The heat has a texture. It sits on the back of your neck like a hand. Asphalt goes soft at the edges of the road, and the tar smells sweet and chemical, and the air coming off it moves the way air does above a stove. Everyone walks differently: slower, wider, along the shaded side, and nobody stands still for long because standing still is worse than walking.
>
> And then there are the sounds of waiting. The fans in the shop doorways, all running at once, making a low argument across the street. The ice-cream cart with its bell. The crows, which get loud and rude in that fortnight, and the kites, which are being flown from every roof by boys who are not supposed to be on the roof. In the evening the power falters — the lights dip and come back and dip again — and everyone in the building says the same sentence at the same time, in the dark, to nobody in particular.
>
> Then the first storm comes. It arrives from the north in the afternoon, and you can see it coming for twenty minutes: a wall of grey with a bruised underside, moving faster than a storm has any right to, and under it the dust lifting off the maidan in a long brown curtain. The wind hits the windows before the rain does. The temperature drops eight degrees in four minutes. And then the rain comes down so hard that the street is a river in the time it takes to close a shutter, and children are out in it, and the smell — that smell of hot dust being hit by water — comes up off the ground and through the whole house.
>
> That is the part I love: not the rain itself, but the twenty minutes before it, when the whole city knows something is about to happen and has not yet been told what. It is the only time of year when the weather is a promise rather than a fact, and I have never in my life been so interested in the sky as I am in that fortnight.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Times of year (report's list):** Christmas, a religious or cultural festival, a season, a birthday, a holiday. The MS allows any of these; the report says only that they invite narrative.
- **Structures:** a sequence of sensory impressions (as here); the same place in that season across three years; the season described by its sounds only; the last day of the season.
- **Keeping it descriptive:** present tense, no chronology, no itinerary, at most one short memory.
- **What to avoid:** “and then we went to my aunt's house and ate…” ; a list of presents; a travelogue; a season with no weather in it; a generic “it's the time for family” paragraph; anything that turns the answer into a story.
