### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[CONSTRUCTED MS-BASED RESPONSE]** *Original response based on Text One and the Results MS; not Pearson-scored. No mark is assigned.*

The opening image of “pale light” filtering through the window, with “nobody about” and only a “faint sound of a radio”, establishes a still, private atmosphere before the hotel itself is named. Brookner then gives the building human dignity: it is “a dignified building”, “a house of good reputation” and “a traditional establishment”. The phrase “well-to-do, the retired, the modest, the respected patrons of an earlier era of tourism” makes its intended guests sound established and high-status. Although the furnishings are “plain”, the linen is “spotless” and the service “impeccable”; this contrast presents modest appearance alongside exacting quality. The hotel is almost personified as a character with a past and its own standards. *(Text One, lines 1–10.)*

The description then turns the absence of attractions into a deliberate feature. The hotel takes “a peculiar pride in its very absence of attractions”, an ironic formulation because it treats a lack as a source of pride. The list of a “muted hush”, no piped music or public telephones, no sauna or hairdresser and a small, dark bar makes the hotel seem deliberately unlike a busy resort. Repeated negatives (“no sauna, no hairdresser”) reinforce this refusal of ordinary modern amenities. The account of housekeeping extends the quiet: the tricolon “all household noises had to be silenced; no vacuuming was heard, no carts of dirty linen were glimpsed” makes the rule seem comprehensive, and even the “discreet rustle” of the maids suggests that service is carefully hidden from guests. The dry line “Naturally, no attempt was made to entertain them” is ironic: a conventional hotel amenity is absent because these visitors value quiet and privacy. *(Text One, lines 11–22 and 23–38.)*

Brookner next explains what this restraint offers instead: “a safe haven, an assurance of privacy, and discretion”. The abstract three-part list reframes the hotel’s quiet as protection for guests who may need rest; it is “a restorative vacation for those whom life had mistreated or merely fatigued”. The hotel is “half empty” and serves a “mere handful” of visitors, but those few are treated as “treasured patrons”. The repetition of “knew it” in the list of doctors, solicitors and accountants makes its reputation sound selective and discreet; the blunt sentence “Travel agents did not know it” distances it from mass tourism. “And the word got round” is a short, understated ending to this passage about exclusivity. *(Text One, lines 21–38.)*

The setting and ending keep the praise qualified rather than extravagant. The lake is “agreeable”; the climate “was not brilliant” but “pleasant”; the town’s resources “were not extensive”; and the walking is “pleasant if unexciting”. These restrained evaluations make the hotel’s appeal sound calm and credible rather than spectacular. The image of scenery as if “painted in the watercolours of an earlier period” supports its old-fashioned, softened atmosphere. The young are said to “hurtle” towards sun and beaches, “jamming” roads and airports, while the hotel takes “quiet pride” in its “isolation from the herd”; the energetic verbs make the contrast with its measured retreat sharper. The references to “unwritten references” and the Huber family’s files suggest that the hotel can choose its guests. Finally, the salon—with its “deep blue carpet”, traditional armchairs and an elderly man playing “mild selections”—sounds warmer and more welcoming than the earlier description of her room led the narrator to expect. *(Text One, lines 39–55.)*

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[CONSTRUCTED MS-BASED RESPONSE]** *Original response based on Text Two and the Results MS; not Pearson-scored. No mark is assigned.*

Hudson begins by describing bad hotels generally: “bedbugs and blocked plumbing”, “dirty linen and broken furniture”, then mould, cigarette burns, faulty wiring and broken locks. These accumulating details create a vivid baseline for what readers might expect from a “worst hotel”. The alliterative “surly, sleazy staff” makes the imagined employees sound especially unpleasant. The contrast between the hotel “proudly proclaiming” itself the worst and the negative description is comic. Its advertising slogans—“it can’t get any worse… but we’ll do our best” and “Now a door with every room!”—use self-mocking humour to explain the campaign’s appeal. The rhetorical question about which “20-something” would not want to brag on social media makes the target audience explicit and encourages the reader to follow the writer’s investigation. *(Text Two, lines 1–12.)*

Her expectations become more specific through the repeated pattern “with … it”: with 550 beds, “it was bound to pack people in like sardines”; with the marketing, “it had to have indifferent staff”; with the nightclub, “it promised little sleep”. The parallel structure makes the fears sound like a reasoned list, while the simile conveys crowding. The adverb “Curiously”, however, signals a turn: the hotel sits in a “renovated” part of Amsterdam with fine art, antique shops, galleries and a deli. The location is already less dismal than the hotel’s branding suggests. *(Text Two, lines 13–22.)*

The first impression inside is still unpleasant. The “special stench” of stale beer and cigarette smoke will “seep” into clothes and hair and linger; the verbs make the smell seem invasive and persistent. A list of peeling posters, vending machines and people waiting makes the lobby sound cluttered. The writer is checked in “en masse” with five others, but the receptionist’s explanation—so she does not have to repeat herself—ends with the dry parenthetical “not exactly customer-focused, but time-efficient”. This aside combines criticism with humour and shows Hudson observing the service first-hand. *(Text Two, lines 21–29.)*

The room complicates the advertised picture. A list of basic furnishings (“two single beds, two lockers, a chair, and a rubbish bin”) confirms that it is a low-budget space, but “Surprisingly” introduces a small hand towel and a full roll of toilet paper. The walls look freshly painted; there are “working lights” and smoke detectors; the window opens for fresh air; and the view includes “blooming tulips”. The repeated “even” accumulates unexpected comforts. The narrator’s question, “Could this hotel really be that bad?”, makes her reassessment explicit; the garden view seems particularly incompatible with the claim that this is the world’s worst hotel. *(Text Two, lines 30–37.)*

The bar and checkout continue the reversal. More than a hundred guests sit together at picnic tables, and Hudson compares the scene to a “school cafeteria”; the young guests and “backpacker fare” make it lively and ordinary, not threatening. She admits that it is “fairly tame stuff” and an environment she “would have relished” on a first backpacking trip. At checkout, the free breakfast, guests who are “mostly happy” and the “minimum of fuss” retrieving the key deposit further soften her expectations. The ironic phrase “disappointed and underwhelmed” means that the hotel has failed to be as bad as promised. In the final judgement it is a “stock-standard low budget, run-down hostel”; the owners’ clever PR campaign, not terrible accommodation, is what makes it memorable. Hudson finishes with the joke, “I won’t tell a soul you’re just in another average backpacker hostel.” This keeps the review’s informal humour to the end. Structurally, the review moves from generic worst-hotel imagery to predictions about this particular stay, tests them against the lobby, room, bar and checkout, then closes with a verdict on the PR. Hudson’s first-person “I” and “we”, informal asides and rhetorical questions make the reversal feel like a personally witnessed experience rather than an abstract judgement. *(Text Two, lines 1–55.)*

**MS alignment check:** This uses the exact Text Two and develops the Results MS’s indicative points on the opening catalogue, alliteration, marketing quotations, rhetorical questions, the repeated expectation structure, the simile, the lobby, adverbs marking surprise, room amenities, the tulip view, bar, positive checkout, ironic judgement, first-person voice and informal register (MS p. 8). Q6 is assessed by AO2; Level 5 is 9–10 and calls for perceptive analysis with discriminating references (MS p. 9). The indicative list is not a checklist; this constructed response remains unmarked.

### Q7 — comparison of ideas, perspectives and methods

**[CONSTRUCTED MS-BASED RESPONSE]** *Original AO3 comparison based on both exact source texts and the Results MS; not Pearson-scored. No mark is assigned.*

The texts describe very different kinds of accommodation and do so from different perspectives. Text One is adapted from a novel and uses a third-person narrator to describe the Hotel du Lac with formal, measured language; Text Two is a non-fiction first-person review in which Hudson visits the Hans Brinker and tests its claim to be the “worst hotel in the world”. The first text is not a direct review: it lets details of the hotel and its established clientele convey its character. The second is openly evaluative, moving from the writer’s expectations to what she actually experiences. *(Text One heading and lines 1–10; Text Two heading and lines 1–18.)*

Their settings produce opposite atmospheres. The QP introduces Text One as a hotel in Switzerland; the passage places it on a lake, with a climate that is “pleasant”, walking that is “pleasant if unexciting”, and scenery compared to “watercolours of an earlier period”. Its “quiet pride” in being “isolated from the herd” makes it sound like a peaceful retreat. By contrast, the Hans Brinker stands in an Amsterdam neighbourhood with galleries, antique stores and bike shops; inside, over a hundred guests gather around picnic tables. The review’s city setting and communal bar feel lively, while the novel’s lakeside hotel is subdued and secluded. *(Text One introduction and lines 39–48; Text Two, lines 19–22 and 38–45.)*

The two writers also contrast amenities and standards. Brookner presents a dignified, traditional establishment with spotless linen and “impeccable” service, but it deliberately lacks a sauna, hairdresser, public phones and entertainment; the rarely encountered chambermaids reinforce its discreet service. Its offering is privacy, discretion and a “restorative vacation”, and it attracts only a “handful” of guests who seem to meet its standards. Hudson initially expects the Hans Brinker to be filthy, overcrowded and noisy; the room is basic, but fresh paint, working lights, a towel, toilet paper and a view of tulips make it more acceptable than its reputation. Thus the Hotel du Lac appears like a selective, high-quality retreat, whereas the Hans Brinker is an ordinary budget hostel whose real conditions are better than its “worst hotel” publicity suggests. *(Text One, lines 4–22 and 23–38; Text Two, lines 13–18 and 30–37.)*

Their approaches to reputation are almost opposites. The Hotel du Lac does little to court “passing trade” and relies on word of mouth among doctors, solicitors and accountants; travel agents “did not know it”. It will not accept a new client without “unwritten references” and a name already on the Huber family’s files, making its quiet reputation deliberately selective. The Hans Brinker’s owners, by contrast, run a “decades-long campaign” with humorous slogans; the campaign has been a “huge viral success”, aimed at young travellers who want to post about surviving the worst hotel. Brookner’s formal, sometimes ironic description makes the hotel seem selective and quietly self-assured; Hudson’s first-person voice, colloquial phrases and rhetorical questions make her account sound immediate and conversational. *(Text One, lines 5–10, 21–38 and 45–52; Text Two, lines 6–12 and 13–18.)*

The conclusions reinforce the difference. Text One treats the hotel’s quiet and discretion as valuable to people “whom life had mistreated or merely fatigued”, and ends by revealing a salon that is more agreeable than expected. At the Hans Brinker, a free breakfast and “mostly happy” guests contrast with the opening promise of a terrible stay; Hudson and her companions leave “disappointed and underwhelmed” because the hostel is merely ordinary, not as bad as its slogan. Her final promise—“I won’t tell a soul you’re just in another average backpacker hostel.”—makes her judgement unmistakable. One text imagines a private haven for carefully selected guests; the other follows a lively, young group through a merely ordinary budget stay. *(Text One, lines 23–38 and 53–55; Text Two, lines 46–55.)*

**MS alignment check:** This compares genre, perspective, setting, guest experience, standard/amenities, reputation and conclusion, with balanced evidence from both texts. It develops the Results MS’s indicative comparison points: fiction versus review, non-judgemental description versus personal evaluation, peaceful retreat versus lively guests, idyllic lakeside setting versus city centre, and luxury hotel versus basic hostel (MS pp. 10–11). Q7 is assessed by AO3; Level 5 is 13–15 and calls for varied, comprehensive comparisons with balanced, discriminating references; a response considering only one text is capped at the top of Level 2 (MS p. 11). This constructed response has no assigned mark or level.
