## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed discursion

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 19) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 21–22), with method from this paper's own report. **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **Foolish is the wrong word**
>
> Ask a climber why she is on the mountain and she will not say *because it is dangerous*. She will tell you about the route, the weather window, the rope-work she has practised for four years. That is the first problem with the statement: it mistakes a considered risk for a careless one, and the two are not relatives.
>
> Consider what the prepared risk-taker actually does. Hillary and Tenzing did not stroll up Everest; they spent weeks establishing camps and carrying loads. Armstrong did not volunteer to be fired into space on a whim; he had flown fighter jets and docked spacecraft before he sat on top of a Saturn V. These people are usually better equipped than the rest of us, and they nearly always have an end beyond the experience itself — a summit, a sample, a first. Without them we would still believe the world was flat and the sea was bottomless, and no amount of caution would have taught us otherwise.
>
> And yet the statement is not simply wrong, and this is where I part company with the easy answer. Every one of those expeditions has a cost that is paid by somebody else. When a walker goes missing on a Scottish ridge, the mountain rescue volunteers go out in the same weather, unpaid, and their families wait at home. When a boat turns back from a storm, the coastguard has already launched. The risk is private; the rescue is public. That is not foolishness, but it is not free either.
>
> There is also the question of who else lives with the decision. A parent of young children who takes up free solo climbing is making a choice that other people will have to survive. I do not think that is wicked. I do think it is a different act from the same climb attempted by someone with nobody waiting.
>
> Which brings me back to the word. *Foolish* is a judgement about thinking, and the people the statement describes are usually thinking very hard indeed. The ones who genuinely deserve it are not the adventurers but the unprepared — the tourist who sets off up a mountain in trainers, the driver who treats a wet road as a challenge. Recklessness is a failure of imagination; exploration is an exercise of it.
>
> So I disagree with the statement, but not entirely. People who look for excitement and danger are not foolish. They are often brave, occasionally selfish, and always somebody's reason to call the emergency services. The adventure is worth defending; the bill is worth admitting.
