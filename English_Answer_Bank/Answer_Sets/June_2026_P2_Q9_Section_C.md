## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed discursive response

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS indicative content (printed p. 8) and its verified AO4/AO5 grids (printed pp. 9–10; PDF pp. 21–22), with method informed by the June 2025 report commentary. **No report or script exists for this paper.** Not a Pearson script, not examiner-marked, **no score claimed**.

> **‘People are entitled to freedom and choice about how they spend their time and what they do, as long as it doesn’t harm others.’ To what extent do you agree?**
>
> I agree with the first half of this sentence almost without reservation, and with the second half hardly at all — because the second half is where all the difficulty has been quietly hidden.
>
> Start with what is surely right. A person's time is the only thing they truly own. Nobody else can spend it for them, and nobody else has to live inside the consequences. When someone says they would rather spend four hours a day playing a game than learning the violin, the honest answer is: that is theirs to decide. The alternative is a world in which somebody else — a government, a school, a parent, a very confident neighbour — decides what counts as a worthwhile evening. History suggests that those people are rarely right for long, and are frequently insufferable about it.
>
> And most people, most of the time, choose reasonably. The assumption behind restricting freedom is usually that we know better than the person living the life, which is a large assumption to make about strangers. Rules that treat adults as children tend to produce adults who behave like children.
>
> So far, so libertarian. But look again at the condition: **as long as it doesn't harm others.** That clause is doing an enormous amount of work, and it does not survive contact with real life.
>
> Almost nothing is solitary. A person who spends their evenings in a way that damages their health will, in twenty years, need somebody to look after them, and that somebody will be a public service or a family member. A choice made alone is usually paid for by other people. And plenty of harm is slow, invisible and legal: the gambling that empties a household's savings, the algorithm that keeps a fourteen-year-old awake until three in the morning. Nobody is punched. Something is still broken.
>
> The harder question is who decides what harm means. If I say my choice harms nobody, and you say it harms you, we are not having an argument about facts; we are having an argument about values, and there is no referee. This is exactly why societies need rules rather than a slogan.
>
> So my position is a qualified agreement. The right to choose how to spend your time should be the default, and the burden of proof should sit with whoever wants to take it away. But the "no harm" clause cannot be treated as a locked door that ends the conversation. Harm is real, it is often indirect, and it is sometimes invisible to the person causing it. The answer is not to abandon freedom but to be honest that it is not free — and that the bill is frequently paid by somebody who never chose anything at all.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Agree fully:** freedom as the default with only minimal, clearly justified restrictions; the state's record of deciding what is “beneficial” as the strongest evidence against it.
- **Disagree:** the harm clause is unworkable, so the statement collapses into either permissiveness or unlimited interference; laws exist precisely because self-assessment of harm is unreliable.
- **Middle routes:** distinguish *harm* from *offence*; distinguish adults from children; argue for regulation of *systems* (apps, gambling, advertising) rather than of individuals' choices; argue that freedom of choice is meaningless without the resources to choose.
- **Using Text One:** the statement comes from that article, so a candidate may engage with its argument — but the rubric forbids **re-telling** the article, so its examples (screen-time statistics, the Florida bill) should not be narrated as content.
- **Avoid:** the stock phrases the June 2025 report lists, a one-sided rant with no concession, and a response that never answers “to what extent”.
