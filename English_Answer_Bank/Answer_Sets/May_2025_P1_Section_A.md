### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** The narrator begins, “We are here in the Yemen at last.” The phrase “at last” suggests anticipation, while “we” places the reader inside a shared journey. The opening then becomes a long, accumulating description of the landscape. “Breathtaking” conveys amazement; the colours “golden” and “purple” make the cliffs seem majestic, and the wadis are “slashed as if with a giant knife”, a violent simile that makes their scale and shape striking. The warning that a vehicle “might sink” on the salt flats introduces danger alongside wonder. The enormous scale of “a quarter of a million square miles” of uninhabited desert makes the country seem vast.

The passage repeatedly shifts between contrasting places. The towns are “as wonderful as the desert”; their tower houses appear “like skyscrapers”, making them recognisable through comparison with Manhattan while still seeming unfamiliar. The tricolon “a din of shouting voices, a riot of colour, unimaginable smells of drains and spices” fills the description with sound, sight and smell, contrasting the busy towns with the silence and emptiness of the desert. In the Empty Quarter, the repeated word “dunes”, the simile “dunes like long fingers” and the “restless wind” suggest a landscape that constantly changes and can physically hurt the traveller.

The drive into the mountains sounds precarious: “crumbling tracks”, “loose gravel”, a “precipitous slope” and the vehicle “lurching” create a sense of instability. The contrast between villages in “permanent shadow” and oases with palms around blue-green pools presents the country as full of startling differences. In Heraz, “huge ramparts” make the mountains seem imposing, while the claim that they are “impossible” to penetrate heightens the challenge. When “Suddenly” the travellers move from bright sun into thick mist, the sharp change in visibility feels surprising and disorienting. The village seems like “some forgotten, hidden world from a childhood story”; the later comparison to travelling “back in time hundreds of years” deepens its mystery.

The structure saves the lush garden for the end, making it a discovery after the difficult journey. Its “cool and mysterious” atmosphere, “rippled” water, marble channels, shade and colourful plants contrast with the exposed mountains and dry tracks. The final short sentence, “It is a magical place”, gathers the narrator’s wonder into a simple, emphatic judgement. The journey is therefore presented as dangerous and demanding, but also as a succession of extraordinary sights culminating in an almost enchanted place.

It aims at the AO2 Level 5 descriptor—perceptive analysis with discriminating references (MS p. 9)—but is an original, unscored worked answer.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Bryson opens with a threat: Handfast Point ends in a “sudden drop” to “seriously frothy seas”. “Sudden” makes the danger seem unexpected, while the contrast between “nerve” and “foolishness” gives his approach a humorous, self-aware tone. He later describes the climb to Ballard Down as a “taxing slog” for an “old puffed-out unfit person like me”, but immediately says it is “worth it for the view”. This contrast prepares the reader for a journey that alternates between strain and reward.

The view is presented as almost overwhelming. Bryson says the hills “rolled and billowed”, then compares them to “a shaken-out blanket settling on to a bed”; the familiar simile makes the landscape seem soft and peaceful. “Plump” hedgerows, “prettily dotted” hillsides and “creamy flecks of sheep” build an attractive, carefully coloured countryside. The emphatic “beautiful beyond words”, “life seems perfect” and “spellbound” convey how strongly the view affects him. The light becomes almost supernatural: “magnificent spears of shimmery light” are compared to “escalators to heaven”, and he imagines hearing “celestial music”. The religious imagery makes a fleeting natural event seem transcendent.

The narrative then turns from looking to walking. “It was the longest mile I can remember walking” is hyperbolic and signals that the pleasant excursion is becoming difficult. The goal does not appear closer, and “I never found the Giant’s Grave” makes the search end in anticlimax. The compressed phrase “sweatily perplexed” captures both physical effort and confusion. A sequence of harsh verbs—“plunged”, “crawling”, “fording” and “falling”—accumulates the obstacles. Bryson’s tricolon “footsore, travel-soiled and decorated about the extremities with interesting rivulets of dried blood” gives a vivid picture of his battered state; “decorated” and “interesting” make the discomfort sound comically understated.

The structure ends by arriving at Corfe Castle after the difficult walk. The plain, positive description “a popular and pretty place” contrasts with the earlier blood, confusion and exhaustion, leaving the reader with a modestly rewarding destination rather than a grand triumph. Through first-person observation, self-deprecating humour and shifts between beautiful scenery and physical hardship, Bryson presents the walk as memorable but far less effortless than its view first suggests.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers present visiting new places as impressive, but Text One is a fictional first-person narrative about several journeys across Yemen, while Text Two is a nonfiction travel-book account of Bryson’s walk along the south coast of England. Text One opens with the shared “We are here in the Yemen at last” and ranges from the Empty Quarter to towns, mountains and a hidden garden. Bryson’s account is more narrowly focused: he walks from Handfast Point towards Corfe Castle. The settings also create different impressions. Torday describes a “quarter of a million square miles” of desert, severe mountain roads and finally an oasis-like garden; Bryson’s “Dorset hills” and “plump hedgerows” create a gentler, cultivated landscape.

Their descriptions are both vivid, but they emphasize different qualities. Torday’s comparison of wadis “slashed as if with a giant knife” makes the Yemen seem dramatic, while his tricolon of “a din of shouting voices, a riot of colour, unimaginable smells” presents the towns as crowded and intense. Bryson instead compares the hills to “a shaken-out blanket settling on to a bed”, making the countryside seem soft and familiar. Both writers express strong admiration—Torday ends, “It is a magical place”, while Bryson calls the view “beautiful beyond words” and is “spellbound”—but Torday’s travel is often by vehicle through a whole country, whereas Bryson’s response is rooted in the sights and discomforts of walking.

The first-person perspectives reveal another difference. Torday’s narrator records what “we” see and includes glimpses of other people, such as herdsmen and boys at an oasis; Bryson meets no one in the passage and gives more space to his own thoughts and setbacks. He cannot find the Giant’s Grave, becomes “sweatily perplexed” and arrives “footsore” with dried blood on his extremities, while Torday’s narrator mainly builds impressions through landscapes, local activity and changes of setting. Both include difficulty rather than presenting travel as entirely perfect: Torday mentions dangerous salt flats and “crumbling tracks”, while Bryson describes the “sudden drop” and getting lost. Their endings resolve these contrasting journeys differently: Torday’s hidden garden becomes a “magical” discovery; Bryson reaches Corfe Castle, described in the more understated phrase “a popular and pretty place”. Overall, both convey fascination with unfamiliar places, but Text One emphasizes the variety and almost other-worldly contrasts of Yemen, while Text Two combines admiration for the English landscape with a humorous account of the walker’s ordeal.

**MS alignment check:** This comparison is integrated across both texts and covers genre, setting, scope of travel, description, perspective, human encounters, difficulty and each ending. It draws on the MS’s comparison points and keeps references balanced (MS pp. 14–16). The AO3 Level 5 descriptor calls for varied, comprehensive comparisons with balanced, discriminating references; this original response has not been awarded a mark.
