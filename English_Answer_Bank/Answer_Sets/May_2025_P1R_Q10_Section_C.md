## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed story

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 20), the AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 21–22) and this paper's own report (pp. 51–53). **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **The notebook in the airing cupboard**
>
> The flat came with a cupboard we could not open. The key had gone missing some time in the previous century, according to the letting agent, who said this in the tone of a man who has decided that a thing is not his problem. For three weeks I kept my towels on top of it and listened to my mother say that we would get someone in.
>
> We never got anyone in. On the nineteenth day I put a screwdriver where the hinge pin should have been and leaned on it, and the door came off in my hands like a tooth.
>
> Inside: a boiler, a tin of paint that had gone solid, and a notebook. School exercise book, blue, the price in old money pencilled on the corner. It smelled of dust and, faintly, of soap.
>
> The first page said *Swifts*. Underneath, a date — 12 May 1994 — and a number: 6. Then a line about the weather, and a line about the chestnut tree opposite, and nothing else. The next page said 13 May 1994, and 8, and *wind from the east, first ones back over the chimney at 6.40.*
>
> Every page. Thirty-one years of the same two or three sentences. *Swifts. Rain. None yet. Twelve, and one went into the eaves next door instead.* And then, in a different pen, in 2009, a small note: *Margaret died in March. Doing this for both of us now.*
>
> My mother found me sitting on the kitchen floor with it. She read a page, and then another, and then she said, in a voice I had not heard her use, “That's the flat above. That's Margaret.”
>
> The last entry in the notebook stops in the middle of a sentence. 14 May, and then a number with the pen still moving. I thought about that for a long time — a woman at a window with her glasses on, writing down eleven or twelve, and putting the pen down forever in the middle of a word.
>
> I have kept the notebook. I have not written in it. But at half past six every evening since, I have stood at the same window with my phone face-down on the sill, and counted, and got it slightly wrong, and counted again.
>
> It was an unexpected find: not the swifts, which had been arriving every May without my noticing for sixteen years, but the fact that somebody had spent three decades proving they were worth counting.
>

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Finds (report's own list):** money, jewels, maps, hidden treasure, mysterious letters; or “finding a friend, a pet or a lost letter”; or “more philosophically… a new sense of meaning or courage”. A find may sit **inside** a larger narrative as long as the focus stays on its effect.
- **Reveal point:** the final paragraph (the report says many candidates did this and it “kept their narratives engaging”).
- **Objects vs. abstracts:** a photo, a key, a recording, a debt, a name; or a change of mind, a diagnosis, a confession.
- **What to avoid:** a plot borrowed from a film or game; a heist-style hunt; more than two settings; “it was all a dream”; a find that is described and then abandoned without consequence.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO4:** a single unbroken narrative line from a stuck cupboard to a window count, with the find reframed in the last line; restrained emotional pressure from concrete detail (the 2009 pen, the unfinished sentence).
- **AO5:** register shift between narration and the logbook quotations, deliberate fragments, a controlled final sentence, and punctuation used to mark the notebook's voice.
