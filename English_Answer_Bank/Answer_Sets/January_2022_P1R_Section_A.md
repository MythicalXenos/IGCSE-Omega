### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[CONSTRUCTED MS-BASED RESPONSE]** *Original response based on Text One and the Results MS; not Pearson-scored. No mark is assigned.*

At first, the narrator presents the journey as familiar but physically substantial: the empty trailer bumps behind the Land Rover and she settles in for a “long drive into town”, normally four or five hours. Her attitude, however, is positive. “Rather enjoyed”, “relished” and “buoyant end-of-term sensations” make the drive sound like a welcome release rather than a chore. The simile “like crossing a border” describes the change from rough track to tarred road, while “a frontier between two states of mind” extends that boundary into a mental one: the project is behind her and she is “on my own for a few days”. *(Text One, lines 1–10.)*

The route initially seems repetitive and somewhat bleak. The road is “straight” through “dry scrubland and patchy forest”, and the narrator later calls it an “unchanging landscape”; both descriptions suggest limited variety. Her account of the routine also makes the trip sound purposeful and tiring: the next day involves a “tiring round of visits” to the bank, department store and merchants, followed by possible repairs and spare parts. The list gives a sense of the work awaiting her, while “merely provisioning” makes this particular trip sound like one more practical errand. The contrast between the long outward drive and the much slower return, with the Land Rover and trailer “heavily loaded”, emphasizes the effort involved. *(Text One, lines 11–22.)*

Small settlements briefly make the landscape more varied. Every ten miles or so she passes a village of “mud huts thatched with palm fronds” and traders’ stalls selling fruit, vegetables, sweetmeats and cola nuts. Yet the journey also carries an uncertain threat: although it “was not dangerous”, travellers are warned not to go after dark, and the narrator speculates about “Brigands and bandits” and “a risk of highway robbery”. Her admission that she is “never absolutely clear” what they should fear makes the warning sound like an unsettling rumour. The arrival at the town is marked by the rattling wheels crossing the “ancient iron bridge” over a river four hundred yards wide. *(Text One, lines 22–30.)*

The town is presented through dense sights and sounds. “Deep ditches”, “empty shops” and “mud-walled compounds” make its outskirts appear poor and worn, while the contrasting neon colours “ultramarine and peppermint” add flashes of brightness. The sound verbs “bellowed”, “pressed” and “knocked” make the traffic and street activity feel loud and insistent. A long list of goods the children try to sell suggests how many surround the vehicle. The simile that soldiers carry weapons “as unconcernedly as newspapers” makes their presence seem disturbingly ordinary. Boyd then contrasts old men sitting and watching with young men “stamping their feet” and playing Ping-Pong, ending that episode with the forceful “ruthlessly smashed and counter-smashed”. *(Text One, lines 31–43.)*

In the town centre, the “press of traffic nudged its way” past a catalogue of civic buildings, making the journey feel slow and crowded. The simile comparing cannonballs to “the droppings of some giant rodent” gives the description a dry, irreverent edge. The setting sun then “bathed everything with a gentle peachy light”, a calmer image that softens the preceding bustle. The final description turns more critical: the airport is “far too large” for the provincial capital, and the “sprawling modern hotel” appears destined to decline. The short final fact that five arrivals and departures a day are “all it boasted” makes the grand scale seem excessive and underused. *(Text One, lines 44–55.)*

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[CONSTRUCTED MS-BASED RESPONSE]** *Original response based on Text Two and the Results MS; not Pearson-scored. No mark is assigned.*

Yanshin begins by addressing the reader with the question, “Trying to figure out how to do a road trip without a car?” The direct question draws in someone who may be unable to drive, short of money or concerned about the environment; the three reasons show that the advice is meant for different needs. He acknowledges the familiar image of a car on a mountain road or coastal path, then reassures readers that road trips are “not reserved exclusively” for drivers. This opening replaces a narrow idea of a road trip with a more inclusive one. *(Text Two, lines 1–8.)*

The first subheading, “Take the train and enjoy the views”, makes the benefits clear before the detail begins. The writer calls train travel “far more relaxing” and builds a balanced sequence of simple actions: “book a seat, turn up, and catch the right train”; the following list, “no route planning, no refuelling, no toll roads”, emphasizes the effort the traveller avoids. The short, informal “the dream!” turns that list into a persuasive verdict. Further benefits include “quality time”, a “precious few hours” for reading and “stunning views”. Named examples such as the Glenfinnan Viaduct, associated with the Harry Potter films, and Switzerland’s Glacier Express make the promised scenery concrete. The “win-win” ending adds an environmental benefit to the enjoyable views. *(Text Two, lines 9–24.)*

The next heading, “Save money by riding on a bus”, foregrounds affordability. Yanshin recognizes a possible objection—buses can seem “shabby and uncomfortable”—but counters it emphatically with “this simply isn’t true!” A list of WiFi, charging ports, air-conditioning and screens supports his claim with practical examples; the detail about generous luggage allowance adds another reassurance. The concession “Admittedly” signals that bus journeys can take longer, but the writer deliberately reframes this as “a positive that travellers should embrace”. Stopping in small towns and seeing “remote, beautiful places” can make the journey itself more rewarding than a faster drive. *(Text Two, lines 25–38.)*

Cycling is presented as the boldest alternative. The writer calls it the “most active and adventurous” option and says it can take travellers “well off the beaten track”. The three-part list “country paths, mountain tracks, and thick forests” creates a sense of varied places inaccessible to cars; the “completely different perspective” contrasts directly with looking out from “the other side of a car window”. Health and environmental benefits extend the appeal beyond sightseeing. The imperative “Definitely give cycling a go” is a direct invitation, while the admission that cyclists must travel lighter and may feel more tired acknowledges a real drawback rather than making the advice sound unrealistic. He immediately balances that cost with the reward of reaching the destination without an engine and having a “super memorable trip”. *(Text Two, lines 39–51.)*

The conclusion turns the article into practical reassurance. The tricolon “plan ahead, do your research and are prepared for a little physical exercise” offers manageable steps, and “it’s not as hard as it sounds” anticipates and answers a reader’s hesitation. The final claim that this kind of trip may be “even better than driving” leaves the reader with the strongest possible endorsement. Across the article, informative subheadings, second-person address, lists and a friendly, colloquial tone make the alternatives sound practical as well as enjoyable. *(Text Two, lines 52–55 and throughout.)*

Q6 is assessed by AO2; Level 5 is 9–10 and calls for perceptive analysis with discriminating references (MS p. 9). The indicative list is not a checklist; this constructed response remains unmarked.

### Q7 — comparison of ideas, perspectives and methods

**[CONSTRUCTED MS-BASED RESPONSE]** *Original AO3 comparison based on both exact source texts and the Results MS; not Pearson-scored. No mark is assigned.*

The writers approach journeys from very different forms and purposes. Text One is an extract from a novel: a first-person narrator recounts one particular drive, while Text Two is an advice article that addresses readers as “you” and recommends ways to travel without owning a car. The narrator in Text One is provisioning a conservation project and making business visits; Yanshin is helping readers plan a road trip, particularly if they cannot drive, want to save money or wish to reduce their environmental impact. One text tells an episode from a working journey, whereas the other offers general advice for travel as recreation. *(Text One, introduction and lines 1–22; Text Two, introduction and lines 1–8.)*

Although the narrator’s journey is demanding, she enjoys it: she has “rather enjoyed this drive” before and “relished” its “buoyant end-of-term sensations”. Her pleasure is private and linked to leaving the project behind. Yanshin is more openly persuasive and extends that pleasure to his readers, promising “quality time”, “stunning views” and a “super memorable trip”. Both therefore suggest that journeys can be enjoyable, but Text One conveys that through one traveller’s experience while Text Two tries to persuade readers that several alternatives can be rewarding. *(Text One, lines 5–10; Text Two, lines 12–24 and 39–55.)*

The texts also contrast the transport and practical challenges. The narrator must drive a Land Rover with an empty trailer along crumbling, potholed roads; the outward journey normally takes four or five hours, and its duration assumes there are no major accidents and the bridges are in reasonable repair. On the much slower, heavily loaded return, the average speed is thirty miles an hour. She is warned not to travel after dark because of a possible “risk of highway robbery”. Yanshin instead sets out three alternatives—train, bus and bicycle—and emphasizes the advantages of each. He does not hide every drawback: buses can take longer, and cycling is tiring and requires travelling lighter. However, he reframes the slower bus journey as a chance to see more and the effort of cycling as more rewarding. The novel presents difficulty as part of one actual trip; the article acknowledges obstacles in order to show readers how they can still travel. *(Text One, lines 1–4, 17–22 and 22–30; Text Two, lines 12–21, 32–38 and 39–51.)*

Their descriptions of place create a further contrast. Text One moves through dry scrubland and patchy forest, villages of mud huts and a town marked by empty shops, ditches, noise and traffic. Its lists of sights and sounds make the town vivid and crowded; the neon lights and the setting sun’s “gentle peachy light” provide moments of colour and beauty amid less attractive surroundings. Text Two selects positive examples to encourage travel: beautiful mountain roads, scenic train routes, “small towns”, “remote, beautiful places” and cycling through forests. The first narrator records a particular and mixed landscape, including poor areas; the advice article presents selected scenery as a reason to choose alternatives to driving. *(Text One, lines 11–18 and 22–52; Text Two, lines 4–8, 18–24 and 34–47.)*

The writers’ methods reinforce these different perspectives. Boyd’s narrative uses first-person observations, sensory details and figurative language: the road feels “like crossing a border”, while music “bellowed” and traffic “nudged its way” through town. These choices let the reader experience the journey as it unfolds. Yanshin organizes his article with practical subheadings, direct address, rhetorical questions and persuasive lists; examples such as the Glenfinnan Viaduct and Glacier Express make his advice specific, and “Definitely give cycling a go” is an explicit call to action. Text One ends on a qualified, disappointing note—the airport is “far too large” and the hotel seems “always heading for decline”—whereas Text Two concludes optimistically that car-free travel may be “even better than driving”. *(Text One, lines 5–10, 31–55; Text Two, lines 1–24 and 39–55.)*

**MS alignment check:** This compares fiction/narrative with informative persuasion, a working journey with travel for pleasure, one car journey with three alternatives, the mixed/poor areas of Text One with the article’s scenic possibilities, the writers’ descriptions and purposes, and their shared suggestion that journeys can be enjoyable. It uses balanced evidence from both texts and develops the Results MS’s indicative comparison points (MS pp. 10–11). Q7 is assessed by AO3; Level 5 is 13–15 and requires a varied, comprehensive range of comparisons with balanced, discriminating references. A response considering only one text is capped at the top of Level 2 (MS p. 12). This constructed response has no assigned mark or level.
