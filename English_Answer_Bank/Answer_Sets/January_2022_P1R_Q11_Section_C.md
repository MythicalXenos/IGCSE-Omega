## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed description

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from this paper's MS (printed p. 17) and AO4/AO5 descriptors (pp. 18–19), with method from this paper's own report. **Not a Pearson script; not examiner-marked; no score claimed.**

> **The button tin**
>
> It is a Quality Street tin that has not contained chocolate since before I was born — a squat cylinder the size of a side plate, once gold, now the colour of weak tea, with a dent above the lid join that my grandmother always said had been made by a falling iron and I have always privately blamed on one of her four sons.
>
> The lid does not screw. It presses, and it sighs when it comes off, and the sigh is the whole reason I keep it on a shelf at eye level rather than in a drawer.
>
> Inside, buttons. Hundreds, and it is impossible to say that in a way that conveys the noise they make: a dry, dense clatter, like rain on a tin roof heard through a wall. They are sorted by nothing. That is the first thing to say about them, because my grandmother sorted everything else in her life with the rigour of a customs officer — her receipts by year, her spices alphabetically, her opinions about her neighbours into two labelled groups — and the buttons she left in a glorious, lawless heap.
>
> Most are ordinary. Small brown ones from shirts, four holes, the thread still in some of them, gone grey with washing. A fistful of navy from what must have been a school uniform. Six pearl ones, slightly yellowed, which she wore on a jacket for my mother's wedding and never again. There are buttons shaped like roses, and one shaped like a fish, and a brass one from a coat that must have belonged to a man, because it is far too heavy for anything she would have worn.
>
> The best of them are the mismatched ones: a single green disc with a chipped rim, a white one with a star pressed into it, one that is not a button at all but a sixpence with a hole drilled through it. I have never asked why. Everyone who could have told me is gone, and the tin is now the only archive.
>
> What makes it memorable is not the tin and not even the buttons. It is the fact that a woman who threw nothing away kept a heap of useless objects in the one container she opened most often — that every time she needed a button for a shirt, she had to put her hand into forty years of them and find one that would do.
>
> That is what I think about when I take the lid off: not grief, exactly. Something closer to instruction. Keep the useful things, certainly. But also keep the ones you cannot justify, and keep them together, and make sure somebody knows what the sigh sounds like.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Objects (report's list):** a camera; a piece of clothing; a snow globe; a tennis racquet; a necklace; a scarf; a tool, a toy, a book, a photograph, a ring.
- **Structures:** describe the object physically, then its significance (as here); the object described in three scenes of ownership; the object and the person it came from, held in balance.
- **Keeping it descriptive:** keep the object physically present, use the senses, and let memory appear in short bursts rather than as a story with events.
- **What to avoid:** a narrative about how the object was lost or found; a list of features with no significance; “it means a lot to me” as the entire explanation; ornate vocabulary used for its own sake — this report specifically notes a lack of ambition in vocabulary, so precision beats decoration.
