## Matching MS criteria

**Indicative content (MS p. 17):** “No indicative content can be specified, since candidates may choose to interpret the title as they wish. Candidates should be rewarded for such qualities as a sense of drama, vivid description, excitement or suspense. NB: Explicit reference to the title may not be mentioned until the end of the story. Examiners should be open to a wide range of interpretation.”

This is one of the few 4EB1 questions with **no** content list: the marks are earned entirely through AO4 and AO5. Any “model” is therefore only one possible realisation of the title.

**AO4 — 20 marks, Questions 9, 10 and 11 (MS p. 18):** *Communicate effectively and imaginatively, adapting form, tone and register of writing for specific purposes and audiences.*

- **Level 3 (9–12):** communicates clearly; clear sense of purpose and understanding of the expectations/requirements of the intended reader; appropriate use of form, tone and register.
- **Level 4 (13–16):** communicates successfully; a secure realisation of purpose and the expectations/requirements of the intended reader; effective use of form, tone and register.
- **Level 5 (17–20):** communication is perceptive and subtle; task is sharply focused on purpose and the expectations/requirements of the intended reader; sophisticated use of form, tone and register.

**AO5 — 10 marks, Questions 9, 10 and 11 (MS p. 19):** *Write clearly, using a range of vocabulary and sentence structures, with appropriate paragraphing and accurate spelling, grammar and punctuation.*

- **Level 3 (5–6):** develops and connects appropriate information and ideas; structural and grammatical features and paragraphing make the meaning clear; varied vocabulary; irregular spellings accurate; accurate and varied punctuation.
- **Level 4 (7–8):** manages information and ideas cohesively and deliberately; wide, selective vocabulary with only occasional spelling errors; a range of punctuation positioned for clarity, with sentence structures managed for deliberate effect.
- **Level 5 (9–10):** manipulates complex ideas using a range of structural and grammatical features to support coherence and cohesion; extensive vocabulary used strategically; punctuation accurate to aid emphasis and precision, with sentence structures chosen selectively for effect.

## What the script actually does (evidence-tagged observation, not a mark)

These observations are mine, keyed to the published criteria; they are **not** Pearson commentary and carry no separate marks.

- **Title withheld until the final line** — “our difficult decision would be worthwhile indeed.” This is the strategy the MS explicitly allows (“Explicit reference to the title may not be mentioned until the end”). The ending re-frames the whole journey, matching Pearson’s phrase “used as an effective ending.”
- **Compressed timescale.** The narrative covers one car journey and one airport queue, matching the general examiner comment that stronger Section C narratives were “tightly plotted and covered a limited timescale” (printed p. 49).
- **Restrained emotional register.** Fear is rendered through physical detail — “My stomach fluttered,” “a lump rose in my throat,” “I am not going to cry” — rather than declared feeling, which is the kind of control AO4 Level 5 rewards.
- **Deliberate sentence-length variation.** Short declaratives (“At last we were at the front of the line.”) alternate with long, comma-listed sentences (“Watching the trams wind their way up to the Getty Museum one last time. Driving on the right side of the road one last time.”), illustrating the AO5 “shifting effectively between shorter and longer sentences.”
- **Embedded foreshadowing.** “On the other end of our journey, there would only be two of us to carry the luggage” plants the split family before the goodbye, so the ending is prepared rather than tacked on. This answers the general examiner complaint (p. 49) that the decision “sometimes… seemed to be an afterthought.”

## General Q10 commentary (ER printed pp. 49–51), kept separate from this script’s judgement

- The question was the most popular option; candidates interpreted “a difficult decision” widely — material choices, relationships, college, moral dilemmas, medical decisions.
- Examiners noted **“a number of unnecessarily gruesome and gory stories,”** sometimes losing focus on the title, and occasionally **over-ambitious** plots.
- Weaker responses **added the decision at the end with little sign-posting**, making the link to the title “tenuous,” and some appeared to be **a prepared story barely adapted** to the title.
- Stronger responses had **fewer, well-developed characters, believable dialogue, tightly plotted narratives over a limited timescale**, and used figurative techniques and wide vocabulary deliberately.

This is question-level feedback, not an extra score attribution for this candidate; the published reasons for the 30/30 are the p. 55 comments above.
