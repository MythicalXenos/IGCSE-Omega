## [CONSTRUCTED GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed talk

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content and the June 2022 P1R report guidance, using verified details from both texts. **Not** a Pearson script, **not** examiner-marked, **no score claimed.**

> Right — hands up anyone who has ever dug a hole on a beach because someone told them there was treasure under it. Keep your hands up if you found anything. That is treasure hunting in one image, and I want to talk about why people still do it, what they actually find, and what goes wrong.
>
> Start with what you need, because the fantasy version skips this bit. Serious hunters use a metal detector that can reach more than a metre down and tell you what kind of metal it has found. That costs around £200; the £30 ones on the market will mostly find bottle tops. Add a GPS so you do not search the same field twice, a small shovel, a trowel, a brush, and something padded to carry a find home — a cotton-wool-lined tin is enough. Old coins and jewellery are fragile, and a pocket is not a storage system.
>
> Then get permission. Walking onto someone else's land without it is trespassing, and taking a find away makes it theft. In practice most landowners say yes, especially if you agree in advance to share whatever you find. If you do find something that counts as treasure under the Treasure Act, you must report it; failing to do so can mean an unlimited fine or up to three months in prison, and one former policeman was jailed for sixteen months after lying about where his coins came from. The Treasure Valuation Committee then sets the value if a museum wants the find, and the money is split between you and the landowner.
>
> And where should you look? Not the city. Generations of building have buried anything worth finding, and most urban finds go to professional archaeologists, who do not get to keep them either. Go to farmland in the countryside. Norfolk has produced more finds than anywhere else in Britain, followed by Yorkshire, Suffolk and Lincolnshire.
>
> So what might you actually find? The headline cases are extraordinary: a Viking hoard of gold, silver and jewels worth £2 million; nearly 2,000 Roman coins in East Yorkshire; 50,000 Celtic coins under a hedge in Jersey, untouched for two thousand years. Smaller finds are far more likely — single coins, rings, buckles, a handful of silver. That is the realistic version, and it is still remarkable: you are the first person to hold the object in centuries.
>
> But be sceptical about the big stories. A lost Nazi gold train was “found” in Poland, and officials were quoted as being 99% sure it was there, until scientists confirmed there were not even railway tracks in the area. Percy Fawcett went looking for a lost city in Brazil in 1925 and never came back. Lasseter's Reef, the Beale ciphers, Oak Island — every one comes with a tantalising map, a convenient death, and no gold. The pattern is the point: remote location, dubious document, previous searchers who nearly made it. Knowing that pattern is your best protection.
>
> Which brings me to the problems. Permission can be refused. Finds can be damaged as you lift them. Legal trouble is real, and so is the argument about splitting the proceeds. Some sites are remote, some are genuinely dangerous, and in some parts of the world treasure hunters have been attacked for what they were carrying. Other people may get to a promising site first. And sometimes your map is simply a story someone invented.
>
> None of that means do not try. It means go out equipped, go out with permission, report what you find, and treat the legends as entertainment rather than instructions. Then, if the detector beeps, you will know exactly what to do — and, just as importantly, what not to.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Combining bullets 1 and 3** is explicitly acceptable to Pearson here, “if they included enough information” — a candidate may run advice and problems as paired contrasts (equipment/problem, permission/legal trouble).
- **Second bullet alternatives:** a talk built almost entirely on the legends (El Dorado, the Mary Rose, Tutankhamun, local hoards such as Sutton Hoo or the Staffordshire Hoard) is fully within the MS, provided the other bullets are covered.
- **Unused Text One material:** the Treasure Act definition list (printed p. 3, lines 33–48: 10% precious metal, 300 years old, two or more coins from the same find, prehistoric objects) would support a much more technical talk; it is deliberately left out here because a peer audience would switch off.
- **Unused Text Two material:** Indiana Jones as a Fawcett derivative, the Hollywood film made 91 years after Fawcett vanished, and the “map found in an atmospheric location, preferably a tomb” detail are all available for humour.
- **Register:** a sceptical, humorous or earnest-serious talk all fit the report's description of successful responses; what it demands is a sustained register and a real sense of speaking to peers.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO1:** all three bullets are separate, developed sections; both texts are used, with Text Two supplying the sceptical intelligence that turns “what might be found” into a discussion rather than a list; nothing is lifted from the source wording.
- **AO4:** spoken register sustained from “Right — hands up” to the final line; direct address, rhetorical question, short sentences for rhythm, one joke, and a two-sentence opener so the first bullet point arrives inside the first half-minute — precisely the fault the report identifies and the strength it praises.
- **AO5:** articles are all present, subject–verb agreement is correct throughout, no comma splices, ‘I’ capitalised, and figures (£200, £2 million, 50,000, 99%, 1925, sixteen months) are punctuated consistently. The word *treasure* is spelled correctly — no risk from the source texts’ own tricky vocabulary.
- **Not claimed:** no examiner mark, level or score. The published 24/30 and 16/30 locators belong to scripts that are not reproduced legibly; this constructed talk has not been marked and is not equated with either.
