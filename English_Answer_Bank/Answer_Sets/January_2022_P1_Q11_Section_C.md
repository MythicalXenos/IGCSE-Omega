## [CONSTRUCTED, GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed description

> **The first night of four**
>
> The thing I remember is the bunk. Not the house, which was enormous and had a staircase you could see through, and not the grounds, which were the sort of thing adults call grounds. The bunk: a metal frame with a mattress like a dry sponge, four feet above a boy called Michael who fell asleep in nine seconds and stayed that way for eight hours.
>
> I was eleven and away for four nights, and by half past ten I had worked out that this was a serious mistake.
>
> What surprised me was that it was not really fear. I had expected fear and had prepared for it, in the way you do — the window, the door, the route to the corridor. What arrived instead was something quieter and much harder to handle. I could not place myself. At home I knew where I was in the dark without thinking: the wall was eighteen inches from the bed, the landing light made a bar across the ceiling, the boiler ticked twice and then stopped. Here all of those certainties had been removed at once, and my mind kept reaching for them, like a tongue for a missing tooth, and finding a metal rail instead.
>
> So I did the thing children do, which is to make bargains. If I lie completely still, I will fall asleep. If I count to three hundred I will fall asleep. If I am still awake at midnight I am allowed to be upset. I negotiated with myself for two hours and got nothing.
>
> The homesickness was not a longing for my mother, exactly, though I did want her. It was a longing for the ordinary. I wanted the sound of the dishwasher being stacked badly. I wanted my sister's radio through the wall. I wanted to be bored in a familiar place, which is a luxury I have never since been able to explain to anyone who did not spend a night on that bunk.
>
> Somewhere around two in the morning I heard the person in the next bed crying, very quietly, trying not to be heard. That was the moment the night changed, though nothing else about it did. I lay there thinking: if she is also awake, then this is not something wrong with me. It is just what the first night is. It is a thing that happens to everybody, in a room, four feet above someone who is asleep.
>
> In the morning there was powdered hot chocolate and a boy who had lost a sock, and the whole thing had shrunk to the size it actually was.
>
> I have been away a great deal since, and I still cannot sleep the first night anywhere. But I no longer take it personally. That is what the bunk taught me: that being unplaced is not a crisis, only a stage, and that it passes somewhere between midnight and the smell of breakfast.
