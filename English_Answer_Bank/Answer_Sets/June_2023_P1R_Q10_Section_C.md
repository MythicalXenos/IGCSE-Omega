## Matching MS criteria

**Indicative content (MS p. 17, quoted in full for Q10):** “No indicative content can be specified, since candidates may choose to interpret the title as they wish. Candidates should be rewarded for such qualities as a sense of drama, vivid description, excitement or suspense. NB: Explicit reference to the title may not be mentioned until the end of the story. Examiners should be open to a wide range of interpretation.”

**AO4 — 20 marks, Questions 9, 10 and 11 (MS p. 18):** *Communicate effectively and imaginatively, adapting form, tone and register of writing for specific purposes and audiences.*

- **Level 4 (13–16):** communicates successfully; a secure realisation of purpose and the expectations/requirements of the intended reader; effective use of form, tone and register.
- **Level 5 (17–20):** communication is perceptive and subtle; task sharply focused on purpose and the expectations/requirements of the intended reader; sophisticated use of form, tone and register.

**AO5 — 10 marks, Questions 9, 10 and 11 (MS p. 19):** *Write clearly, using a range of vocabulary and sentence structures, with appropriate paragraphing and accurate spelling, grammar and punctuation.*

- **Level 4 (7–8):** manages information and ideas, with structural and grammatical features used cohesively and deliberately across the text; wide, selective vocabulary with only occasional spelling errors; positions a range of punctuation for clarity, managing sentence structures for deliberate effect.
- **Level 5 (9–10):** manipulates complex ideas, utilising a range of structural and grammatical features to support coherence and cohesion; uses extensive vocabulary strategically, rare spelling errors do not detract from overall meaning; punctuates with accuracy to aid emphasis and precision, using a range of sentence structures accurately and selectively to achieve particular effects.

Because Q10 has no indicative content, the grids do all the work: there is no list of “acceptable plot points” to satisfy, and the marks are decided on execution — which is why the same 30 marks can be earned by completely different stories (see the P1 comparison below).

## What the script does — and why it earned 30/30

These observations are mine, keyed to the published criteria and the question-level commentary; they are **not** Pearson commentary and carry no marks.

**Structure and framing (the AO4 “control of structure and tone”):**

- **A date, not a plot summary, opens the story.** The first legible words are “30th December, 2007,” with the framing idea “it would just [struck] a date, to many” — the title’s claim is set up as a proposition the story will then disprove. This is why the opening “immediately grabs the reader’s attention” despite being the messiest paragraph on the page.
- **The title is withheld to the last line, and the withholding is earned.** The final line is “The day [struck “her world”] everything had changed.” — the candidate’s own revision moved away from a metaphor toward the task’s exact wording. Pearson’s commentary warns that adding the title “without a link to the narrative” fails; here the link is the photograph, the date and the birth, so the closing phrase lands as a conclusion rather than a label.
- **The narrative is framed by a present-tense scene.** The story opens in 2007, leaves the hospital in the middle, and returns to a bedroom in the present (“Come look at this dog video!”) before the photograph closes the loop. That is the “shifting effectively between past and present” Pearson names in the AO5 comment.
- **One viewpoint is sustained throughout.** Everything is filtered through Kris’s limited understanding: Mum has “a human in Mum”; the baby clothes are “dress-up”; the dinosaurs are “strangled”; the ward is recognised only as the place “from when she dislocated her arm.” The examiner calls this “sensitivity and credibility” — the limitation of the viewpoint is the story’s technique, not a limitation of the writing.

**Language (the AO5 “precise and varied” vocabulary):**

- **Register control inside a child’s voice.** The narration stays simple, but the vocabulary is adult and exact where it matters: “the subject of asphyxiation was green”, “ushered to the car”, “prodding at the weird bundle”, “burrowing into Gran’s chest”. The joke of “asphyxiation” applied to a plush toy only works because the surrounding prose is plain.
- **Punctuation does structural work, not decoration.** Parenthesis for the deflating aside “(and Barney’s educational speech)”; the em dash to introduce the green dinosaur; the colon before the photograph’s caption; the mid-sentence question “Hadn’t they already attended church this morning?” which keeps the child’s logic audible.
- **Sentence variety is visible even in a short extract.** Long cumulative sentences (“She looked down to see herself being held by Gran’s wrinkly, soft arms”) sit beside three-word beats (“Gran was asleep.”) and one-line paragraphs used for the present-day interruption.
- **Dialogue is punctuated and attributed.** Speech marks are used throughout, with the speakers clear from context — exactly the failure the cohort commentary describes in weaker scripts (“too much direct speech without the necessary speech marks”).

**What is *not* doing the work:** there is no gore, no twist ending, no elaborate plot. The whole story is a birth and a photograph. That is the point worth teaching: this script wins because it chooses a small, credible event and controls it, which is precisely what Pearson says the more successful candidates did.

## What a learner should take from it (without copying it)

- **Choose one narrow moment you could actually know about.** A sibling’s birth, a move, a diagnosis, a results day. Pearson explicitly notes that stronger candidates “often chose topics that were within their experience and so more credible.”
- **Give yourself a frame.** Here: date → remembered scenes → present-day interruption → physical object holding the date. Any two of those will do; all four is why it reads as designed rather than narrated.
- **Hold one viewpoint, even a limited one.** A child’s misunderstanding generates detail for free. So does an unreliable adult, a visitor, or someone hearing the story second-hand.
- **Plan the revision; do not perform it.** This script’s page is full of crossings-out, and it still scored 30/30 — but the crossings sit in the first and last paragraph. The middle is clean because it was drafted in the plan, not on the page.
- **Do not imitate the exact title line.** “The day everything had changed” works here because the photograph carries the date. A closing line that arrives without a physical or structural anchor is the failure Pearson describes.
- **Length:** the transcribed text runs to roughly 480 words against the paper’s “approximately 400 words” — over, but not damagingly so. The guide length is a floor for development, not a hard cap.
