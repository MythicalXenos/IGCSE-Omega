> **The man with the brass weight**
>
> Mr Haddad was the science technician, which meant he was the person who set out the equipment before the lesson and put it away afterwards, and for two years I thought that was all he was.
>
> He was in his sixties, thin, with a moustache that had gone the colour of pencil shavings, and he wore a brown overall coat over his own clothes with a biro clipped to the breast pocket and a small brass weight, fifty grams, in the pocket itself, which he took out and balanced on his palm while he thought. I have never seen another person do that. He would stand at the bench with the weight in his hand, looking at a broken clamp or a mis-set balance, and he would not speak until he had decided something, and then he would say one sentence.
>
> The help he gave me was narrow and it changed my life. I was fifteen and I could not do practical work. I could recite the method and I could not carry it out: my hands shook when anyone watched, I spilled, I set the Bunsen too high, and by the spring term I had arranged my whole timetable to avoid the front bench. Mr Haddad noticed because he noticed equipment, and one Thursday he put a tray in front of me after school — beaker, funnel, filter paper, a wooden clamp — and said, *Do it slowly. I am not watching.* And then he turned his back and sorted a cupboard, and the sound of him sorting that cupboard is the sound I still use when I need to concentrate.
>
> He did that for eleven weeks. No praise, no explanation, no sympathy, and never once a comment on my nerves. When I got it right he put out the next tray. When I got it wrong he said, *Again,* and went back to the cupboard. The last week he stood up, took the brass weight out of his pocket, weighed a beaker of water in front of me, and said, *You are careful. That is worth more than being quick*, and put the weight away.
>
> What that help did was not to make me good at science, although I passed. It gave me a procedure for being frightened: put the equipment out, do it slowly, keep your hands busy and let someone else's back be turned. I use it in every exam I have taken since, and in two jobs, and once in a hospital corridor.
>
> I went back to the school last year. He had retired and the prep room had been cleared, and the new technician let me stand in the doorway, and I could not think of anything to ask her except whether they still had the brass weight. They did not.
