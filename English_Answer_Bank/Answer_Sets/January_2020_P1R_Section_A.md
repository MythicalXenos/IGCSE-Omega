### Q3 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Amin begins by anticipating people who “dismiss or pooh-pooh” a day devoted to friendship, then challenges them with the image that life is “a bleak, dusty desert” without friends. The metaphor makes life without friendship seem barren, while the playful “pooh-pooh” establishes a conversational, lightly humorous voice. The contrast between an ordinary Thursday night and “the most exciting day of the week” shows how friends can turn routine into something enjoyable; the unglamorous chores that follow make that contrast comic and familiar.

She next describes friendships as “the informal learning centres through which humans gain all kinds of information and experience”. The dash introduces a deliberately eclectic list: “acquiring wall-climbing skills needed in order to steal the neighbour’s fruit”, “analysing behavioural patterns of the opposite sex” and “sharing and revelling in bizarre similarities”, before ending with jokes “no one else gets”. Moving from a mischievous practical skill to social observation and private humour makes the learning seem wide-ranging and enjoyable rather than formal; the fruit theft keeps the tone playful. The parenthetical question “when he says he likes me does he really mean he likes me?” dramatizes the kind of social interpretation friends share. Amin then says friends come “in so many shapes and forms” and may arrive “completely unexpectedly”. The examples range from “that shy little girl who took the Class III entrance exam with you” to “the precocious eight-year-old who claimed to have two boyfriends”, then include “your kid’s class teacher”, work colleagues and “someone you just met at the bus stop”. This progression across ages and settings makes friendship seem open-ended rather than confined to a chosen peer group. The shift from a “core group of very close buddies” to “other less intense friendships” also recognizes different degrees of closeness and the varied roles friends can fill.

Amin then signals that these examples are only “scratching the surface”. Her accumulating metaphors—“shoulders you unashamedly cry on”, “cushioning”, a “calming voice”, a “pool of optimism” and “saviours”—present friends as emotional protection in increasingly serious situations. The progression from comfort to “complete despair” culminates in the stark statement that friends can be “the reason why you are alive”, making their importance sound profound rather than merely social.

The passage broadens its argument from private life to society: friendship is a “key ingredient” that helps societies function and prevents people “tearing each other apart”. References to hostile neighbours, aggressors and wars show the consequences when relations fail. At the end, the Winnie the Pooh example returns to the humorous “pooh-pooh” at the opening. The final list—friends help us “draw strength, regain hope” and find “unadulterated happiness”—ends positively, while “Try pooh-poohing that” turns the opening joke into a direct challenge to anyone who doubts friendship’s value.

**MS alignment check:** This response develops the MS’s non-exhaustive points on the informal opening, the desert metaphor, lists, contrasting registers, direct address, social/national friendships, Winnie the Pooh and the opening/ending “pooh-pooh” link (indicative content, MS p. 4). The AO2 Level 5 descriptor is on MS p. 5. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.

### Q6 — language and structure analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Rana first gives Aristotle authority by describing his “significant and lasting contributions” and calling him a “most promising student” who asked many questions and answered even more. The biographical praise and reference to his influence over more than 2,000 years encourage readers to take his ideas seriously. The article then organizes those ideas with headings: “The accidental friendships”, “The friendship of the good” and “All you need to know”. This structure moves from common relationships to the type the writer considers most desirable, then summarizes the argument for a general reader.

The writer does not dismiss friendships of utility and pleasure as worthless. He explains their everyday settings—work, older people, college and sports—and says Aristotle “didn’t necessarily see them as bad”; they can be “fine, and even necessary”. This reassuring qualification makes the account balanced. However, the phrases “lack of depth” and “there is far more out there” establish a hierarchy and prepare the reader for the “friendship of the good”.

That preferred friendship is “based on a mutual appreciation” of virtues and “often lasts until the end”. The contrast with relationships that change when a benefit or life phase ends presents virtue-based friendship as intentional and durable. Rana’s positive language—“beauty”, “pleasurable and beneficial” and “sweeter joys”—makes it sound emotionally rewarding, not simply morally admirable. The metaphor “when they do blossom” suggests that such relationships grow over time, reinforced by the trio “trust, admiration, and awe”.

Rana also makes the philosophy accessible through direct address: “You’re a lot more likely to connect” with someone after sharing hardship or seeing them grow. This brings an abstract idea into familiar experience. Finally, the compact closing contrast—life is “long enough” for most things but “too short for the wrong kinds of friendship”—turns the article’s argument into a memorable reason to value lasting, virtuous relationships.

**MS alignment check:** This response develops the MS’s points on Aristotle’s credibility, the headings, examples of friendship types, the reassuring treatment of accidental friendships, the promise of “far more”, positive language, the “blossom” metaphor, personal address and the summative ending (indicative content, MS p. 7). The AO2 Level 5 descriptor is on MS p. 8. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers present friendship as valuable, but Amin argues for its broad importance in everyday and public life, while Rana explains different kinds of friendship and judges which is most worthwhile. Text One opens by speaking directly to “you” and uses the humorous “pooh-pooh” and the “bleak, dusty desert” metaphor to make its case accessible and persuasive. Text Two is more formal and analytical: it introduces Aristotle’s authority, then uses headings to organize an explanation of utility, pleasure and “the friendship of the good”.

Their focus differs too. Amin presents friendship as a general source of companionship and support, listing friends who offer a shoulder to cry on, a “pool of optimism” or help in “complete despair”. She also widens the argument to countries and societies, where friendship helps prevent conflict and supports trade. Rana concentrates on the quality and duration of individual relationships. He explains that friendships based on utility or pleasure may be “fine, and even necessary”, but argues that virtue-based friendships are deeper and “often last until the end”. Text One is largely non-judgemental about the different people who can become friends; Text Two explicitly ranks types of friendship.

The writers also use different kinds of examples to make their ideas convincing. Amin moves from familiar routines, such as Thursday nights and chores, to the varied people who may become friends, then to nations and Winnie the Pooh. Her mixtures of colloquial humour and more formal phrases such as “emotional safety net” keep a wide-ranging argument lively. Rana uses examples such as work relationships, college friends and shared hardship to clarify Aristotle’s categories. His repeated qualifications make the explanation measured, while positive words such as “beauty”, “joy” and “trust” encourage readers to aspire to the virtuous form.

Both texts end by affirming friendship’s positive effect, but with different emphasis. Amin’s final accumulation—friends help people “draw strength, regain hope” and find “unadulterated happiness”—returns to the emotional support at the heart of her article. Rana concludes that life is “too short for the wrong kinds of friendship”, reinforcing his distinction between temporary and lasting bonds. Overall, Amin persuades readers that friendship is essential across personal and social life; Rana uses Aristotle’s framework to explain why some friendships are more valuable than others.

**MS alignment check:** This comparison is sustained across both texts and develops the MS’s points on Text One’s persuasive, general and personal perspective; Text Two’s informative, analytical treatment of friendship types; Text Two’s judgement of friendship quality; and both writers’ positive views of friendship (indicative content, MS pp. 9–10). It uses balanced references and addresses the AO3 Level 5 descriptor on MS p. 11. This is a constructed, unmarked answer.
