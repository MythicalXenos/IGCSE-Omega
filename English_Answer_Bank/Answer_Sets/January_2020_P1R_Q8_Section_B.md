## [CONSTRUCTED GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed article

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content and the January 2020 P1R report guidance, using verified details from both texts. It is **not** a Pearson script, has **not** been examiner-marked, and **no score is claimed**. It is an article with a headline and standfirst, no salutation or sign-off, and a school-website voice: informal but not sloppy, addressed to fellow students.

> # Friendship
> ### Three kinds of friend, and why the last one is worth waiting for
>
> Quick test. Think of the people you spoke to today. Now think of the ones you would ring at eleven at night if something went badly wrong. The gap between those two lists is the whole subject of this article.
>
> **The types of friends you actually have**
>
> Aristotle, who thought about this more carefully than most of us ever will, said friendship comes in three kinds. The first is **useful** friendship: the person you sit next to in chemistry because they take good notes, or the colleague who covers your shift. It works, it is genuinely useful, and it ends the moment the benefit does — which is why he thought it more common among adults. The second is **pleasure** friendship: the football team, the group chat, the people you laugh with until your ribs hurt. Aristotle noticed this kind is more common when you are young, and he was right that it can fade as quickly as the phase that produced it.
>
> The third kind he called the friendship of the good, and it is built on respect rather than usefulness or fun. It is the friendship that survives seeing someone at their worst, or the two of you coming through something hard together. These are the ones that last. Most of us have a couple. Some of us have one. If you are reading this and realising you have none yet, that is not unusual, and it is not permanent — but it is also the reason the next section matters.
>
> **Where you actually find them**
>
> Everywhere, which is the unhelpful but true answer. School is the obvious one: the girl who took the entrance exam the same morning as you, the boy who claimed to have two girlfriends in Year 4, the person in the year below who turns out to be the only other person who likes the same band. College, work, sports clubs, drama, volunteering, a random queue at a bus stop — all of it. Family counts too: cousins, and the friends your parents brought with them. What does not work is waiting to be found. Friendship is not a personality trait you either have or lack; it is a series of small decisions to talk to someone twice.
>
> **Why it makes your life better**
>
> Here is the part that sounds like a poster and is nonetheless true. Friends are the people you cry on without apologising, the ones who talk you down after a fight with your mum, and the ones who make a boring Thursday feel like the best night of the week. They change how you see things: you learn what other people's lives are actually like, not the version on their story. They make you braver — you try things you would not have tried alone. A good friendship can even stop a fight before it starts, because it is much harder to be cruel to someone whose side you have heard.
>
> Text One puts it better than I can: friendships are the reason some of us are still here. Winnie the Pooh, of all characters, was made the UN's Ambassador of Friendship, and his friends were a bear, a piglet, a donkey, an owl, a kangaroo, a rabbit and a boy — not one of them alike. That is the whole thing, really. You do not need to find your exact copy. You need people who are worth the effort, and you need to be worth theirs.
>
> So: message one person this week. Not the group chat — one person. See what happens.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

The MS list is explicitly non-exhaustive (“there are other possibilities… Reward all valid points”). Creditable alternatives not used in the model:

- **Types of friends:** “friendships between communities” and “friendships between countries/governments (for political or economic purposes)” are both MS-listed and untouched here; Text One develops the international angle at length (diplomatic ties, trade, war), so a candidate wanting that route has full source support.
- **Where you make friends:** “at social gatherings” is the one MS location not used. Any locally plausible setting — a mosque or church youth group, a part-time job, an online community with real-world meetups — is equally creditable when tied to the bullet.
- **Why friends matter:** “mutual respect”, “support in work/study situations”, “support in social situations” and “can make us positive” are MS points this article does not spell out in those terms. Text Two also supplies the sober version of the third bullet (a life “too short for the wrong kinds of friendship”), which is a valid alternative conclusion.
- **Form:** a candidate could reasonably include a byline, a pull-quote or a mini interview quote. The report rewards an engaging, audience-aware article rather than a fixed template; what it penalises is a piece that drifts into a letter, an essay or a summary.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO1:** all three bullets are developed in separate, structurally signposted sections; the *types of friends* bullet is built on the text’s Aristotle framework rather than a bare list, both texts are used, and specific details (utility/pleasure/good; the entrance exam; the bus stop; Winnie the Pooh) are reworded rather than lifted in whole sentences.
- **AO4:** the article announces itself as an article — headline, standfirst, bold subheadings, a quiz hook, direct address to a student readership, a closing call to action — with none of the letter furniture that would misrepresent the form. It is not too brief (the report’s named weakness) and the register holds from start to finish.
- **AO5:** varied sentence lengths with deliberate short paragraphs for pace, semicolons and dashes, correct apostrophes and articles, no comma splicing, and capital “I” throughout.
- **What is not claimed:** no Pearson or examiner mark, no level or score. The AO5 ceiling on this grid is Level 4, 7–8.
