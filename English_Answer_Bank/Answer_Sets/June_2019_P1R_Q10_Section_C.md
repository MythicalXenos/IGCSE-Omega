> **The Challenge**
>
> The bench is still there, third from the lock, though the wood has been replaced and the plaque has not. Arthur sat on it at ten past seven with a flask he did not want and a map he did not need. He had driven this road for thirty-one years, and the canal had been the thing he saw out of the window between stops.
>
> His daughter had said, on the phone, in the voice she used for him now: “You don't have to do it today.”
>
> He had said, “I know,” which was true, and had put the phone down, which was not an answer.
>
> The walk to the coast is twelve miles along the towpath. There is no hill in it and no hazard beyond the wet planks of the second bridge, and he had told himself for four years that he would do it, and had not, and the reason was not his knees.
>
> For the first two miles the morning was doing what mornings do: the water grey and flat, a heron lifting off like something being unfolded, a man on a narrowboat burning something in a pan. Arthur walked at the pace he had always walked, which his wife had called his inspector's walk, and by the third mile he had stopped noticing the view and started noticing the counting. Bridges. Locks. Swans. He counted them the way he used to count fares, and the counting did the thing counting does, which is to make a long stretch of time survivable.
>
> At the sixth lock he sat down and drank the tea he did not want, and found that he did want it. A woman with two dogs asked whether he was all right, and he said he was, and she said, “Only you looked like you were thinking,” and went on before he had to answer. He was grateful for that. He had spent four years being asked how he was by people who wanted a short answer, and the truth had never once fitted into the space available.
>
> The last three miles were bad. Not dangerous — nothing happened at all — but the towpath came out of the green tunnel and there was nothing between him and the sky, and the wind was against him, and the sea began to be a sound rather than a rumour. He walked with his head down and his hand on the rail of the sea wall like a man on a ship.
>
> Then the wall ended and there was water, and beyond the water the low grey line of the point, and gulls doing their pointless, beautiful work above it.
>
> He stood there for a long time. He had imagined this part as an arrival, with something said. There was nothing to say; his wife had been dead for four years and the coast had not changed and he was seventy-one and his feet hurt.
>
> What changed was smaller and more useful than that. He walked back to the station, bought a ticket, and on the train home wrote a list of the other eleven things he had told himself he would do one day.
>
> The challenge was never the walking.

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Challenge types (report's list):** exams; a quest; starting a business; illness in the family; sport; mountain climbing; travel; survival.
- **Endings:** success with a benefit; obsession and a fall (“too arrogant to take basic precautions”); failure that teaches something; the challenge abandoned honestly.

- **What to avoid:** dialogue that displaces the plot; a dark plot with no connection to the title; a prepared essay with the title dropped in; memorised descriptive paragraphs; an ending unrelated to the events; a challenge with no reason given.
