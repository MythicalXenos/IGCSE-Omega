## Exact QP prompt — Q8 (printed p. 15)

Section B instruction as printed: “Answer the question in this section.” / “You should spend 1 hour on this section.” / “Use ideas from BOTH Text One and Text Two in the Extracts Booklet to answer this question.”

> 8 Write a letter to your local newspaper explaining why you think there should be more opportunities for after-study activities in your local community.
>
> You should include:
> - the reasons why there are not enough activities
> - the types of activities that could be offered
> - the benefits of after-study activities.
>
> Think carefully about the purpose of your letter and the audience for whom it is intended.
>
> (30)

## [CONSTRUCTED GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed model letter

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content and the 2019 report guidance, using verified details from both texts. It is **not** a Pearson script, has **not** been examiner-marked, and **no score is claimed**. Following the report, it uses a salutation and sign-off without addresses, sustains the register throughout, and addresses the editor and readers rather than pretending the newspaper will fix anything itself.

> Dear Editor,
>
> I am writing as a student in our town who has spent four years walking past the same noticeboard outside the sports hall: one football club, one choir, and not much else. When your paper asked recently why teenagers spend so much time indoors, the implication was laziness. It is not. The truth is that there is very little for us to join, and there are good reasons why.
>
> First, the reasons. Some of them are attitudes. A London head teacher whose school now races yachts says that adults always ask “what if it goes wrong?” and never “what happens if it goes spectacularly well?” — and that fear of a damaged minibus or a cancelled trip is enough to stop a club before it starts. Some of them are financial: kit, venue hire and staff time all cost money, and few departments have any. Some are practical. Students worry they are not fit enough, or that they will be the worst in the room, or that the activity on offer is not the one they wanted, and so they never sign up. And some are simply about information: unless a teacher happens to mention it, most of us never find out what exists.
>
> Yet the activities we could offer barely need inventing. A state school in Haringey began with dinghy sailing in Poole Harbour, restored a 22-foot yacht bought on eBay, and ended up completing the Fastnet Race and competing at Cowes Week. Closer to home, we could run team sports, but also art, music, drama, science and maths clubs, a choir, a cadet unit, a chess or radio club, and volunteering with a local charity. Renovation is a genuinely popular option too: give a group a tired storage shed, a set of tools and a deadline, and you will not be able to get them to go home.
>
> The benefits are the strongest part of the case. Pupils who raised every penny for their sailing programme by giving between forty and fifty talks to clubs and suppliers learned how to speak to influential adults, and a crew picked on ability rather than friendship learned, in their teacher’s words, “interpersonal skills” and how to “manage themselves”; above all, they learned “resilience”. The same applies to any club. Responsibility, organisation, adaptability and the work ethic that comes from turning up every Tuesday are exactly what employers and universities say they want, and an activity on a college application says more about a student than a column of grades. Just as importantly, clubs mix people who would otherwise never speak: different year groups, different backgrounds, different abilities. That is how barriers come down.
>
> I can already hear the objection that we are overworked and that another club would add stress. It is a fair point, and the answer is not to pile on activities but to offer a range and let students choose one. The Haringey example also answers the money argument, since none of their funding came from the school budget: pupils, sponsors and local businesses paid for it. Risk can be managed with training, supervision and sensible kit. What cannot be managed is another decade of teenagers going home to a screen because nothing else was available.
>
> I am not asking your newspaper to solve this. I am asking your readers. Parents: ask the school what is on offer and offer to help run it. Local businesses: sponsor one club, and put your name on the minibus. Governors: protect one line in the budget for it. If we start asking “what happens if it goes spectacularly well?”, the answer is a generation of young people who can sail, build, organise and speak up for themselves.
>
> Yours faithfully,
>
> A Year 11 student

## Accepted alternatives recorded

The MS list is explicitly non-exhaustive (“there are other possibilities… Reward all valid points”), and the report confirms that candidates could use their own local knowledge. Any of the following are creditable alternatives to points used in the model, not errors:

- **Reasons:** worries about failure; lack of equipment; lack of time; too much to do. Only “negative attitudes”, “cost”, “fitness”, “might not like activities offered” and the information gap are used in the model; the remaining four are equally valid routes.
- **Activity types:** fundraising, yacht racing, sports clubs, art, music, drama, cadets, science, dinghy sailing and renovation all appear in the model in some form. Acceptable substitutes not used: dance (Text Two), French club (Text Two), maths as a club treasurer role (Text Two), and any locally plausible activity such as a debating society or community garden.
- **Benefits:** the model uses develop skills, speak to influential people, gain confidence-adjacent claims, work ethic, social skills, organise/adaptable, enjoyment-adjacent motivation, friendships, different backgrounds, overcoming problems, applications, future employment. Acceptable substitutes not used: “have good experiences”, “learn strength”, “develop friendships” as an explicit bullet-worded point.
- **Own examples:** the report records candidates arguing that social media and gaming have a negative impact, that too much housing is built on green space, and that school days involve sitting down too long. These are examiner-accepted local arguments, not off-task material — but they must be tied back to the three bullets.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO1:** all three bullets are addressed, with the first bullet given a full paragraph rather than a token sentence (the report’s Level 3 cap for omitting it), a wide range of points, and both texts used — Text One for the lived example and Text Two for the practical and informational obstacles.
- **AO4:** salutation and sign-off, no addresses (per the report), sustained first-person student voice throughout, direct address to readers in the final paragraph, rhetorical questions, listing, and a purpose that stays “argue for provision” rather than “order the newspaper to act”. The register is formal but not inflated, and it does not switch to speech/article mode.
- **AO5:** varied sentence structures, deliberate short sentences for emphasis, semicolons and dashes used for control, quotations integrated with punctuation, and no comma splicing, homophone or apostrophe errors of the kind the report lists.
- **What is not claimed:** this model has not been marked by Pearson or by an examiner, no level or score is asserted, and it should not be presented to a learner as a 30/30 script. Its AO5 ceiling is the printed Level 4 (7–8) band.
