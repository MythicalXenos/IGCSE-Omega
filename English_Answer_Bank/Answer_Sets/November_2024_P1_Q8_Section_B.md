## [CONSTRUCTED GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed school-website article

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the November 2024 report's Q8 commentary and the two source texts, with no quoted wording. **Not** a Pearson script, **not** examiner-marked, **no score claimed.**

> **Let's celebrate!**
>
> *Why the best week of the school year is not the one you think — and how to make more of them.*
>
> Something strange happens in the last week of the summer term. Nobody does any work. Somebody brings in a speaker. A teacher you have never spoken to cries slightly at an assembly. And a boy in my maths group, who has said perhaps nine words all year, finds out he got a six, and the corridor outside the hall makes a noise like a goal has been scored.
>
> **Why we should celebrate**
>
> It would be easy to file all of that under “nice but unnecessary” — a bit of decoration on the serious business of school. I do not think it is decoration. I think it is the serious business, or at least it is what holds the serious business together.
>
> Psychologists who study celebration say the urge to mark things is not a modern invention or a marketing trick; it appears to be built into us. We mark the good changes and, just as importantly, we gather around the bad ones. There is a reason for that: we are social animals, and celebrations are one of the few things that reliably strengthen the network we depend on. When you are low you tend to disappear — you stop replying, you eat lunch somewhere else — and the cure for that is not solitude. It is other people, in a room, doing something together.
>
> There is also a physical side to it, which I find genuinely interesting. Celebrating produces the chemicals you get from achievement and company; a hug releases one that lowers the stress hormone. So the celebration is not merely a reward for the good news. It extends the good news, and it takes the edge off the bad week. It gives a shape to a term that otherwise blurs into one long Tuesday.
>
> **What to celebrate**
>
> Everything, apparently. Birthdays are the obvious one, and they have a longer history than you would guess: pharaohs were said to be reborn as gods when they were crowned, so their coronation became their birthday and the party followed. Romans sent written invitations on wooden tablets. A cake for a fiftieth birthday once involved olive oil and grated cheese, which I would like us to bring back.
>
> Then the ones that matter more here. Results day. A driving test passed first time. Somebody's first payslip. A place at college. A teacher's last day after twenty-two years. Eid, Diwali, Christmas, New Year — the turn of the calendar is a four-thousand-year-old excuse to start again, and it began as an eleven-day festival, so if you thought one evening was a lot, imagine eleven.
>
> And then the ones that need inventing, which are the best ones. Someone got through a horrible week. Someone came back to school after being ill. Someone finally spoke in a seminar. Celebrate the small. Celebrate the silly. Celebrate the survived.
>
> **Ways to celebrate**
>
> It does not have to be a party, and it should not have to cost anything — a school where celebration means spending money is a school with a problem. So: say it out loud. A shout-out in assembly, a name on a board by the library, a message in the group chat that is actually about someone rather than a meme.
>
> Write it down. A card is the cheapest technology ever invented and it is the one people keep. The Romans were doing it two thousand years ago on wood; you have paper and a pen and, allegedly, an opposable thumb.
>
> Do something together that is not a lesson. A five-a-side match, a walk, a playlist for the coach, a table in the canteen with an actual cake on it. Eat something. Dress up, if you like — one writer points out that celebration is cathartic precisely because it lets people overeat and dress up and break the normal order of things for an afternoon.
>
> And plan the next one. The best celebrations have a date attached. Put the next one in the calendar before this one ends, and while you are there, set yourself one thing you want to be celebrating this time next year. Make it small enough to actually do. Most people abandon their plans because they have a wish instead of a plan, and you have far longer than January to make this work.
>
> **Your turn**
>
> The celebration board goes up outside the library on Monday. It will be blank. Put a name on it — a friend, a teacher, the caretaker who has been here longer than the building — and a reason in six words or fewer. Then come and tell me it did not make anybody's week. Go on. Celebrate something.

*Word count: 798 words including title, standfirst and subheadings.*

## Accepted alternatives recorded

- **Indicative content is not available for this paper**, so this section lists what the *report* and *texts* establish as legitimate, and nothing is attributed to a mark scheme.
- **Occasions:** the report names Valentine's Day, the birth of a new child, passing an examination, a new job or promotion, and “finding a reason… if your life was dull”. Religious and cultural festivals (Eid, Diwali, Christmas, New Year), national holidays, weddings, anniversaries, sporting wins and school events are all squarely within the texts and the task.
- **Ways:** anything from a hug, a card and a shared meal to an assembly shout-out, a party, an award, a trip, or a written message. The task says “ways to celebrate”, not “parties”, so a candidate may argue for quiet or free celebrations.
- **Unused Text One material:** the Bible reference to a pharaoh's feast around 3000 BCE (p. 2, lines 10–12); women's birthdays only becoming common from the 12th century (p. 2, lines 18–19); “trauma bonds people during these occasions” (p. 3, lines 18–20); the “automatic pilot” argument and gratitude practice (p. 3, lines 40–50).
- **Unused Text Two material:** the “mental reset” argument (p. 4, lines 15–18); giving things up rather than adding them (p. 5, lines 10–20, including limiting social media); self-care scheduling an hour a day or a day a week (p. 5, lines 42–48); rewards at milestones (p. 5, lines 52–55).
- **Register:** a school-website article can be warm, funny, campaigning or gently sentimental. What the report penalises is the form slipping into a speech, a letter or a promotional leaflet.
