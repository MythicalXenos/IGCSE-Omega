### Q3 — language, evidence and argument analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** The writer presents smartphone use as a serious issue for teenagers, but argues against treating phones themselves as wholly harmful. She opens with her personal experience of scrolling through social media and seeing a graphic about young people’s screen time. This establishes a familiar first-person voice, while the precise figures—“six hours for 8–10-year-olds”, “nine hours for 11–14-year-olds” and “7.5 hours for 15–18-year-olds”—make the scale of use seem measurable rather than guessed. The short sentence “These are shocking numbers” gives her reaction emphasis. She also acknowledges the irony of using a screen to reflect on screen time, presenting herself as self-aware rather than detached from the issue.

The phrase “Like many people” places the writer alongside her readers, and her own screen-time check makes the topic personal. She is “surprised” by the hours but also “uncomfortable with the blanket criticism” of phones. The hyperbolic phrase “terrible for humanity” represents an extreme view she does not share. Instead, she calls smartphones “useful, practical tools for communication and connection”. The paired positive adjectives acknowledge their value, while “tools” emphasizes their everyday function. Her balanced position is explicit: the positives are forgotten too easily, but the negative effects are real.

The next section accumulates consequences of extended screen time. Her confident adverb “definitely” signals that she treats the emerging evidence of harm as established, especially for younger users. The writer says phones can “distract children” from learning and real-world experiences and “drain valuable time”; she then links filtered posts with FOMO, constant comparison and “feeling low”, before mentioning cyberbullying and targeting vulnerable teens. The list moves from lost attention to emotional and social harms, making the issue appear broad. “Quite simply” gives the claim about wasted time a blunt, emphatic tone. She strengthens the discussion with references to experts, Professor Jean Twenge and research from 2010–2015, alongside dates such as 2010 and 2017. These details lend authority to her discussion of adolescent mental-health concerns; she then distinguishes the pandemic’s acceleration of those problems from earlier trends.

However, the writer calls an outright ban a “crude reaction”. That adjective suggests an oversimplified response to a complicated problem. She argues that bans are hard to implement, can invite public criticism and may prevent sensible regulation. The abstract nouns “freedom” and “choice” frame a competing principle, and her qualification “as long as it doesn’t harm others” shows that she is not dismissing young people’s safety. She also argues that banning phones would not prepare teenagers for a world “increasingly reliant on tech and screens”. The conditional “wouldn’t” presents a practical objection, not merely a defence of screen use.

The writer’s alternative is constructive: “open conversations”, education and more guidance. The inclusive “we need” makes this a shared responsibility, while the tricolon “parents, teachers and those in pastoral roles” spreads it across adults who can support teenagers. She lists feasible actions—screen-time limits, household rules, online-safety education and school no-phone policies—and presents the school policy as helping students focus on learning and “in-person experiences”, rather than as punishment. She then shifts attention from phones to the apps and companies behind them. “Perhaps” softens the transition, but “should be compelled” is a firmer call for regulation of companies whose apps target young people. She gives Florida as a specific policy example: the bill would ban social-media accounts for under-14s and require parental permission for 14- and 15-year-olds. The politician Paul Renner’s claim that adults must “step in for them” is included without explicit comment, leaving the reader to judge whether this intervention is justified and complicating her earlier emphasis on freedom and choice. The alliterative “wild west” idiom depicts the earlier lack of oversight as chaotic; “We know enough now” implies that stronger boundaries can be introduced. The final wording, “maintain the positive aspects” while “reducing the negative consequences”, restates her balanced aim. Ending with the measured judgement that more than six hours of screen time is “worrying” leaves the reader with a clear concern but not a demand to ban smartphones.

### Q6 — language, structure and experience analysis

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** The writer begins in the middle of a journey: “It happened while I was on a plane to Zagreb in Croatia.” The opening is brief and initially withholds what has happened, creating curiosity. She then describes the phone “randomly glitching out” before it “just… died completely”. The colloquial “glitching out” makes the failure sound sudden and familiar, while the ellipsis before “died completely” conveys the shock of the moment. “Maniacally jabbed” gives the writer’s reaction a frantic quality. Her short reassurance, “Life goes on”, seems optimistic, but the next sentence pivots with “But” as the reality of travelling without a phone “truly sank in”.

The repeated “no” in the list—“no boarding pass, no email access, no banking app, no contacts, no map or Uber app, no way to tell the time”—makes the practical losses accumulate. “Without my phone, I’d have nothing” sounds absolute, and “hurtling towards another country” adds speed and vulnerability to the situation. The writer calls the first twelve hours “a state of logistical panic”. Even after a relative and partner help her solve the return-trip problem and she borrows money, she insists, “But still, I had no phone, for – did I mention? – 72 hours.” The repeated duration shows how long the interruption feels to her, while the self-aware aside adds humour. She adds that this is the longest she has gone without her phone in at least a decade, emphasizing the novelty of the challenge. The alliteration in “little light-up box” makes the device seem both insignificant and reassuringly technological, capturing the comfort she expects it to provide.

When she tries to relax, she calls the experience “uncomfortable, obviously”; the casual “obviously” assumes a like-minded reader and keeps the voice conversational. Instead of checking her phone, she “just stared numbly into space”, a contrast that makes her dependence feel habitual and leaves her momentarily directionless. She has to “remember it with my brain” rather than take a photo, and not knowing the news or whether anyone is contacting her feels “like a phantom itch”. The simile gives the absence a nagging, almost physical quality. Her inability to “swipe through TikTok” leads her to “mentally swipe through my own memories”, a comic substitution that reveals how thoroughly the phone gesture has become routine. She ends the sequence by saying she “just sat with my thoughts”, presenting an ordinary offline activity as something unfamiliar. These descriptions invite sympathy, although a less sympathetic reader might find her reaction childish or churlish.

The account then widens from one trip to a shared habit. The figures “58 times a day” and “4 hours 37 minutes” put her experience in a wider context. She says conversations about phone addiction have faded “not because it’s no longer concerning … but because it’s too late”. The short, blunt statement “Ten-year-olds have iPhones now” makes phone use seem established from childhood. Calling phones “our constant companions” and saying that reducing use would be “impossible” presents them as part of ordinary life, not an occasional tool.

On the second day, however, she “actually started to get used to it”. The word “actually” marks this as a small surprise, and “It’s astonishing how quickly your body adapts” suggests that she can manage without the device. A list of alternatives—reading, visiting museums and “chewing on a huge plate of dim sum”—adds ordinary, sensory experiences to the account; she says she was “really savouring the taste”, making the small pleasure seem vivid. Yet she refuses a falsely uplifting ending: “I really missed having a phone.” The short sentence is direct and emotionally plain. Her qualification that she could function “at least on a brain-body level” without being “constantly attached” to a device is cautious: she could manage physically, while still feeling the phone’s emotional pull.

The greater difficulty is navigating without the phone. She states, “This is not a world that’s built for phone-free life.” She then gives practical examples: taxis must be booked at actual ranks, maps have to be checked, and she must “buy a paper train ticket” and remember the easiest route back. Her comparison to how she imagines “the ’90s” makes these tasks seem anachronistic, but “without everyone else living the same way” stresses the social mismatch. The parenthetical “which are few and far between” makes the taxi problem more concrete, while “frightening” and “scary” signal her unease. Calling the phone a “tiny digital entity” makes it seem both small and powerful over everyday life; the clause “that could fail us at any moment” underlines how fragile this vital connection is. Her rhetorical question—“what would have happened if I had?”—asks the reader to imagine being stranded, although she admits that she was not. The uncertainty makes the experience feel risky without claiming that the worst actually happened.

She buys an iPhone the next morning and is “immediately plugged back into the world”. The metaphor presents the phone as an instant connection and suggests how isolated she felt without it. Looking back at the 72 hours, she admits she had made no major revelations about reducing phone use and had actually wanted to spend more time scrolling. Her emphatic tricolon—“scrolling! Googling things! Absorbing pointless information from ‘online’!”—and exclamation marks make her return to unchanged habits comic and energetic. In the final paragraph, the personal account becomes a broader, pessimistic claim: “even if I wanted to spend less time on my phone, I actually couldn’t. None of us could.” The short sentences and inclusive “we” turn her experience into a shared one. “Locked into this life” suggests a trap, and “no easy way” leaves the reader with her sense that reliance is built into modern routines. Overall, the writer is able to adapt physically, but still misses her phone and sees its wider practical importance as difficult to escape.

### Q7 — comparison

**[OFFICIAL MARK-SCHEME WORKED ANSWER]** Both writers show that smartphones are embedded in modern life, but they approach the issue from different directions. Text One is a researched argument about teenagers’ screen time and how to reduce its harms; Text Two recounts one writer’s sudden loss of a working phone while travelling. Text One argues that banning phones is not the answer, while Text Two shows both how difficult phone-free life can be and how quickly a person can adapt to temporary disconnection.

Text One establishes the scale of young people’s use with figures such as “six hours for 8–10-year-olds” and refers to expert research on mental health. Those statistics give the argument a public, evidence-based perspective. Text Two also supplies figures—people checking phones “58 times a day” and scrolling for “4 hours 37 minutes”—but its main evidence is personal: the writer’s phone dies on a flight to Zagreb, leaving her without a boarding pass, email, banking app or map. The contrast is between Text One’s research about a population of teenagers and Text Two’s first-person account of one traveller’s practical dependence.

The writers also differ in how they weigh the benefits and harms. Text One acknowledges that smartphones are “useful, practical tools for communication and connection”, then lists harms such as distraction, lost time, FOMO, comparison and cyberbullying. The writer’s balanced wording—she is “surprised” by her own screen time but “uncomfortable with the blanket criticism”—makes her position measured rather than anti-phone. Text Two dramatizes what the phone provides by repeating the list of things the writer cannot access. Her sentence “Without my phone, I’d have nothing” sounds absolute, although the story later shows that she does adapt and can function without being constantly attached to the device. Both texts therefore recognize the phone’s importance, but Text One focuses more on the harms of overuse, while Text Two emphasizes reliance and the practical benefits of having a working phone.

Their solutions and outlooks are notably different. Text One calls banning phones for under-16s a “crude reaction”, argues that bans would not prepare teenagers for a technology-dependent world, and offers alternatives: guidance for families and schools, limits, online-safety education, no-phone policies and action on the apps companies provide. Its final aim is to preserve the positives while reducing the negatives. Text Two is less optimistic about changing behaviour: “Ten-year-olds have iPhones now” and phones are “our constant companions”. The writer does manage three days without one, but the following morning she buys a replacement and says, “None of us could” simply spend less time on phones. Her final metaphor, “locked into this life”, sounds more resigned than Text One’s proposals for practical boundaries.

The forms reinforce these perspectives. Text One moves from striking statistics and the writer’s own screen-time habits to research, counterarguments and specific guidance; its inclusive “we” makes the issue collective. Text Two follows the chronology of a single trip, using colloquial phrases such as “randomly glitching out”, comic self-awareness about returning to “scrolling!” and “Googling things!”, and a final shift from “I” to “None of us”. The story invites readers to imagine the inconvenience of phone failure, while the article asks them to consider how teenagers can be protected without losing useful technology. Both writers are concerned with smartphones’ place in everyday life. Text One closes with the measured judgement that more than six hours of daily screen time is “worrying”, after proposing education and practical boundaries; Text Two ends with “no easy way”, a more resigned view of escaping society’s dependence on phones.

**MS alignment check:** This comparison develops the MS’s contrasts between teen screen use and a phone-free travel experience, expert evidence and personal experience, harm and reliance, Text One’s guidance/solutions and Text Two’s more pessimistic outlook. It also recognizes the shared positive functions of phones and the writers’ use of them, with balanced references to both texts (MS pp. 13–14).
