## [CONSTRUCTED GRADE 9 TARGET] Constructed website contribution

**Label:** original writing for this exact task, built from the MS indicative content and the June 2022 report guidance, using verified details from both texts. **Not** a Pearson script, **not** examiner-marked, **no score claimed.**

> **Why I proofread my texts now (and why you should too)**
>
> I have a confession for this website. Last year I sent a message to a group chat that was supposed to say “I'll be there at eight”, and instead said “I'll be there at eight?” — with a question mark, because my thumb slipped. Four people asked whether I was cancelling. One asked whether I was annoyed. Nobody asked whether I was coming. One character cost me an evening of explaining, and it is the reason I now read anything longer than a text message twice before I press send.
>
> That is the whole argument of this contribution: unclear writing is not a small thing, and it is not really about rules. It is about whether the person on the other end can tell what you meant.
>
> **What unclear writing actually costs you**
>
> The damage starts small and spreads. Punctuation that could go two ways makes your reader do the work of guessing, and readers who have to guess usually guess wrong — or give up. Miss the comma and “Shall we eat, Grandma?” becomes the far less comfortable “Shall we eat Grandma?”. Emojis cause the same trouble in a different alphabet: use the wrong one and a friendly sentence arrives sounding sarcastic, or worse, hostile, when you meant nothing of the kind. Send a wall of unbroken text and the message gets skimmed, then ignored. And underneath all of that sits the judgement nobody voices: if you did not take care over the sentence, what else did you not take care over?
>
> **Technology has not made this worse. It has made it faster.**
>
> There is a lazy version of this argument that says phones ruined writing. I do not believe it. Twenty years ago most people barely wrote at all — you rang, or you met. Now almost everyone writes, constantly, to a real audience, and adjusts what they write depending on who is reading: nothing like the message you send your friends, everything like the one you send your head of year. That is practice at adapting register, and most people get surprisingly good at it. Emojis are not the death of English; they are gestures, doing the work a raised eyebrow does in speech, and they do it concisely. Abbreviations that once set off alarm bells — *lol*, *idk* — are now simply part of a register we switch in and out of. We did not lose the formal sentence. We gained a whole informal one alongside it.
>
> **And yet grammar still matters**
>
> It matters because it is the cheapest signal of competence available. One careful sentence tells a reader you are attentive, and one careless sentence tells them the opposite, whether or not that is fair. Employers are not reading your CV for the pleasure of it. Grammar is also, bluntly, a courtesy: it is the difference between making your reader work and doing that work for them. And on a day when it is done well, it is more than courtesy — the English language is a genuinely beautiful instrument, and it deserves the effort.
>
> So: reread before you send. Put the comma where it belongs. Label the emoji. Your reader will never thank you, but they will understand you, and that is the point.
>
> **Over to you — what is the worst misunderstanding a missing comma has ever caused you? Tell us below.**
>
> *Word count: 570 words including the title line.*

## Accepted alternatives recorded

The MS list is explicitly non-exhaustive, and the report expressly permits merging bullets 1 and 3.

- **Merging bullets:** a candidate may run “problems caused by unclear communication” and “why good grammar matters” as one argument, provided both are fully covered — the report calls this “acceptable”.
- **Other personal examples:** autocorrect disasters, a mistyped name on a form, a message read as rude, a badly punctuated exam answer marked down, the “let’s eat Grandma” family of jokes, misread tone in a group chat.
- **Unused Text One material:** the “three groups” taxonomy (Purists, Rebels, Don’t-know-don’t-care, p. 2 line 19 to p. 3 line 6) is lively, quotable and entirely unused here; a candidate could build the whole contribution around those three types.
- **Unused Text Two material:** the five kinds of internet user (Old, Full, Post-, Semi-, Pre-internet, p. 4 lines 21–27) and McCulloch’s “no moral virtue in using language in the most uptight way” (p. 4, lines 12–13) both offer a stronger counter-argumentative edge than this model uses.
- **Style:** advisory, humorous, confessional or polemical are all within the report’s description of successful contributions; what it demands is a sustained register and a clear sense that this is a website contribution.

## Why this model targets the top bands — and what it does not claim

- **AO1:** all three bullets are separately developed, each anchored in the text the report identifies as the better source for it, with personal material added as the report praises — and no lifted sentences.
- **AO4:** the form is unmistakably a contribution: a first-person, self-deprecating opening; subheadings that carry the argument rather than decorate it; direct address throughout; a closing question to readers. Crucially it never resembles an essay, and it draws no fake website furniture, which the report says cannot be rewarded.
- **AO5:** the register includes deliberate ambition (“the cheapest signal of competence available”, “a different alphabet”, “an instrument”), accurate commas and dashes, correctly punctuated quotations, and no comma splices or missing articles. The word *grammar* is spelled correctly throughout — a specific error the report singles out.
- **Not claimed:** no examiner mark, level or score. The two published locators (30/30 p. 33; 15/30 p. 35) are Pearson's own marks on scripts that are not reproduced; this constructed answer is not compared to them as if it had been marked.
