## Part 1 — What the question is

**QP, p.21 (May 2025), verbatim:**
> **9** 'New opportunities present new challenges.' Discuss.
> (Total for Question 9 = 30 marks)

**Section C rubric, verbatim:** "Answer ONE question from this section. You should spend 1 hour on your chosen question. **Do not re-tell events from Text One or Text Two in the Source Booklet.** Write approximately 400 words."

**MS p.21, verbatim:**
> "As no audience is specified, the examiner is assumed to be the audience.
> Candidates are free to agree or disagree with the statement and may present a variety of arguments.
> Content may include references to: we are constantly presented with different types of new opportunities whether at home, in the workplace and socially; challenges can involve learning a new skill, developing new friendship groups, getting out of your comfort zone; depending on the nature of the new opportunity some might be too difficult or unappealing; it is better to be safe and happy without challenging yourself.
> Examiners should be open to a wide range of interpretation."

**Three consequences, all quoted:**
1. **No audience named** → you are writing to the examiner. Not a speech, not a letter, not a blog. First person is fine.
2. **"may present a variety of arguments"** → both sides is legitimate. It is not a debate you must "win", and it is not a lecture you must stay neutral in.
3. **You may not use the source texts.** June 2023 ER: less successful candidates "relied heavily on the texts for their ideas."

**Read the middle column.** Twenty marks and **not one of them mentions argument, opinion, sides, balance, or evidence.** The whole AO is: *is there a purpose, and is it being realised?* Every "how do I structure a discussion" question is really a question about making the purpose visible.

### The banned openings, quoted by name

> June 2025: "Some opened with a **philosophical quotation** or with their **'most crucial reason'**."
> June 2024 / 2025: "'**I am going to be making strong and robust points on my stance**', '**these points fall short of the mark because…**', '**as the dust settles, where do we find ourselves?**'… '**indubitably exigent**'."
> Nov 2024: "'**Clearly a topic like this calls for deep thinking**', '**It is important to tell people what side they are on**', '**I am going to present some robust points**'… '**I will continue to convince you to switch to my side**'."

Why they fail, in their words:
- June 2024: "These phrases were ineffective as they were **pompous and overly formal and broke up the flow of the candidate's argument**."
- Nov 2024: "It seemed that these candidates were **more engaged in remembering the scaffolding than they were in constructing a considered argument**."
- June 2025 Introduction: "**heavily scaffolded responses were seen to Question 9 which also should be discouraged as they do not allow candidates to develop their own ideas.**"

**The test for any sentence you write in the intro:** does it contain the actual words or ideas of the statement? If the sentence would work equally well on any other question, delete it.

## Part 5 — The two real 17/30 scripts, dissected

Both complete, both authentic, both from June 2023 (`Verified_Candidate_Responses/June_2023_Section_A.md`). These are the floor. Read them until you can see the failure coming.

### Script A — "Fresh air and exercise are the keys to happiness." Discuss. 17/30 (AO4 11/20 L3, AO5 6/10 L3)

> "**Some people would agree that fresh air and exercise are the keys to happiness but I understand why some wouldn't.** Some people might assume that by exercise people mean you have to be skinny/fit to be happy, which is of course very untrue. Although many researches do show that physical exercise can improve mental health."
> "**Fresh air and just being outside in the sun will give you a good dose of vitamin D.** A vitamin D deficiency has many symptoms including and depression therefore fresh air/increasing vitamin D will infact make you happier. However, some could say that getting fresh air/going outside would decrease your mood depending on the weather. For example if it was raining you would obviously feel sad maybe a bit irritated."
> "**Being cooped up indoors can increase stress levels** and make it harder to sleep at night which obviously will increase fatigue. There are so many things to do with staying indoors and not being active that cause sadness and depression."
> "**Yet I still do not believe that fresh air and exercise are the keys to happiness.** My reasoning for this is, there are many different things that make everyone happy. Not everybody has the same brain, body, likes or dislikes. For example, a person with social anxiety will not always get happiness from going outside. Some people get the most amount of happiness from just sitting down and watching their favourite movie tucked in their bed."
> "**Some may say that,** never mind a singular persons likes, its human nature to spend all your time outdoors and moving."
> "**In conclusion, my opinions on 'Fresh air and exercise are the keys to happiness' are that it is false,** those are not the only things you need to be happy."

**Five failures, each with a fix:**

1. **Intro announces a stance instead of the issue.** "Some people would agree… but I understand why some wouldn't" = I have not decided. → **Fix: sentence 1 says what the statement claims about the world; sentence 2 says what you'll argue.**
2. **Paragraphs are topics, not arguments.** Vitamin D / weather / being indoors / social anxiety / what others say / conclusion. Each one starts a new subject. → **Fix: each paragraph must answer the previous one. ¶2 begins where ¶1 stopped.**
3. **No example is developed.** Social anxiety is one sentence. The movie in bed is one sentence. → **Fix: one example, six sentences, with a name, a place and a consequence.**
4. **The counter-case is one line in a corridor.** "Some may say that… its human nature" is a sentence long, unexplored, then dropped. → **Fix: give the other side its own paragraph and take it seriously for five sentences before answering it.**
5. **Conclusion = the intro, second-hand.** "my opinions… are that it is false" tells you nothing you didn't read in paragraph four. → **Fix: the conclusion states the modified claim — what you believe now that you didn't believe at the start.**

### Script B — "You do not need to earn a lot of money to be successful." Discuss. 17/30 (AO4 11/20, AO5 6/10)

> "**I partly do agree with this statement,** As I once Mansa Musa exclaimed on his death bed, 'Starting from scratch makes it more worthy.' 'The lower the investment the worthier the result'"
> … "A study conducted by university of Ohio in 2014 shows that 76% of successful people had started with less money." … "a survey conducted by Forbes in 2012 showed…" … "**without it you are nothing.**" … "**Although I do agree money isnt important** it surely gives you a boost towards your goal."

**Two extra failures:**
6. **Invented research earns nothing.** The examiner credits the *technique* ("supports points made with references to research and the use of statistics") and still puts it at AO4 11/20. **Three fabricated statistics did not lift it above Level 3.**
7. **Contradiction inside four lines.** "without it you are nothing" then "money isnt important." Nov 2024 names this exactly: "began by agreeing with the quotation but contradicted themselves later."

## Part 6 — The method

### The plan (4 minutes, in the answer booklet, words only)

| Slot | What goes in it | Words in the answer |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | What the statement claims about the world — in your own words, and the **one word** you'll put pressure on | 0 (it becomes your intro) |
| **2** | The **best case for** it, with **one named example** (person, place, consequence) | 90–110 |
| **3** | **Why** it happens — the mechanism. This is where marks are won, because it's the paragraph nobody else writes | 90–110 |
| **4** | The **case against** — the exception, the cost, the person it fails | 90–110 |
| **5** | **Your answer** to slot 4, and the **modified claim** you end on | 70–90 |
| | **Intro written last of all, from slot 5** | 40 |

**Do not** write a plan longer than this. Nov 2024 and June 2025 both say long plans are "not a good use of time".

### The intro — two sentences, three shapes

**Shape A — accept, then narrow.** (safest, works on every Q9 in the archive)
> S1: *"[Statement's claim], and nobody who has [done the obvious thing] would deny it."*
> S2: *"What the statement hides is [the qualification]; [the thing most people miss]."*
Example on this paper: *"Every new opportunity brings a difficulty with it, and nobody who has moved school or started a job would deny it. What the statement hides is the order: the difficulty usually arrives first, and the opportunity is what we find inside it."*

**Shape B — attack one word.**
> S1: *"The word [X] is doing all the work in that sentence."*
> S2: *"[Why the word is wrong or too strong], which is why [your line]."*
Example: *"The word 'present' is doing all the work. It suggests opportunity and challenge arrive together, like a gift with a bill stapled to it, when most people meet the difficulty first and only call it an opportunity afterwards."*

**Shape C — one concrete case, then the general claim.**
> S1: a specific person in a specific situation (2–3 short sentences).
> S2: *"That is not an exception; it is the shape of [the statement's subject]."*
Example: *"A student is offered a place at a college four hundred miles from home. Nothing about that is hard until the second week, when the library is full of strangers. That is not an exception; it is the shape of every opportunity worth taking."*

**All three pass the same test:** both terms of the statement appear, in your own words, and the sentence could not be pasted onto another question.

**Banned in the intro:** rhetorical question that names nothing · dictionary definition · "since the dawn of time" · "both sides will be discussed" · any reference to your own stance machinery ("my argument", "I will now") · a quotation you half-remember.

### The body — four sentence-roles, repeated

Every paragraph, in order:

1. **Claim** — one sentence, no hedging, states the point of the paragraph.
2. **Evidence** — one named example, or one hypothetical ("imagine a nurse who…"), or one analogy. June 2024 credits "analogies" explicitly.
3. **Analysis** — *why* the example shows the claim. Two sentences. This is the part candidates skip and it is where AO4 Levels 3→4→5 live.
4. **Link** — one sentence that pushes to the next paragraph, or concedes ground.

**Sentence-stem bank — connectives only** (Jan 2023 credits these by name: 'On the one hand…', 'Whilst', 'Conversely', 'For example'):
> On the one hand · Conversely · Admittedly · Even so · This is not to say · The difficulty is · What follows from this is · It would be dishonest to pretend · The stronger objection is · In that case · That is only true when

**Use none of these:** "I am going to", "my stance", "robust points", "as the dust settles", "it is important to note that", "in today's society", "indubitably", "not only… but also" (unless you mean it).

### Rhetoric — six devices, each doing a job

| Device | Job | On this statement |
|---|---|---|
| **Concession** | proves you can see the other side | "It would be dishonest to pretend that caution has no cost — turning down a place you cannot afford is not timidity, it is arithmetic." |
| **Definition pressure** | wins the argument by owning a word | "The statement uses 'opportunity' as though it were always a gift; often it is only a door someone has to open in the dark." |
| **Hypothetical** | gives you an example you can control | "Imagine a girl offered a scholarship four hundred miles from home. In October she is lonely; by June she has built a life out of unfamiliar parts." |
| **Tricolon** | rhythm and range in one sentence | "New work, new country, new language: each arrives with its own invoice." |
| **Question that names the terms** | engages without being a hook | "If the challenge is what makes the opportunity worth having, can it really be described as a cost?" |
| **Short sentence after a long one** | forces the reader to stop | "Challenges are the weather." |

Two rules: **every device must contain the statement's terms**, and **no device may be lifted from a revision sheet** — that is the whole difference between "the use of questions" scored and "as the dust settles" penalised.

### The conclusion — resolve, don't restate

Three sentences:
1. **What you now think**, stated plainly and including the qualification.
2. **The strongest thing the other side got right** — one clause.
3. **The line to finish on** — short, quotable, no "in conclusion".

Example: *"So the statement is true, with one correction. New opportunities do not usually manufacture new challenges; they put you in the position where the old one — that growth is uncomfortable and cannot be faked — can no longer be avoided. The opportunity is not the reward for surviving the challenge. It is the door the challenge is standing in."*

**Do not begin with "In conclusion".** It's a wasted sentence and it invites the reader to skim.

## Part 7 — The 60-minute clock

| Minutes | Job |
|---|---|
| 0–4 | Plan in the slots |
| 4–46 | Write: intro last, body first if that helps. 40 words per minute is not needed — you write at ~27 wpm, so 400 words ≈ 15 minutes of actual pen |
| 46–54 | **AO5 pass**: punctuation, apostrophes, capitals, comma splices, spelling of basic words |
| 54–58 | **AO4 pass**: does every paragraph have claim + evidence + analysis + link? Is the concession a real paragraph? Does the conclusion say something new? |
| 58–60 | Cross out nothing. Leave it. |

The word count is "advisory" (exam reports say so explicitly). Do **not** count words. Do **not** cross out to hit 400.

## Part 8 — The 20-minute drill (do this before you sleep)

1. **4 minutes.** Take the statement. Fill the six slots with **words only**, not sentences.
2. **6 minutes.** Write the intro in **all three shapes** (two sentences each). Pick the one whose sentence 2 commits hardest.
3. **10 minutes.** Write **only the concession paragraph** — five sentences, claim + one named example + two analysis sentences + link. Not the whole essay.

Then stop. Marked cold tomorrow, not tonight.

Why the concession paragraph and not the opening? Because it's the paragraph both 17/30 scripts don't have, it's the one the examiners name as the difference between one-sided and balanced, and it's the hardest to write — so it's the one worth rehearsing.
