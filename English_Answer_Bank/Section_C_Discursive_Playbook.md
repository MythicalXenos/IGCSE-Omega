## 2. What examiners reward — verbatim

**Structure and openings**
- "These responses had **a clear structure with a strong introduction and conclusion**." (June 2023, May 2025)
- "**Thoughtful introductions**, logically ordered points, and effective conclusions." (Nov 2024)
- "Ideas were **well sign posted** and the reader was appropriately addressed, with a range of ideas." (Nov 2020)
- "Included sentence starters such as: 'On the one hand…', 'Whilst', 'Conversely', 'For example', suggesting that they had a good understanding of what they [were doing]." (Jan 2023)

**Argument**
- "Adopted a **persuasive and argumentative tone** and had clearly been prepared to write this kind of response." (June 2023, May 2025)
- "Generally took a **broader view** and looked at the positives and negatives… and generally came to a **more balanced judgement**." (June 2023)
- "Developed a **balanced and mature argument**, often **acknowledging that challenges are not always negative** but may foster personal development." (May 2025)
- "**Considering counter arguments** and making a balanced conclusion." (Nov 2024)
- "Structured their responses coherently with **well-balanced arguments** that fully supported their views… able to write **in depth and breadth**." (Jan 2023)

**Technique**
- "A good range of rhetorical techniques… **the use of questions, expert opinion, anecdotes and listing**, to convince the reader to their own side." (June 2023, May 2025)
- "**Persuasive language features such as rhetorical questions, counterarguments, and analogies.**" (Nov 2024)
- "Used **well-integrated anecdotes or hypothetical examples**" and an "**engaging, confident voice**". (May 2025)
- "**Detailed examples**… e.g. having to learn a new language and make new friends." (May 2025)

**The 26/30 Q9 exemplar's own commentary (May 2025, p.45)**
- AO4: "communicates successfully throughout and is **sharply focused on purpose**, with some sophisticated use of form, tone and register. The response meets all of the Level 4 criteria and **the strong sense of purpose moves it into Level 5**."
- AO5: "Structural and grammatical features are used cohesively and deliberately. An extensive range of vocabulary is used and punctuation is accurate."
- Examiner note: "Note how the response **maintains the focus on discussing the statement** and provides **a range of ideas**."

## 3. What examiners penalise — verbatim

**Argument failures**
- "Problems with both **maintaining a clear argument and structuring** their responses." (repeated every series)
- "**One sided** or poorly supported." (June 2023, 2024, 2025)
- "Points that were quite predictable and **found it difficult to sustain an argument, often leading to repetition**… muddled ideas or were very brief." (June 2019, 2022, 2024)
- "**Some began by agreeing with the quotation but contradicted themselves later.**" (Nov 2024)
- "Some candidates **read 'challenges' as problems** and stated that these were not interesting, but inevitable." (Jan 2023)
- "Sometimes these were **too personal and not discursive, sounding more like a narrative**." (Jan 2023)
- "Some wrote **narrative responses**." (May 2025)
- "Offered **lengthy 'sources' rather than their own opinion**." (Nov 2024)
- "**Relied heavily on the texts** for their ideas." (June 2023) — Section C says "Do not re-tell events from Text One or Text Two".

**Openings penalised by name** — this is the most specific evidence that exists about introductions:
- "Some opened with a **philosophical quotation** or with their **'most crucial reason'**."
- "A few scripts used phrases that had been **pre-taught**… '**I am going to be making strong and robust points on my stance**', '**these points fall short of the mark because…**', '**as the dust settles, where do we find ourselves?**' … '**indubitably exigent**'."
- Nov 2024 adds: "quotations at the beginning such as '**Clearly a topic like this calls for deep thinking**', '**It is important to tell people what side they are on**', '**I am going to present some robust points**'. Structural phrases… '**I will continue to convince you to switch to my side**'."
- Why it fails, in the examiners' words: "**These phrases were ineffective as they were pompous and overly formal and broke up the flow of the candidate's argument**" (June 2024); "**It seemed that these candidates were more engaged in remembering the scaffolding than they were in constructing a considered argument**" (Nov 2024); "These responses **do not allow candidates to fully demonstrate their own writing skills**."

**Heavy pre-preparation**
- June 2024 programme summary: least successful candidates "produced **heavily scaffolded or learned essays** in response to Section C (Questions 9 and 10)."

**AO5 — the same error list every series**
- "**Comma splicing**; missing or misused **apostrophes**; problems with **homophones**; not capitalising '**I**'; missing capital letters at the start of sentences; subject–verb agreement; verb tenses."
- "**Punctuation and vocabulary choices sometimes lacked variety.**"
- "**Over-ambitious vocabulary which was not effective or appropriate.**"
- "Less successful responses **often did not paragraph at all**."
- "Candidates should be encouraged to **proofread**."
- "The word count is **advisory**… assessed on their merits, not on the number of words." Do not count words or cross out to hit 400.

## 4. Reconciling the contradictions

**(a) "Be persuasive and argumentative" vs "come to a balanced judgement."**
Not a contradiction if read as a sequence: the **tone** is committed throughout, and the **judgement** at the end is qualified. What is penalised is *one-sidedness* — never conceding — and also *hedging* — conceding everything and concluding nothing. The pattern the reports describe is: take a line, put the other side properly, then answer it.

**(b) "Use rhetorical questions" vs "don't open with a pre-taught hook."**
Both are in the reports. "**the use of questions**" is credited as a technique (June 2023, May 2025) and "**rhetorical questions**" again in Nov 2024. What is penalised is a *ready-made phrase that isn't about the statement*: "I am going to be making strong and robust points on my stance" could open any essay on any topic. **The test is whether the sentence contains the actual terms of the statement.** A question that names those terms works; a question that would fit any topic does not.

**(c) "Sentence starters were credited" vs "pre-taught phrases were penalised."**
Jan 2023 credits 'On the one hand', 'Whilst', 'Conversely', 'For example'. Those are **connectives** — they mark the argument's structure and cost nothing. The penalised phrases are **personality** ("my stance", "robust points", "as the dust settles"). Rule: connectives fine, performance not.

**(d) School guides say "open with a rhetorical question / imagine yourself in the situation".**
That is common advice and it is not contradicted by the reports, but it is not supported by them either. The reports' only concrete statements about openings are the penalties in §3. Do not treat a school guide as examiner evidence.

## 5. The procedure

**Word budget for ~400 words:** intro 40 · ¶1 100 · ¶2 100 · ¶3 100 · conclusion 60.

**Intro — two sentences, no more.**
- **S1: name the two terms of the statement and say what the relationship between them actually is.** Not "I am going to discuss…". Not "Since the dawn of time…". Not "Have you ever…?" unless it names the terms.
- **S2: state the line you will take, including the qualification you will end on.** This is the "strong introduction" the reports credit, and it prevents the "began by agreeing then contradicted themselves later" penalty, because the qualification is announced up front.

**Body — three moves, in this order:**
1. **The case for the statement**, with one detailed example (May 2025 credits "detailed examples… e.g. having to learn a new language and make new friends"). Depth over breadth: one example developed beats five listed.
2. **The concession** — where the statement is too simple, wrong, or only sometimes true. This is the paragraph that stops the answer being "one sided".
3. **The answer to the concession** — what survives of your line after it. This is the paragraph that stops the answer being a hedge.

**Conclusion — resolve, don't restate.** The 17/30 script ends "my opinions on '…' are that it is false, those are not the only things you need to be happy" — that is the opening sentence, second-hand. A conclusion should state the *modified* version of the claim you now hold.

**Rhetoric — use four, each doing work:** a question that names the terms; a list of three for range; a hypothetical ("Imagine a student who…"); a direct concession ("It would be dishonest to pretend…"). Connective signposting throughout: On the one hand / Conversely / Admittedly / Even so.

**AO5 five-minute check:**
1. Every sentence has an end-stop; no comma splices.
2. Apostrophes: it's / its, don't / dont, people's.
3. Capital I; capitals at sentence starts; none mid-sentence.
4. At least one semicolon, one colon, one dash, one question mark.
5. Sentence lengths varied — at least one under six words.
6. Paragraphs visible: 5.

## 6. Evidence gaps — stated, not hidden

- **No top-band Q9 script exists in readable form in this repo.** The May 2025 Q9 exemplar (26/30) is reproduced as page images in the examiner report and cannot be transcribed. So there is **no authentic example of a top-band Q9 introduction** available here.
- The only two readable authentic Q9 scripts score **17/30** and both are weak in the same way (see `Verified_Candidate_Responses/June_2023_Section_A.md`). They are reliable evidence of what fails, not of what succeeds.
- Everything in §5 about *how* to build the intro is **inference from the grids plus the reports' penalties and credits**, not observation of a marked script. It should be taught as such.
- The constructed model in `Answer_Sets/May_2025_P1_Q09_Section_C.md` is **my writing, unmarked**, and is not evidence of anything.
