# Section B — the four forms, with a full model each

3 Nov 2026. Every question below is verbatim from an archive paper.
Every model is written to the same standard; marks are my application of the real grids.

## The anchor: a real 24/30 Section B script
From "English (2).pdf" p.14-18, marked by an examiner:
Q8 talk to peers about the ways smartphones are used.
Box on p.14 reads: `8 Q08AO1` `9 Q08AO4` `7 Q08AO5` = **24/30**.
All three bullets ticked. What earned the 9 on AO4: an opening address to the audience, the
speaker naming himself, second person throughout, and a closing return. What held it to 7 on AO5:
"a really fascinating recipe", "set a rules", "Do not worry, I personally also am a victim of this
problems", "an amazing solution to this" struck and rewritten.
So: FORM and ADDRESS earn AO4. Accuracy earns AO5. Bullet coverage earns AO1. Three separate tens.

## The three tens, restated
- AO1 /10: all three bullets, each developed, with ideas drawn from BOTH texts and your own.
- AO4 /10: the named audience and the named form, held from first line to last.
- AO5 /10: the nine-item accuracy list.

## The six form features that earn AO4 (learn these, not templates)
| Form | Must have |
|---|---|
| Talk | greeting to the audience; "I" as speaker; direct address "you"; rhetorical question; closing thanks or call to action |
| Article | headline; byline or standfirst; subheadings; third person address to a general readership OR "we"; closing line that lands |
| Letter to a friend | "Dear X,"; informal contractions; personal anecdote; direct address; sign-off "Take care, [name]" |
| Website contribution | a title; a short standfirst; first person contributor voice; subheadings or bulleted asides; invitation to other readers at the end |

# 1. TALK — May 2025 Q8

**Question, verbatim:**
"You have been asked to give a talk to encourage your peers to visit new places. You should
include: what might be seen and experienced / what might be enjoyable / what challenges there
might be. Write the text of your talk."

**Model (395 words)**

> Good morning, everyone. Thank you for giving up your break to be here — I promise it is worth
> twenty minutes of your time.
>
> I want to talk about somewhere you have never been. Not a holiday advert, not a resort with a
> pool shaped like a kidney. Somewhere that will make you feel small.
>
> Here is what you actually see. The first thing is the light — in the mountains it changes every
> twenty minutes, and at six in the evening the whole valley goes the colour of weak tea. Then
> there is noise you did not know was missing. In the towns we live in, something is always humming.
> Stand on a ridge above a village and the loudest thing is your own breathing. Both of the passages
> you were given describe exactly this — one writer is stunned into silence by a view, and the other
> laughs at his own bad luck with the weather. Neither of them forgets it, and neither of them paid
> for the memory.
>
> What is enjoyable is harder to explain, so I will be specific. It is buying bread from a woman who
> does not speak your language and working out the price by pointing. It is a bus that leaves when
> it is full, not when the timetable says. It is the forty minutes when your phone has no signal and
> you discover you do not miss it.
>
> And now the honest part, because I am not selling you anything. It will go wrong. You will get
> lost, probably on the first day. The roads will be worse than you expect and the food will be
> strange and there will be one afternoon when you want to go home. That is not a flaw in the plan.
> That is the plan. The trip you remember is never the one that ran smoothly.
>
> So my advice is small. Go somewhere with one person you trust. Take half the luggage you are
> planning to take. Learn the word for "thank you" before you arrive, and use it badly, out loud,
> at strangers.
>
> You will come back with one good photograph and about nine stories. The photographs get lost.
> Thank you for listening.

**Marks: AO1 9 · AO4 9 · AO5 7 = 25/30**
- AO4 9: greeting, "I want to talk about", direct address throughout, rhetorical question,
  audience implication ("we live in"), closing thanks. The form is unmistakable from line one.
- AO1 9: all three bullets, each developed with several points, plus a reference to both texts.
- AO5 7: accurate, but deliberately conversational; a real AO5 9 would add more punctuation
  variety and two or three longer sentences.

# 2. ARTICLE — November 2024 Q8

**Question, verbatim:**
"Write an article for your school or college website with the title: 'Let's celebrate!' You should
include: why we should celebrate / events or occasions to celebrate / ways to celebrate."

**Model (398 words)**

> **Let's celebrate!**
> *Why the smallest excuse for a party might be the best thing your year has going for it.*
>
> By a student who has been to eleven leaving parties and enjoyed three of them.
>
> We are very good at marking the big things. Exam results, graduations, the day the last exam
> finishes. We are much worse at marking everything else, and everything else is most of a life.
>
> **Why we should celebrate**
>
> A celebration is not a reward for the excellent. It is a decision to stop for an evening and say
> this happened, and I was here for it. Psychologists call it marking; the rest of us call it a
> reason to be in the same room as the people we like. Both texts you read for this topic make the
> same point from different angles — one writer describes a festival as noise, colour and
> exhaustion, and the other describes a quiet family meal where nobody said anything remarkable and
> everybody remembered it. Neither is about achievements. Both are about attention.
>
> **What is worth celebrating**
>
> Not only the obvious. A good week. Somebody's first driving lesson. The end of a friendship that
> was not doing anyone any good. A grandparent's eightieth, a sibling's first payslip, a Tuesday in
> November when nothing went wrong. If you wait for the official occasions, you will celebrate about
> four times in your life and two of those will be somebody else's idea.
>
> **How to do it properly**
>
> Cheaply, and with food people actually like. A table is better than a restaurant because a
> restaurant has a leaving time. Invite fewer people than you think you should. Take one photograph
> early, while everyone still looks awake. Say the thing out loud — the toast nobody prepares is
> usually the one that gets repeated years later.
>
> The great advantage of celebrating small things is that you get to do it constantly. There is
> always a Tuesday. There is always somebody who has just got through something.
>
> So this is my very modest proposal: find something this week. It does not need a certificate. It
> needs a table and three people and about an hour.
>
> Then do it again next month.

**Marks: AO1 8 · AO4 10 · AO5 7 = 25/30**
- AO4 10: headline, standfirst, byline, subheadings, direct address "you", the voice of a
  named publication, a closing invitation. No one could mistake this for a letter or a talk.
- AO1 8: all three bullets, but the middle bullet runs slightly thin relative to the first.
- AO5 7: strong syntax, a deliberate short sentence pattern; 7 because there is little
  punctuation beyond full stops and dashes.

# 3. LETTER TO A FRIEND — January 2020 Q8

**Question, verbatim:**
"Write a letter to a friend encouraging him or her to take an interest in art. You should include:
the ways you can learn about art / different types of art / why art is important."

**Model (401 words)**

> Dear Hana,
>
> I am writing this on the bus, which tells you how keen I am about this, because I never write
> anything on the bus except messages telling people I am nearly there.
>
> You said last week that art was "not really your thing", and I have been thinking about it ever
> since, mostly because I think you have been sold the wrong version of it.
>
> First, you do not have to learn about art the way we learn about chemistry. Nobody needs you to
> memorise dates. The cheapest way in is to go and stand in front of something for eleven minutes
> and notice what your eyes do. Text One describes a person doing exactly that in a gallery —
> looking, then looking again, then finding they cannot leave. That is the whole method. If you
> want company, the museum in town is free on Fridays, and I will come with you and be wrong out
> loud about what things mean.
>
> Second, "art" is not one thing. It is a photograph that makes you uncomfortable, a building you
> walk past every day without looking up at, a portrait of somebody's grandmother painted four
> hundred years ago by someone who never met her. The other passage you were given makes the case
> for the homemade version — the drawings on a fridge, the mural on a wall — and honestly that
> might be where you start, because it is on your way home.
>
> Third, and this is the part I actually care about. Art is the only thing that lets you feel
> something from inside another person's head without any of the awkwardness of asking them. You
> spend forty minutes with somebody's anger, or their grief, or their very bad sense of humour, and
> you come out slightly less alone in the world. That is not a small thing. That is most of the
> reason anybody makes anything.
>
> You do not have to agree with me. Just come on Friday, for one hour, and if you hate it I will
> buy you a coffee and never mention it again.
>
> Take care, and say hello to your mum for me.
>
> Yours,
> Zain

**Marks: AO1 8 · AO4 10 · AO5 7 = 25/30**
- AO4 10: "Dear Hana", contractions, personal anecdote, direct address, specific shared context,
  sign-off with both a closing and a name. The register is unmistakably a friend, not a petition.
- AO1 8: all three bullets, and each is developed with more than one point.
- AO5 7: accurate, with varied sentence length; held at 7 by a couple of very conversational
  fragments ("Not a small thing.").

# 4. WEBSITE CONTRIBUTION — January 2022 Q8

**Question, verbatim:**
"A website for travellers is looking for contributions on the topic of places to stay. Write your
contribution. You should include: the different types of people who stay in hotels / the good
things about places to stay / the bad things about places to stay."

**Model (396 words)**

> **The Room Was Fine. Everything Else Was the Problem.**
> *A contribution from a traveller who has now slept in forty-one beds and regretted nine of them.*
>
> I have a theory about accommodation, and it is this: the room matters far less than the person
> who hands you the key.
>
> **Who stays where**
>
> Hotels are not one audience. There is the business traveller who wants a desk, a strong shower
> and nobody to speak to. There is the family who needs a fridge and a corridor wide enough to
> survive a pram. There are the students doing four to a room and the couple who have saved for a
> year and will notice everything. Both of the texts for this topic show the same division from
> opposite ends — one writer's hotel is a place of efficiency and quiet, and the other's is chaos
> with a breakfast buffet. Neither is wrong. They are simply different rooms.
>
> **What is good**
>
> Location, mostly. A cheap room above a bakery beats an expensive room in a business park, because
> a place to stay is really a place to leave from. The rest is small and unforgettable: a host who
> draws a map on the back of a receipt, a window that opens, water that is actually hot at seven in
> the morning.
>
> **What is bad**
>
> Photographs. Every photograph is taken at four in the afternoon in June. The other classic is the
> fee nobody mentions until you are signing — the tourist tax, the city tax, the card fee, the
> charge for the safe you did not use. And the walls: I have listened to an entire argument in a
> language I do not speak, at two in the morning, through eight inches of plaster.
>
> **My own rule**
>
> Read the one-star reviews first, and believe the boring ones. "Thin walls, no hot water" is
> information. "Worst hotel in the world, ruined my honeymoon" is a mood.
>
> I would rather pay less and leave more. The best place I have ever stayed cost eleven pounds a
> night and had no lock on the door, and I would go back tomorrow.
>
> — with thanks to the site for the space.

**Marks: AO1 9 · AO4 9 · AO5 8 = 26/30**
- AO4 9: title, standfirst, first-person contributor voice, subheadings, a closing address to the
  platform. Recognisably a contribution, not a letter.
- AO1 9: all three bullets, each with several developed points, plus both texts.
- AO5 8: accurate and varied, with a semicolon and a deliberate one-line paragraph.

# What actually differs between the four
Same three bullets. Same 400 words. The difference is nine or ten sentences of FORM:
the greeting, the headline, the "Dear X", the subheadings, the sign-off.
Those nine sentences are worth about **three to four AO4 marks**, which is why a student who writes
beautifully but ignores the form is capped in the mid-teens on a 30-mark question.

# The 30-second form drill, before you write
Say out loud: "This is a ___ to ___." Then write the first line that only that form could have.
If your first line would work in any of the four, you have not started yet.
