# Q9 — introductions, body paragraphs, conclusions, planning, and the format of each type

Built 2 Nov 2026 from primary sources only: the Q9 examiner commentary in
`Source_Notes/Q9_Examiner_Commentary_All_Series.md` (11 series, extracted from the PDFs),
the three verified scripts in `Verified_Candidate_Responses/`, and the 24 Q9 prompts on record.

---

## 1. The two questions are not the same question

Twelve of the 24 prompts use **"Discuss"**. Twelve use **"To what extent do you agree?"**

The examiner distinguishes them explicitly, and names the failure of each.

**"Discuss" fails when you never actually discuss.** June 2023, on *fresh air and exercise*:

> "Some did not discuss and just wrote about fresh air being good for you in as many ways as they
> could think of."

Four reasons in favour is not a discussion. It is a list.

**"Agree" fails when one reason is stretched.** January 2020:

> "Weaker candidates tended to be repetitive in their points, not developing them adequately, or
> developing a single point at length at the expense of alternative views."

So:

- **Argumentative ("To what extent…")** — you have a position and you push it. The engine is
  pressure. You win by making one case undeniable.
- **Discursive ("Discuss")** — you have a question and you work it. The engine is movement. You win
  by arriving somewhere you could not have predicted from the first line.

Note that both still engage the other side. June 2024 and June 2025 both name *"one sided or poorly
supported"* as a weakness of less successful answers. What differs is the **proportion**: an
argumentative answer can spend four paragraphs on its own case and two sentences on the other side.
A discursive answer has to give the other side real room before it decides.

## 2. The format of each

There is no required format. Twenty-three of the 24 prompts name no audience, no format and no
bullet points. The mark scheme says *"As no audience is specified, the examiner is assumed to be the
audience."*

But the reports describe a shape that scores, in three consecutive series (June 2023, June 2024,
June 2025):

> "These responses had a clear structure with a strong introduction and conclusion."

And in November 2020, November 2021 and June 2022:

> "offering strong arguments with some balance where they considered both sides of the discussion
> before completing with a strong conclusion."

### Argumentative — five blocks

| Block | Job | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Opening | Your position, in your own words, with the question's terms in it | 40–70 words |
| 2. Reason one | The one you'd defend in an argument | 90–110 words |
| 3. Reason two | A different kind of reason, not a second version of the first | 90–110 words |
| 4. The other side, answered | Give it its best case, then take it apart | 90–110 words |
| 5. Landing | Where you actually stand, narrowed | 20–40 words |

### Discursive — five blocks

| Block | Job | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Opening | Name the tension inside the question, not the topic | 40–70 words |
| 2. The case for | Its strongest form, with a real example | 90–110 words |
| 3. The case against | Its strongest form, with a different real example | 90–110 words |
| 4. What actually decides it | The distinction that settles the argument | 90–110 words |
| 5. Where it lands | The position the discussion has arrived at | 20–40 words |

**Block 4 is the whole difference.** In an argumentative answer it is the counter-argument you
answer. In a discursive answer it is the *thing you notice* that makes the two sides stop contradicting
each other. That notice is what the top band means by "perceptive".

## 3. Planning — three minutes, same method for both

Do not think about the topic. Run five steps.

**Step 1 — pull out the two key words.** From the prompt, not the topic.
*"People are entitled to freedom and choice."* → **freedom** and **choice**, and the word doing the
work is **entitled**.
*"New opportunities present new challenges."* → **opportunities** and **challenges**; **new** twice.
*"The best things in life are free."* → **best**, **free**.

**Step 2 — ask what the fight is actually about.** Every prompt hides a real disagreement, and it is
never the one on the surface.

- *Best things are free* → it isn't about money, it's about **time**. The walk is free; the hour to
  take it is not.
- *Freedom and choice* → it isn't about whether freedom is good, it's about **who pays when someone
  uses it**.
- *New opportunities* → it isn't about whether challenges exist, it's about **whether you are allowed
  to refuse**.
- *Art at school* → it isn't about art, it's about **what school is for**.

Finding this in minute two is what separates 16 from 19. It's also the thing you cannot get from a
template.

**Step 3 — four places, one line each.** Fill these in the margin:

1. At work — ______
2. At school or studying — ______
3. In a family or with friends — ______
4. The exception — who does this not work for? ______

Four lines, ninety seconds. Concrete: a person, an object, a moment. Not "society" or "people".

**Step 4 — sort them into the five blocks.** For argumentative, 1, 2 and 3 become reasons and 4
becomes the other side. For discursive, 1 and 2 become the two cases and 4 becomes block 4 —
the thing that decides it.

**Step 5 — write one sentence of the opening.** Just one. Then start.

The fourth place is the one people skip and the one examiners reward. November 2024, on the best
things in life:

> "Some more thoughtful responses considered that even things that are ostensibly free, such as
> family, friendship or education, require some degree of sacrifice and effort and so cannot be
> considered free."

That is the exception column, promoted into the argument.

## 4. Introductions

### What three sentences have to do

1. **Get the question into your own sentence.** Its key words, used — not quoted back at the examiner.
2. **Say something.** A claim, or a tension. Not "this is a broad and complex topic".
3. **Give a reason to keep reading.** One concrete thing: a scene, a number, a person.

That's it. Three sentences. You are not summarising, not defining terms, not announcing your
structure.

The examiner's own note on the 29/30 script (June 2022):

> "The opening sentence immediately grabs the reader's attention."

### Four openings that work — argumentative

**(a) The position, plainly.** For when you know what you think.
> *"People are entitled to freedom and choice."*
> Everyone says they believe this until it costs them something. The neighbour's dog. The cousin who
> drops out. The colleague who says the thing you find offensive. Freedom and choice are easy to
> defend in the abstract, which is why the statement is worth taking seriously — and why it still
> needs a limit.

**(b) The scene.** For when you have one moment that contains the whole question. The 29/30 used this
and scored 19/20.
> "It's the 18th century. You; a writer, sit down at your desk… But in reality, it's the 21st
> century."

**(c) The half-concession.** "Most people would say X. They are half right."
> Most people would agree with this statement without thinking about it. They are right about the
> principle and wrong about the price.

**(d) The narrow claim.** Take a big statement and shrink it to something you can defend.
> This is true for about half the people it's said about, and the half it isn't true for is the
> interesting one.

### Four openings that work — discursive

**(a) The tension in one breath.** Name what makes the question hard.
> *"New opportunities present new challenges."* Discuss.
> A new opportunity is usually described as a door opening. It is more accurate to say the door
> opens and the floor disappears. Nobody who has moved country or started a school where they knew
> nobody would call the experience simply good — and almost everyone would do it again.

**(b) The word that's doing the work.** Interrogate one word in the prompt.
> The word to notice here is *entitled*. Nobody is arguing about whether freedom is nice. They are
> arguing about who has to pay when somebody uses it.

**(c) The scene.** Same as argumentative, and it works just as well.

**(d) The honest position.** You don't have to pretend to certainty in a discussion.
> I don't think there is one answer to this. I do think there is a better answer than the one most
> people give, and it starts with the word "free".

### What gets punished in an opening, quoted

This is the one place examiners name specific failures, four series running.

November 2023, June 2024, November 2024, June 2025:

> "Some opened with a philosophical quotation or with their 'most crucial reason.'"

> "A few scripts used phrases that had been pre-taught and were familiar from previous series. These
> phrases were ineffective as they were pompous and overly formal and broke up the flow of the
> candidate's argument."

Named: *"I am going to be making strong and robust points on my stance"* · *"these points fall short
of the mark because…"* · *"as the dust settles, where do we find ourselves?"* · *"Clearly a topic
like this calls for deep thinking"* · *"It is important to tell people what side they are on"* ·
*"I am going to present some robust points"* · *"I will continue to convince you to switch to my
side"* · *"indubitably exigent"*.

Verdict: *"more engaged in remembering the scaffolding than they were in constructing a considered
argument."*

**Rule: no quotation, no announcement of intent, no definition of the topic as "complex" or
"broad".** Your first sentence should be a sentence you could only have written about this question.

### The one that scored 16 and why it stopped there

June 2024, 24/30:

> *"I much prefer working in a group than on my own."* I think most people would agree with this
> statement. But I think there are positives and negatives of both working in a group, or alone.

Everything is correct. Nothing is at stake. "There are positives and negatives of both" tells the
examiner you will not be arguing, and the examiner wrote back *"effective, without being
sophisticated."* Same opening, one word changed:

> …But I think most people are wrong about *why* groups work, and it is not the reason they give.

That is the whole upgrade: from announcing a survey to making a claim.

## 5. Body paragraphs

### Four things in every paragraph

1. **A claim that names the question's subject.** So the examiner can see you're still answering.
2. **Something you can see.** A person, a place, a moment. Photographable.
3. **A short sentence.** Six words or fewer. This is the landing.
4. **What it adds up to.** The turn — cost into gain, or assumption into reversal.

Nine in ten weak paragraphs have 1 and 3 and nothing else. Or 1 and 2, and no landing.

### A real one, from the 26/30 script (June 2025)

> "…getting a scholarship to a world renowned school is just as bad. I've seen toppers go to
> universities like Rochester or Harvard and barely manage to keep up there. **The pressure is
> unlike any other.** You even have to keep up your social life whilst also doing all of the chores
> at home yourself. **There's barely any time left to study.** But, of course, being able to
> overcome this leaves you with an impeccable ability of discipline and time management."

- Sentence 1 — the claim, and it answers the question in its first clause (*"just as bad"* = as bad
  as a challenge).
- Sentence 2 — something seen. Named universities. A real observation, not "many students struggle".
- Sentences 3 and 5 — the short ones. Five words and seven words.
- Sentence 4 — the physical detail, in "you". Chores, social life, in the same hour.
- Sentence 6 — the turn. Cost becomes gain, and it contains the word *challenge*'s cousin,
  *discipline*.

### A paragraph that fails, and why

June 2023, 17/30:

> "Being cooped up indoors can increase stress levels."

A claim with nothing in it. You cannot picture it. There is no person, no room, no hour. And it is
followed by another claim of the same kind, so the paragraph is four assertions stacked, going
nowhere.

January 2020 names the other failure:

> "developing a single point at length at the expense of alternative views."

One paragraph stretched to 250 words. The examiner reads it as thin, not thorough.

### Length and number

**70–110 words per paragraph. Four to six paragraphs, plus the opening and the ending.**
The 29/30 is 436 words in five blocks. The 24/30 is 641 words in ten short ones. The 17/30 is 293
words in six blocks of equal length, and 293 words is the real reason it scored 17.

## 6. Conclusions

The reports ask for a *strong* conclusion, not a long one. June 2023, June 2024 and June 2025:
*"a clear structure with a strong introduction and conclusion."* November 2023 and November 2024:
*"considering counter arguments and making a balanced conclusion."*

**One to three sentences.** Say where you landed. Do not summarise, do not introduce anything new,
do not write "In conclusion".

### Three that scored

**A question.** June 2022, 29/30 — the answer is 436 words and ends:
> "Who knows how much time we have left?"

**A qualification.** June 2024, 24/30:
> "I disagree to a point, as working in a group at times can be unsustainable for your emotional
> wellbeing."

**A concession then a verdict.** June 2025, 26/30:
> "There may still be some who oppose this view, but opposing arguments fall flat when up against
> points like the challenge of parenting."

### The rule

The last sentence must contain **the question's subject** and **your position**, in a form only you
could have written. Everything else is optional.

### Written for you, on real prompts

*Freedom and choice:*
> Freedom is worth defending. What is not worth defending is the pretence that it costs nothing.

*New opportunities:*
> So the statement is true, but not in the way it is usually meant. The challenge is not the price of
> the opportunity — it is the opportunity.

*The best things in life are free:*
> Nobody remembers the receipt. They remember who was at the table.

## 7. Two full worked plans

### A. Argumentative — *"People are entitled to freedom and choice." To what extent do you agree?*

**Key words:** freedom, choice, **entitled**. Fight: who pays when someone uses their freedom.

**The plan (four places):**
1. Work — the colleague who is "just being honest".
2. School — the phone ban, and every student's list of reasons it's unfair.
3. Family — the relative whose choices you have to live with.
4. The exception — the person whose choice removes someone else's.

**Blocks:**
1. Opening — the position (opening (a) above).
2. Reason one — the phone ban paragraph. *"Those reasons are real. None of them is the point."*
3. Reason two — the colleague. Freedom of speech is not freedom from consequence; the cost lands on
   whoever is in the room.
4. The other side — the strongest version: freedom that is only exercised where it's convenient isn't
   freedom. Answer: nobody is arguing for convenience, only for cost. Then: the choice is real, the
   bill is real, and pretending otherwise loses both.
5. Landing — *"Freedom is worth defending. What is not worth defending is the pretence that it costs
   nothing."*

**Why this earns Level 5 and not Level 4:** the "exception" column turns the whole piece from
"freedom is good but has limits" (predictable — named as a weakness in January 2022) into a claim
about *who bears the cost*, which is a thing the examiner hasn't read forty times that morning.

### B. Discursive — *"New opportunities present new challenges."* Discuss

**Key words:** opportunities, challenges, **new**. Fight: whether you're allowed to refuse.

**The plan:**
1. Work — a promotion that removes the thing you were good at.
2. Study — the scholarship and the isolation.
3. Family — moving country; a cousin with two suitcases.
4. The exception — the person who would have been better off saying no.

**Blocks:**
1. Opening — the tension: *"the door opens and the floor disappears."*
2. The case for — the cousin in Toronto. Real detail: the bus, the landlord, the eleven-day email.
   *"None of that was on the brochure. All of it was the opportunity."*
3. The case against — the person who should have refused. A promotion that made someone worse at their
   job. The challenge wasn't growth; it was loss with a new title.
4. **What decides it** — the distinction: a challenge you *chose* teaches you something; a challenge
   imposed on you mostly teaches you that it was imposed. So the statement is true for the
   opportunities you actually chose, and false for the ones you were pushed through.
5. Landing — *"The challenge is not the price of the opportunity. It is the opportunity."*

**Why block 4 matters:** without it you have written "opportunities are challenging but also good",
which is what *"almost without exception candidates argued"* (June 2025) — i.e. the whole cohort.
With it, you have made a distinction. That is the difference between "communicates successfully" and
"perceptive and subtle".

## 8. Signposting: what's fine and what's punished

Worth knowing, because the two look similar and only one is penalised.

**Fine, and credited.** January 2023:

> "These candidates had often been well prepared for this type of task and included sentence starters
> such as: 'On the one hand…', 'Whilst', 'Conversely', 'For example', suggesting that they had a good
> understanding of what they were being asked to do."

**Punished.** The theatrical ones — the eight phrases listed in section 4.

The difference is whether the phrase is doing grammatical work or performing preparation.
"Conversely" links two ideas. "As the dust settles, where do we find ourselves?" announces that a
human has been trained.

## 9. The one-page version

**Plan, 3 min.** Two key words → what the fight is really about → four places (work, school, family,
the exception) → five blocks → one opening sentence.

**Opening, 3 sentences.** Question's words in a sentence of yours · a claim or a tension · one
concrete thing. No quotation, no announcement, no "broad and complex".

**Paragraphs, 70–110 words each.** Claim with the question in it · something you can see · a sentence
of six words or fewer · what it adds up to.

**Middle.** Argumentative: two reasons, then the other side answered. Discursive: one side, the other
side, then the distinction that decides it.

**Ending, 1–3 sentences.** Question's subject + your position, in your words only. No "In conclusion".

---

## 10. Practice, in order

1. Take *"We only regret the chances we do not take."* Write only the opening, three sentences.
   Check: are both key terms in it, in your own sentence?
2. Take the same prompt. Write only block 4 — the other side, given its best case, then answered.
3. Take *"Discuss whether travel broadens the mind."* Write only block 4 of that one — the
   distinction that decides it.
4. Then a full answer, timed.
