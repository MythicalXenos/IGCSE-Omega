# Section B — the text rule, and every form explained

3 Nov 2026. All quotations below are from the 4EB1 Paper 1 examiner reports (pef PDFs) and the
mark schemes, not from secondary sources.

## PART 1 — Do you mention the texts? NO.

The question paper says "Use ideas from BOTH Text One and Text Two". It does not say "refer to",
"mention" or "quotes from". And the examiner reports are unusually blunt about what happens when
candidates do more than use the ideas:

**Nov 2024 report:** "All examiners commented that there was **quite a lot of evidence of direct
lifting from the texts which limited achievement**."

**Jan 2023 report:** "Examiners commented that there were some candidates who **lifted information
from the texts or who used very close re-wording.**"

**Jan 2023 report, on the unsuccessful:** "A few unsuccessful responses **simply copied out the
texts.**"

**Nov 2024 report, on the most successful:** "They were able to **successfully re-work the texts,
bringing in their own interpretations** to enhance their response."

**Jan 2023 report, on the most successful:** "taking ideas from the texts and **extending and
personalising these**."

So the rule is:

| Do | Do not |
|---|---|
| Take facts, examples, statistics and images from both texts | Quote them |
| Re-word them into your own voice | Re-word them closely ("very close re-wording" is named as a fault) |
| Add your own interpretation, experience or opinion | Say "as Text One says" or "the writer of Text Two describes" |
| Cover all three bullets with points drawn from both | Copy whole sentences |
| — | Write anything that reads like a summary of a text |

**Why explicit mention is unnecessary:** the AO1 grid (10 marks) says nothing about texts. Its
Level 5 bullet is "Selection and interpretation of **the given bullet points** is apt and is
persuasive in clarifying the points being made." The texts are the *raw material* for the bullets,
not a thing to cite.

**Why it actively costs you:** a sentence about the text is a sentence not doing the job, and it
breaks the form. No real article says "as I read in a text I was given". The moment you write that,
AO4 drops, because the audience you invented would never read it.

**The one legitimate trace:** if a text contains a statistic, an anecdote or an image you can use,
use the *substance* as though it were your own knowledge. "Roughly six hours a day" is fine.
"Text One says that teenagers spend six hours a day on their phones" is not.

## PART 2 — The five forms, and the differences that matter

Every Section B prompt in the archive (13 of them) asks for one of five forms. Here is what each
requires, and where the confusions you asked about actually are.

### A. TALK / SPEECH
Papers that have used it: 2020, Jun 2021, Nov 2021, Jun 2023, May 2025.
Report language: "the appropriate register for a **talk to peers**".

Features that earn AO4:
- An opening that greets the audience out loud: "Good morning, everyone."
- "I" as a speaker and "you" as the room.
- Signposts that are spoken, not written: "First", "Now the honest part", "My last point".
- At least one rhetorical question addressed to the room.
- A close that thanks them or asks them to do something.

Why it is the easiest form: the register is the same as the letter to a friend, so one voice does
both.

### B. ARTICLE for a SCHOOL or COLLEGE WEBSITE
Papers: Nov 2024 ("Let's celebrate!"), Jun 2026 (handwriting).
Report language: "appropriate format and style for an **article for a school or college website**".

Features:
- A headline (often the given title).
- A standfirst — one italic line under the headline that sells it.
- Subheadings dividing the three bullets.
- "we" for the school, "you" for the reader.
- A close that lands.

### C. ARTICLE for a MAGAZINE aimed at young adults
Papers: Nov 2023 ("Time to learn something new?").
Report language: "appropriate register for an **article for young adults**".

**How it differs from the school-website article** — this is the distinction you asked for, and it
is real:
| | School/college website | Magazine for young adults |
|---|---|---|
| Who is speaking | a member of the community ("we at this school") | a columnist, an individual voice |
| Relationship to reader | fellow student, equal | writer to reader, mildly authoritative |
| Tone | friendly, encouraging, institutional | wittier, more opinionated, first person singular |
| Structure | subheadings tied to the bullets | subheadings, punchier; a pull-quote style line |
| Ending | an invitation to act | a line with a twist in it |
| Risk to avoid | sounding like a teacher | sounding like an advert |

Both are articles. The difference is **whose voice it is** and **how much personality is allowed**.
A magazine piece may open with an opinion; a school website piece usually opens with a shared
situation.

### D. WEBSITE CONTRIBUTION
Papers: Jan 2022 (places to stay), Jun 2022 (clear written communication).
Report language: "contribution".

This is the one candidates most often turn into an article, and it is close — but:
- The prompt says a website "is looking for contributions". You are **one voice among many
  contributors**, not the publication.
- So: a title, a contributor standfirst ("a contribution from someone who…"), first-person voice,
  subheadings or asides, and a closing line addressed **to the site or to other readers**
  ("thanks for the space", "I read the comments here more than I should").
- It is the only form where addressing the *platform* is appropriate.

### E. LETTER
There are two kinds in the archive, and they are not the same.

**E1. Letter to a FRIEND** — Jan 2020 (art), Jun 2024 (climate), Nov 2025 (manners).
Report language: "the appropriate register for a **letter to a friend**".
Features: "Dear [name],", contractions everywhere, a shared reference ("you said last week"),
personal anecdote, direct advice to them, "Take care," plus your name.

**E2. Letter to a NEWSPAPER** — Jun 2019 (after-study activities).
Report language: letters were discussed generally; the form is a formal letter for publication.
**How a formal letter differs from an informal one:**

| | Letter to a friend | Letter to a newspaper |
|---|---|---|
| Salutation | "Dear Hana," | "Dear Editor," |
| Opening | personal news, a shared memory | the issue, stated at once |
| Pronouns | "you" and "I" | "we", "our community", "readers" |
| Register | contractions, slang, jokes | no contractions, measured, sometimes indignant |
| Evidence | anecdotes about the two of you | local facts, numbers, a named place |
| Purpose | to persuade a friend | to argue a case in public and ask for change |
| Sign-off | "Take care, Zain" | "Yours faithfully, Zain Rahman" (+ address line if asked) |
| Length of sentences | short, varied | longer, controlled, one rhetorical crescendo |
| Risk | sounding like an essay | sounding like a chat |

**The single test:** would you say this sentence out loud to the person you are writing to? If yes
for a friend and no for an editor, you have the register right.

### FORMAL vs INFORMAL SPEECH — you asked, and the answer is: it does not arise
All five talk questions in the archive are "a talk **to your peers**". There is no formal-address
talk on record. So do not prepare one. If a future paper asks for a talk to parents or a speech at
an assembly, the changes are: no contractions, "Good evening, parents and teachers", no slang, and
the rhetorical questions become inclusive ("how many of us…") rather than matey ("ever noticed…").

## PART 3 — What the reports say earns marks, in their own words

**AO4 (10 marks)** — repeated almost identically in every report since 2020:
> "The most successful responses had a strong sense of audience and purpose and included
> **personal touches, humour and rhetorical questions** to engage the audience."
> "More successful candidates were able to create a **lively and engaging style** that suggested
> they had a well-developed understanding of the required approach."

**AO1 (10 marks)**
> "More successful candidates used a wide range of appropriate points of information from **both
> texts, supported with perceptive comments.** They covered all the bullet points in detail…
> They were able to successfully **re-work the texts, bringing in their own interpretations**."
> "A few used their own ideas very successfully."

**AO5 (10 marks)**
> "More successful candidates used a varied range of correctly spelt vocabulary and a range of
> appropriate punctuation. They used a range of different sentence structures to help them create
> particular effects. These responses employed accurate paragraphing. There was often **evidence of
> proofreading.**"
> Common errors: "missing basic sentence punctuation; the use of very long, unstructured sentences;
> comma splicing; missing or misused apostrophes."

**One planning note, identical in every report:**
> "The most useful plans were relatively short but allowed candidates to focus and organise their
> ideas effectively. **Plans should be in the answer booklet** rather than on an additional sheet."

## PART 4 — The one-page procedure for Section B

1. **Name the form in one sentence.** "This is an article for a school website aimed at students."
2. **Write the first line only that form could have.**
3. **Three body paragraphs, one per bullet.** In each: a point from Text One, a point from Text
   Two, then your own comment or example. That proves AO1 range and covers the bullet.
4. **Keep the voice.** "you" and "we" (or "I" in a letter/contribution) in every paragraph.
5. **Personal touches, humour, one rhetorical question.**
6. **Last line returns to the audience and the purpose.**
7. **Four minutes: read backwards for the nine accuracy items.**

## The five-bullet summary you can memorise
- Talk: greet them, tell them, thank them.
- School article: headline, standfirst, subheadings, "we".
- Magazine article: headline, standfirst, opinion first, a twist at the end.
- Contribution: title, who I am, subheadings, thanks to the site.
- Letter to a friend: Dear X, contractions, "you said last week", Take care.
- Letter to a newspaper: Dear Editor, no contractions, our community, Yours faithfully.
