# Section A — how to spot register, and what to say about the four big features
3 Nov 2026. For Q3, Q6, Q7.

## 1. REGISTER — four tests, in this order

| Test | Formal | Semi-formal | Informal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contractions | none ("do not") | some | many ("don't", "you've") |
| Pronouns | "one", "people", passive | "we" | "I" and "you" |
| Vocabulary | "innate", "commemorated", "cathartic" | mixed | "don't force it!", "imagine eleven" |
| Sentences | long, subordinate clauses, semicolons | varied | short, dashes, exclamations, fragments |

Direct address and rhetorical questions pull a text towards informal even when the vocabulary is
formal. Headings and subheadings signal semi-formal web writing.

### The sentence to write
"The writer's register is ______, shown by ______. This makes the reader feel ______, which
suits her purpose of ______."

### Effects, by register
- INFORMAL: closeness. Reader feels advised by a friend rather than lectured. Lowers defences, so
  advice is easier to accept. Suits a blog.
- FORMAL: authority and distance. Reader feels the piece has been researched and edited. Suits an
  article for a serious publication.
- SEMI-FORMAL: expert but approachable. Reader feels informed, not instructed.

Worked, on Nov 2024 Text Two:
"The register is informal, shown by contractions and direct address — 'don't force it!', 'Try
something new that scares you a little'. This makes the reader feel personally advised rather than
lectured, which suits a blog: the writer needs the reader to actually try a resolution, not just
agree with one."

## 2. SUBHEADINGS — what to say
Three things, and you should use two:
1. It breaks a long piece into units the reader can scan before committing to reading.
2. It tells the reader what each section is for, so nothing is discovered late.
3. It makes each point feel separate and complete, which suits advice: the reader can act on one
   without reading the rest.

Contrast sentence for Q7, when only one text has them:
"Text Two uses subheadings, which let the reader scan and select; Text One runs continuously, so the
reader must follow her order — which suits a persuasive argument, because her sequence is the
argument."

## 3. STRUCTURED PARAGRAPHS AND SEQUENCING — what to say
Look for three things:
- ONE idea per paragraph (topic sentence first, or last).
- The ORDER: general to specific, problem to solution, past to present, personal to expert.
- DISCOURSE MARKERS: "But", "So then", "For instance", "Which leads me to the next point",
  "Let's not forget".

Effects to name:
- "It moves the reader from X to Y, so by the time she reaches Z the reader has already accepted
  the premise."
- "Each paragraph answers the question the previous one raised, which makes the piece feel
  inevitable rather than argued."
- "The short final paragraph acts as a conclusion, so the piece ends on a judgement rather than
  trailing off."

Worked, on Nov 2024 Text One:
"Her paragraphs move from history to psychology to chemistry to gratitude — four kinds of evidence
in a row. That order matters: once the reader has accepted that celebration is ancient, the claim
that it is 'innate' is much easier to accept."

## 4. QUOTATIONS AND EXPERTS — what to say
Three levels, from weakest to strongest:
1. "She uses expert opinion." (Level 2 — a label)
2. "She quotes a named expert with a job title and a clinic — 'psychotherapist Dr Sheri Jacobson of
   Harley Therapy'. Naming the qualification makes the claim sound tested rather than felt."
   (Level 3/4 — label + evidence + effect)
3. "She quotes four different specialists in a row — a psychotherapist, a mindfulness psychologist
   and a consumer psychologist — so no single viewpoint carries the argument. The reader is left
   feeling the conclusion is shared by a profession, not invented by one writer." (Level 4/5)

Also in this family: dates, statistics, historical detail ("since 2000 BC", "3,000 BCE"), and
direct speech in quotation marks. Direct speech matters because it reads as testimony rather than
summary — the reader hears the expert rather than the writer's account of them.

## 5. THE RULE THAT TIES IT TOGETHER
Feature + evidence + effect + so what. Never stop at the feature.
"The writer uses expert opinion" earns nothing on its own. "The writer uses a named psychotherapist
with a clinic, which makes the claim sound tested rather than felt, so the reader accepts the
premise before the argument begins" earns the mark.
