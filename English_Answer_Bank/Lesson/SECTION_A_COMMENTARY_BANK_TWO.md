# Section A — the rest of the commentary bank
3 Nov 2026. Companion to SECTION_A_HOW_TO_COMMENT.md. Every example is from the Nov 2024 texts
(Text One: Keegan, celebrations. Text Two: Sweeney, New Year).

## THE RULE
FEATURE -> EVIDENCE -> EFFECT -> SO WHAT.
The effect is what happens to the reader. The "so what" is why the writer wanted that.

## THE SO-WHAT LADDER — climb to rung 3
Rung 1 (feelings): "This makes the reader feel included."
Rung 2 (beliefs): "This makes the reader feel included, so they accept that celebration is normal."
Rung 3 (beliefs + action): "…so they accept that celebration is normal and are therefore more willing
to act on her advice."
Rung 3 is where Levels 4 and 5 live. Two or three per answer is enough.

## BANNED EFFECTS — these earn nothing
"engages the reader" · "draws the reader in" · "makes the reader want to read on" · "catches the
reader's attention" · "creates a picture in the reader's mind" · "makes it interesting"
They are true of every text ever written, so they say nothing about THIS text.

## 1. DIRECT ADDRESS
Spot: second person — "you", "you've", "your".
Say: "She addresses the reader directly as 'you' — 'try something new that scares you a little' —
which turns general advice into an instruction aimed at one person."
Effect: the reader cannot stay a spectator; the sentence is now about them.
So what: advice aimed at you is harder to ignore than advice aimed at people in general.

## 2. RHETORICAL QUESTIONS
Definition: a question asked to make a point, not to get an answer.
Say: "She asks 'where does our urge to rejoice collectively actually come from?' and answers it in
the next paragraph. It makes the reader feel this was a question they already wanted answered, and
it carries them into the next section."
Warning: one in every paragraph feels mechanical. Two or three in a text is normal.

## 3. FIRST-PERSON ANECDOTE
Anecdote = a short personal story. First person = "I".
Say: "She admits 'Last year, I made a resolution to eat out less, and it wasn't until then that I
realized how much I genuinely love cooking.' This is the only place the writer is a person rather
than a voice, which makes her advice sound tested rather than researched."
So what: the reader trusts someone who has failed at the thing they are advising.
This is the contrast Text One does NOT have — her authority comes from experts, never herself.
That is the best Q7 sentence available in this paper.

## 4. EXPERT QUOTATION — the breadth version
Say: "Three named specialists appear in a row — a psychotherapist, a mindfulness psychologist and a
consumer psychologist — so the conclusion feels shared by a profession rather than invented by one
writer."
A job title and a clinic ("of Harley Therapy") make a claim sound tested.
Effect: the reader stops asking "is this true?" and starts asking "how do I do it?"

## 5. DATES, STATISTICS, HISTORICAL RANGE
Spot: "3,000 BCE", "4,000-year-old", "since 2000 BC", "11-day festival".
Say: "She anchors the claim in a date — 'around 3,000 BCE' — which moves celebration out of opinion
and into history: the reader is no longer being persuaded, they are being informed."
Breadth of range (Egypt, the Bible, Rome, the 12th century) makes a practice feel permanent, which
makes it feel natural, which makes it feel right.

## 6. LISTING / RULE OF THREE
Rule of three = three items in a row, which sounds complete.
Say: "She lists 'birthdays, weddings, anniversaries and national holidays', which makes celebration
feel constant rather than occasional — the reader recognises their own calendar in it."
Text Two's "'wake-up, work, eat, sleep, repeat'" is dull on purpose: the rhythm enacts the routine.
And it is five items, not three, with one repeated.

## 7. SHORT SENTENCE AND FRAGMENT
Fragment = words with no main verb, written as a sentence for effect.
Say: "After a long account of Roman birthday invitations she writes 'In short: please come to my
party.' It is a fraction of the length of the sentence before, so the joke lands and her own voice
breaks through."
Reusable line: "The short sentence after a long one stands out because length is the only thing in
prose the eye can actually see."

## 8. DASHES AND ASIDES
An aside = a remark to the reader, half outside the sentence.
Say: "She interrupts herself with dashes — 'while we're far more networked these days… we still need
other people' — which gives the impression of a thinking voice rather than a finished essay."
Effect: the reader feels spoken to, not written at.

## 9. REPETITION AT THE START
Repeating the same opening words across sentences. Text Two does it five times: "New Beginnings…"
Say: "Five headings begin with the same two words, so the piece feels like a list of reasons being
ticked off — the reader can feel the argument accumulating."
Do not write "anaphora" in the exam. Describe it.

## 10. METAPHOR
Metaphor = describing one thing as if it were another.
Spot: "a mental reset", "darker thoughts", "antidotes to low mood", "Unpacking a little emotional
baggage is a great way to travel forward", "the midnight threshold".
Say: "She calls the new year 'a mental reset' and a bad habit 'emotional baggage' you can 'unpack'.
Both make change sound like clearing up — familiar and manageable rather than frightening."
Method for any metaphor: name the two things compared, then say what the comparison makes the reader
feel is possible, easy, or inevitable.

## 11. HUMOUR AND IRONY
Irony = saying something your tone shows you do not quite mean.
Spot: "so if you're struggling with one night of celebrating, imagine eleven."
Say: "She undercuts her own history with a joke, which stops the piece becoming a lecture."
Effect: the reader relaxes; someone who makes you laugh is harder to argue with.

## 12. MODAL VERBS
Modal verb = can, could, should, must, may, might.
Text Two repeats "can": "New Beginnings CAN Launch Long-Term Action", "the new year CAN bring you
happiness". Text One's question "how do we approach it mindfully?" implies duty.
Say: "She repeats 'can' rather than 'must', which offers possibility instead of obligation — her
advice never accuses anyone of having failed."
Text One argues celebration is "innate" — a fact about humans, not a choice.
One writer offers options; the other explains a need. That is a difference in perspective.

## 13. INCLUSIVE "WE"
Say: "'We' places the writer inside the group she describes, so she is thinking aloud with the reader
rather than advising from above."
Effect: the reader feels part of a majority, and majorities are hard to disagree with.
When a text switches from "we" to "you", the switch is the interesting moment. Point at it.

## 14. STRUCTURE — two sentences to have ready
Order: "Her paragraphs move from history to psychology to chemistry to gratitude — four kinds of
evidence in a row — so by the time she claims celebration is 'innate', the reader has already been
given the evidence for it."
Ending: "The piece ends on a judgement rather than a summary — 'enjoying how far you've come is
about as uplifting as it gets' — which leaves the reader with a feeling rather than a fact."

## 15. THE LEVEL 4/5 SENTENCE
One per answer is enough. Shape: Text One wants X; Text Two wants Y; therefore they are not really
about the same thing.
"The two writers are not both recommending celebration. Text One argues celebrating is a need —
'it's innate', part of 'the human experience' — so her reader is reassured. Text Two argues the new
year is an opportunity, with goals that are 'measurable and attainable', so her reader is instructed.
One wants the reader to feel that celebrating is what we already are; the other wants the reader to
do something with it. Given they are on the same subject, that is the most interesting difference
here."
