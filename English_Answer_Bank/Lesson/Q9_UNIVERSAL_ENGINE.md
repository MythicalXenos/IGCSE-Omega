# Q9 — the universal engine. Works on any prompt, any series.

Built 2 Nov 2026, 23:5x, from: 24 Q9 prompts on record, 11 examiner reports, 23 mark-scheme
indicative-content lists, 5 marked scripts (17, 17, 18, 21, 24, 26, 28, 29).

---

## 1. The finding this rests on

Every Q9 prompt on record is one of three machines. The machine decides the essay. A candidate
who can identify the machine in thirty seconds can answer any prompt the exam board invents,
including one nobody has seen.

| Machine | Count on record | Examples | What the essay must do |
|---|---|---|---|
| SWEEP | 15 of 24 | only · never · always · everyone · too much · best · keys to · have to | Find the boundary. Say how far it holds and where it breaks. |
| COMPARISON | 2 of 24 | "more interested in X than Y" · "I much prefer X than Y" | Attack the measurement. Show the comparison is weighing the wrong thing. |
| CONDITION | 1 of 24 | "as long as it doesn't harm others" | The condition IS the answer. Define the harm, then test the claim against it. |
| PLAIN CLAIM | 6 of 24 | "New opportunities present new challenges." · "People need each other." | Supply the missing word. The claim is vague; make it precise and then test it. |

SWEEP is the default and covers two thirds of the record. Learn that machine first.

### How to run the SWEEP machine
1. Circle the sweep word. It is the word that overreaches.
2. Ask: what would have to be true for this to be false in EVERY case? Usually something
   unglamorous (money, time, health, other people's decisions).
3. Find the place where the sweep word is too big. That place is the essay.
4. State the boundary in the second paragraph. Everything after it obeys the boundary.

### How to run the COMPARISON machine
1. Circle both nouns. Ask what is being measured and on what scale.
2. Show the two nouns are not the same kind of thing, so "more" cannot be applied.
   Celebrities are packaged; family are not. Groups are chosen; solitude is chosen differently.
3. Then concede the grain of truth in the comparison, and land on the mismeasurement.

### How to run the CONDITION machine
1. The subordinate clause ("as long as...") is the whole question.
2. Define the harm concretely, with one case.
3. Then test: does the claim survive your definition? Usually yes for small harms, no for large.
4. The essay is that test.

### How to run the PLAIN CLAIM machine
1. The claim is vague by design. Choose the precise version you will argue.
2. "New opportunities present new challenges" -> which challenges, for whom, and are they always
   a cost or sometimes the point?
3. Then run it as a SWEEP against your own precision.

## 2. The five-step desk procedure (three minutes, every prompt)

1. **Identify the machine** (above). Thirty seconds.
2. **Fix the proportion.** Decide, now: all / most / about half / only for some. Write it down as a
   single clause. Every paragraph obeys it. This is the single most common failure in the archive:
   the essay argues the claim for two paragraphs, then reverses, then reverses back.
3. **Fill four places** with one concrete line each: work · school or study · family or friends ·
   the exception (who does this not work for). The exception becomes the concession.
4. **Fix the turn.** Exactly one reversal, in paragraph 4, out and back inside the same paragraph.
   Not five reasons and no turn (the 17/30 script). Not two reversals (draft 3).
5. **Write the opening sentence last, or badly first.** Its only job is to make sentence two easy.

## 3. The universal paragraph rule

Every paragraph needs four things, and they are the same four regardless of prompt:
- a claim that contains the prompt's key words
- something a reader could photograph
- one sentence of six words or fewer
- a final clause that says what the paragraph adds up to

Named failures, all evidenced:
- claim with nothing to picture ("Being cooped up indoors can increase stress levels" - 17/30)
- single point stretched long (Jan 2020 report)
- five reasons, no turn (friend's 17/30)
- the same idea five ways ("fronts / crafted / tailored / artificially crafted" - draft 4)

## 4. The universal conclusion rule

Two jobs: name the proportion, then say what follows from it. One to three sentences.
Never: "In conclusion", "Overall", a summary of the paragraphs, a second reversal.
Evidence: the 29/30 ends on a question; the 24/30 on one qualified sentence; the 26/30 on a
concession plus verdict. None summarises.

## 5. The universal accuracy list (nine items, three minutes, read backwards)

Every report since 2020 lists the same faults. These nine catch almost all of them:
1. Sentence punctuation present (no run-ons).
2. No comma splices (comma + independent clause).
3. Apostrophes: its/it's, plural s vs possessive 's, celebrities vs celebrities'.
4. Homophones: their/there/they're, your/you're, lose/loose, of/have.
5. Capital I; capitals at sentence starts; NO random capitals mid-sentence.
6. Subject-verb agreement (people... leave -> leaves).
7. One tense per paragraph.
8. Missing words; missing a/an/the; "neither... nor", "between... and".
9. Paragraphs: 5-7, each 70-110 words.

## 6. The universal bans (named by examiners, four series running)

Scaffolding phrases: "indubitably exigent" · "as the dust settles, where do we find ourselves?" ·
"I am going to be making strong and robust points on my stance" · "these points fall short of the
mark because..." · "Once we have embraced a stand point we must look at the whole picture" ·
"I will continue to convince you to switch to my side" · "I do not pretend to know everything" ·
"It is important to tell people what side they are on" · "Clearly a topic like this calls for deep
thinking" · "I am going to present some robust points".
Also penalised: philosophical quotations as openers · invented statistics · lifting Section A
texts · narrative answers · over-ambitious vocabulary ("artificialness", "regretful" for outcomes,
"opportunity cost" used as if it distinguished two options) · lack of punctuation variety.

## 7. The honest arithmetic for a grade 8/9

Grade boundaries (raw/100): Jun 2025 69/64/59/56/53/50/37/25. Grade 9 = 69, grade 8 = 64.
Section marks: A 30, B 30, C 30.
If Section C improves from 18 to 22 (+4) and nothing else changes, a 55 becomes a 59 — grade 5.
A grade 9 requires Section A near 25-26, Section B near 24, Section C near 22-24. Section C is one
third of the paper; it cannot carry a 52-56 to a 69 on its own. Any plan that says otherwise is
not honest.
Therefore the engine above is worth learning because it is transferable to Q9 every year, not
because it alone produces a 9.
