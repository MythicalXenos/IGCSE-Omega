# Section B - what is actually true about form, plus exact lengths, plus the running order
3 Nov 2026. Source of authority: Source_Notes/Official_Pearson/Writing_Tasks_Guidance_2024_extract.md

## 1. THE FOUR CLAIMS HEARD IN THE CAR - checked against Pearson

CLAIM: "Article and website contribution are the same."
FALSE, but they are close. Both are continuous prose with a headline, an opening that says what the
piece is about, and sequenced paragraphs. The difference is WHO IS SPEAKING.
  - An ARTICLE is written by a writer FOR readers. It reports and it comments. Stance: "here is the
    situation, and here is what I think of it."
  - A CONTRIBUTION is written by a member of a community TO that community. Stance: "I am one of you,
    and here is my view." Pearson's contribution kit is: headline/title, strapline, sub-headings if
    appropriate, an introductory overview paragraph "containing the whole article in a nutshell",
    then sequenced paragraphs.
  Same furniture. Different voice. Examiners mark the voice (audience/purpose), not the furniture.

CLAIM: "I can/should put bylines on both."
NO. Pearson lists a by-line under NOT REQUIRED for the contribution. It is not forbidden anywhere,
but it earns nothing and costs time. Do not bother.

CLAIM: "No byline on magazine."
Also no requirement anywhere. There is no magazine-specific rule about by-lines. Same answer: skip.

CLAIM: "No subheadings in magazines; subheadings in website contribution and article."
UNSUPPORTED. Pearson says an article "may use bullet points and sub-headings", and a contribution may
use "sub-headings if appropriate". Nothing forbids sub-headings in a magazine article. Use them where
they help the reader; leave them out where they would chop up a short piece.

## 2. WHAT ACTUALLY EARNS THE MARKS IN SECTION B
Nov 2024 examiner report, verbatim: "The most successful responses had a strong sense of audience and
purpose and included personal touches, humour and rhetorical questions."
The marks are in the VOICE, not the furniture. A script with a perfect headline and a by-line, written
in flat neutral English, scores lower than one with no by-line and a voice that knows who it is
talking to.

## 3. EXACT LENGTHS
Section B total target: 350-420 words. Section C (Q9): 400-450 words.

| Form | Paragraphs (incl. opening/closing) | Words per paragraph | Sentences per paragraph |
|---|---|---|---|
| Article - school/college magazine | 5-6, plus headline | 60-90 | 3-5 |
| Article - website | 5-6, plus headline | 60-90 | 3-5 |
| Contribution to a website | 5, plus title | 65-90 | 3-5 |
| Letter to a friend | 4-5 | 40-70 | 2-4 |
| Letter to a newspaper/editor | 5 | 60-90 | 3-4 |
| Speech / talk | 5-6 | 50-80 | 3-5 |
| Q9 discursive | 5-6 | 70-90 | 3-5 |

Sentence length across all of it: average 12-15 words. At least one sentence of 6 words or fewer per
paragraph. One long sentence (25+) somewhere per piece, not in every paragraph.

## 4. WHAT A PARAGRAPH IS
One idea. When the idea changes, the paragraph changes. A paragraph of 4 sentences that all make the
same point in different words is one bad paragraph, not four good sentences.
Signposts at the start of a paragraph are what mark the shape for the examiner: "Firstly" / "But" /
"However" / "Even so" / "The result is" / "So what does that mean for us".

## 5. RUNNING ORDER: A -> B -> C
Reasons, in order of weight:
1. Section A needs the most time per mark, and it is where he runs out. Measured this morning: 27
   minutes spent and Q3 not finished. A must be first, because A is the section that gets eaten.
2. Section A is the most method-heavy section. It is the only one where he is applying a four-step
   procedure under time. Freshness pays there more than anywhere else.
3. Reading the texts first warms the ideas for Section B. He may not lift from the texts, but he may
   use the thinking.
4. Section C is his strongest section (typed draft 21/30; friend's 17/30). If time squeezes anywhere,
   squeeze the section where a slightly rushed version still scores well. That is C.

## 6. THE CLOCK (relative minutes from the start of writing)
  0-8     read both texts, once, with a pen
  8-13    Q1, Q2, Q4, Q5 - the five free retrieval marks
  13-33   Q3 (10 marks, six points)
  33-53   Q6 (10 marks, six points)
  53-78   Q7 (15 marks, five comparison paragraphs)
  78-83   stop, breathe, count Section A answers
  83-125  Section B (30 marks, one piece)
  125-165 Section C, Q9 (30 marks)
  165-180 check: punctuation pass, apostrophes, comma splices, missing words, paragraphs
Do not start C before minute 120. If A has overrun to minute 90, write B in 35 and C in 45 and accept
it - but never leave C blank.

## 7. IF C RUNS OUT
Write the introduction, two body paragraphs and the short conclusion. A finished short C scores far
above an unfinished long one. Same for B: a finished 300-word article beats an abandoned 500.
