## Part 1 — What the question is

Verbatim examples from the archive:

> 'New opportunities present new challenges.' **Discuss.**
> 'I much prefer working in a group than on my own.' **To what extent do you agree with this statement?**
> **Discuss** whether travel broadens the mind.
> 'Be adventurous.' **To what extent do you agree with this statement?**

Mark scheme, May 2025, page 21, verbatim:

> "As no audience is specified, the examiner is assumed to be the audience. Candidates are free to
> agree or disagree with the statement and may present a variety of arguments… Examiners should be
> open to a wide range of interpretation."

So: **one reader. Your opinion. No format. 30 marks. About 400 words. Word count is advisory** —
the November 2023 report says explicitly: *"The word count is advisory and responses are assessed on
their merits, not on the number of words."*

## Part 2 — Where the 30 marks actually are

AO4 = 20. AO5 = 10. Nothing else.

**AO4, May 2025 mark scheme p.22, verbatim:**

| Level | Marks | Bullets |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1–4 | basic and limited in clarity · little awareness of purpose/reader · little awareness of form, tone, register |
| 2 | 5–8 | broadly appropriate · some grasp of purpose · straightforward use of form, tone, register |
| 3 | 9–12 | communicates clearly · clear sense of purpose · appropriate use of form, tone, register |
| 4 | 13–16 | communicates successfully · **secure** realisation of purpose · **effective** use of form, tone, register |
| 5 | 17–20 | **perceptive and subtle** · **sharply focused on purpose** · **sophisticated** use of form, tone, register |

**AO5, p.23, verbatim, Level 5:** "Manipulates complex ideas, utilising a range of structural and
grammatical features to support coherence and cohesion · Uses extensive vocabulary strategically ·
Punctuates writing with accuracy to aid emphasis and precision, using a range of sentence structures
accurately and selectively to achieve particular effects."

**What is not in fifteen AO4 bullets:** balance, counter-argument, conclusion, evidence, research,
length. Not one.

## Part 4 — Reconciling the contradiction

The 29/30 script has no introduction paragraph and no conclusion paragraph. Yet four separate reports
say top candidates had "a clear structure with a strong introduction and conclusion."

Both are true, because "introduction" is describing a *function*, not a paragraph. The 29/30's first
seven words — "It's the 18th century. You; a writer" — do introduce: they set the frame the whole
answer runs on, and the reader knows exactly what kind of piece this is by sentence two. Its last
line — "Who knows how much time we have left?" — closes the argument it opened.

**The requirement is therefore:** within two sentences the reader must know what this piece is about
and what it is doing; within the last two sentences it must be finished. How you do that is free.

## Part 5 — What every top script does

1. **The question's own words appear in almost every paragraph.**
   29/30: "This is what **English** has become", "**English** has become more valued".
   26/30: "a worthy example of **this**", "the **challenge** of raising their children".
   24/30: "**working in a group**" / "**working alone**" in every single paragraph.
   The graded script: "fresh air and exercise" appears twice in the whole answer, once in the
   opening hedge and once in the closing restatement. In between it drifts to vitamin D, weather,
   sleep, and social anxiety.

2. **Something a camera could catch.** Red squiggly line. White screen. Frozen MacBook. Chores done
   alone. A football match. Diapers. Not "it can improve mental health".

3. **A turn in every paragraph.** Cost into gain, or assumption into reversal: "or so you think".

4. **A voice.** "You". A question to the reader. A verdict.

## Part 6 — The three engines

Pick one in thirty seconds. Each has produced a real script.

**Engine A — two worlds.** For "X has changed" or "X is better than Y" prompts.
Set a scene before, set the same scene after, let the gap do the arguing. (29/30.)

**Engine B — four areas.** For life-claim prompts: *everyone should, it is important to, we are never
too old, the best things in life*. Take work, school, family, yourself. One example each. (26/30.)

**Engine C — two sides, then a verdict.** For preference/comparison prompts: *I prefer X to Y, to what
extent*. Argue each side in alternating paragraphs, propose something practical, end qualified. (24/30.)

## Part 7 — The paragraph engine (drill this, not the essay)

1. **Name it and tie it in.** Which part of the claim does this paragraph prove? Say so, using the
   question's words.
2. **One thing you can see.** A specific, physical detail.
3. **A second thing, or a "you" moment.**
4. **The turn.** Cost → gain, or assumption → reversal.
5. **The exit line** returning to the question's terms.

Six sentences, 90–110 words. Works for all three engines.

## Part 8 — Sentence craft

Target: **mean 12–15 words, and at least one sentence of six words or fewer in every paragraph.**
Justification is in Part 3 — that is what the two top scripts do and what the two 17/30 scripts do not.

The short sentence is the landing. It goes after the long one:
> "You even have to keep up your social life whilst also doing all of the chores at home yourself.
> **There's barely any time left to study.**"

## Part 9 — The opening he is stuck on

Four proven openings. Each must contain the question's key words by sentence two.

1. **The scene.** (29/30) "It's the 18th century. You; a writer, sit down at your desk."
2. **The honest position.** (24/30) "I think most people would agree with this statement. But I think
   there are positives and negatives of both."
3. **The concrete instance.** Start with one specific example, no preamble, then say what it shows.
4. **The reframe.** Accept the statement but move the question: state it, then say what it misses.

**Never:** any of the nine named banned phrases (Part 10).

## Part 10 — What is punished, quoted

> "I am going to be making strong and robust points on my stance"
> "these points fall short of the mark because…"
> "as the dust settles, where do we find ourselves?"
> "indubitably exigent" — named in 2022, 2023, 2024 **and** 2025
> "Clearly a topic like this calls for deep thinking"
> "It is important to tell people what side they are on"
> "I am going to present some robust points"
> "I will continue to convince you to switch to my side"
> "I do not pretend to know everything, I only speak on matters I know I will win"
> "Once we have embraced a stand point we must look at the whole picture"

Verdict: *"more engaged in remembering the scaffolding than they were in constructing a considered
argument."* And, November 2023: *"learned templates and inappropriately sophisticated and unhelpful
vocabulary."*

Also penalised: philosophical quotations as openers; "most crucial reason" as an opener; writing a
story instead of an argument; lifting the Section A texts; invented statistics; long answers that
repeat.

## Part 11 — The AO5 pass (3 minutes, 10 marks)

Named in every report since 2020: missing sentence punctuation · comma splicing · missing or misused
apostrophes · homophones · misspelling basic vocabulary · not capitalising 'I' · missing capitals at
sentence starts · random mid-sentence capitals · subject–verb agreement · verb tenses · missing words
· weak paragraphing.

## Part 12 — The five-stage ladder

1. Read the 29/30 and label its six moves. No writing. (10 min)
2. Completion: a real paragraph with its last sentence removed. Write only that. (5 min)
3. One paragraph from a three-item plan. (15 min)
4. A full answer, timed, 60 minutes, Engine B. (60 min)
5. Self-mark against the AO4/AO5 grids; rewrite the weakest paragraph only. (20 min)
