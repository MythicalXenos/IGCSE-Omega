# Section B — five forms, one full model each, on real prompts

3 Nov 2026. Every question is verbatim from an archive paper. Every model written to the same
standard, all three bullets covered, both texts referenced, ~390-410 words.

## The three tens
| AO | Marks | Earned by |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | 10 | all three bullets, each developed; ideas from BOTH texts plus your own |
| AO4 | 10 | the NAMED audience and the NAMED form, held from first line to last |
| AO5 | 10 | the nine-item accuracy list |

9 on AO4 came from opening address + speaker naming himself + direct address + closing return.

## The form sentence — say this before you write
"This is a ___ to ___." Then write a first line that ONLY that form could have.
- Talk: greeting + audience named out loud
- School website article: headline + standfirst + subheadings
- Magazine article: headline + standfirst + pull-quote voice + subheadings
- Website contribution: title + contributor standfirst + first-person voice + subheadings + sign-off to the site
- Letter to a friend: "Dear X," + shared context + contractions + "Take care," + name

# 1. TALK — June 2023 Q8

Question, verbatim:
"You have been asked to give a talk to your peers about the importance of walking and getting
outside. You should include: advice about different types of walks / the benefits of walking
outside / the problems you might experience. Write the text of your talk."

Model:

"Good afternoon, everyone — thanks for staying behind. I will keep this short, which is itself part
of the argument.

I want to talk about the cheapest thing you can do for yourself, and the thing all of us are giving
up. Walking. Not the gym, not the running club, not anything with a subscription.

First, the types. There are three worth knowing. There is the walk to somewhere, which does not
count. There is the loop — one hour, no destination, out of your door and back again, which is the
one I recommend. And there is the long one, once a month, the kind that takes a whole afternoon and
makes you eat like an animal afterwards. The loop is the one that changes your week; the long one is
the one you remember. The passages you were given make the same point in two very different ways:
one writer is out in weather and loves it, and the other is out in weather and hates every minute
and goes again anyway. Both of them came back different.

Second, the benefits, and I will not pretend they are complicated. You breathe better. You sleep
better — genuinely, that is measurable. Things that seemed enormous at four in the afternoon look
survivable by six. And there is the small one nobody mentions: you start noticing the street you
have lived on for eleven years. There is a tree outside the shop. I found it in March.

Third, the honest part. It will rain. Your shoes will be wrong. There will be a dog. Around the
second week it will feel like a chore you invented for yourself, and you will skip three days and
feel stupid about it.

So here is my advice, and it is only one thing. Do not make it a habit. Make it an appointment.
Tuesday, after school, forty minutes, with someone who will knock on your door.

That is it. Thank you."

Marks: AO1 9 - AO4 9 - AO5 7 = 25/30
AO4 9: greeting, "I want to talk about", "you" throughout, rhetorical framing, closing thanks.
AO1 9: third bullet is honest rather than padded; both texts named as a contrast.
AO5 7: accurate; deliberately spoken register with fragments ("Walking.").

# 2. ARTICLE for a SCHOOL or COLLEGE WEBSITE — June 2026 Q8

Question, verbatim:
"Write an article for your school or college website to persuade your peers to improve their
handwriting. You should include: the importance of handwriting / why people do not use handwriting
regularly / the problems of bad handwriting."

Model:

**Your handwriting is getting worse. Here is why that matters.**
*Nobody is asking you to be calligraphic. We are asking you to be readable.*

There is a moment every teacher has had: a good answer, in an exam, that cannot be marked. Not
because it is wrong. Because nobody can read it.

**Why it still matters**
Handwriting is the only part of your education you do in real time, in public, with no undo. It is
also slower than typing, and that slowness is the point — you cannot write as fast as you think, so
you have to decide what you actually mean before it goes down. Both of the texts you read for this
topic come at the same idea from opposite ends: one describes writing as a craft with care in it,
the other as a tool that has simply been replaced. Neither writer is arguing for neatness. Both are
arguing for being understood.

**Why we stopped**
Obvious answer: keyboards. The less obvious one is that we never practise. You write by hand in an
exam and nowhere else, so handwriting is the one skill you are assessed on and never trained for.
Add to that the notes you have to take in forty minutes, where speed beats legibility every time,
and the result is predictable.

**What it costs**
Marks, first. An examiner with three hundred scripts reads what is easy to read. Then speed: people
with poor handwriting write less in the same time, because they slow down to stay readable and lose
the thread. Then the quieter cost — a page you cannot read back is a page you cannot revise from.

**What to do about it**
Ten minutes, twice a week. Copy one paragraph, slowly, and stop when it stops being neat. Hold the
pen further back. Write on the line instead of above it. That is genuinely the whole method.

Your future examiner has never met you. Give them a fair chance.

Marks: AO1 8 - AO4 10 - AO5 7 = 25/30
AO4 10: headline, italic standfirst, four subheadings, "we/you", a closing line that lands.
AO1 8: all three bullets; the second is the strongest because it goes past "because phones".
AO5 7: accurate, varied sentence lengths; 7 not 8 because punctuation range is limited.

# 3. ARTICLE for a MAGAZINE aimed at young adults — November 2023 Q8

Question, verbatim:
"Write an article for a magazine aimed at young adults entitled 'Time to learn something new?' You
should include: different types of things that might be learnt / advice about how to learn something
new / reasons why learning something new might be difficult."

Model:

**Time to learn something new?**
*You are not too old, too busy, or too far behind. You are just doing it in the wrong order.*

By the time you finish school, you will have been taught roughly forty subjects and chosen about
none of them. That is the argument for learning one thing badly, on purpose, as an adult.

**What counts**
Anything with a wrong answer. A language, an instrument, a sport, code, driving, bread. The useful
test is not "will this be useful" but "can I tell whether I got it right" — because feedback is what
separates learning from hobbies. Both of the texts you read for this topic make the point from
different angles: one celebrates the beginner's clumsiness, the other admits how much of it is
boring repetition. They are both right, and that is the honest shape of it.

**How to actually do it**
Pick something you can do for twenty minutes. Twenty minutes beats two hours, because twenty minutes
happens. Find the cheapest possible version of the equipment, so quitting costs you nothing and
starting costs you nothing. Then find one person who is slightly better than you and copy them
shamelessly. And keep a record — a notebook, a voice note, a photograph of the page. Progress is
invisible day to day and obvious over a month, and you will need the proof.

**Why it is hard**
Three reasons, all real. Time, obviously. Embarrassment, less obviously — being bad at something in
front of other people is a skill almost nobody practises after the age of sixteen. And the plateau:
week one feels like flying, week five feels like nothing is happening, and week five is when most
people stop.

The solution to all three is the same. Make it small enough that stopping would be more effort than
continuing.

Then be patient with yourself for about six weeks. That is roughly how long a thing takes to stop
being a decision and start being a Tuesday.

Marks: AO1 9 - AO4 10 - AO5 8 = 27/30
AO4 10: title as given, italic standfirst, byline voice, subheadings, direct address, punchline close.
AO1 9: every bullet has three specific points rather than one general one.
AO5 8: semicolon, dash, colon, deliberate fragments; still readable.

# 4. WEBSITE CONTRIBUTION — June 2022 Q8

Question, verbatim:
"A website is asking for contributions about the importance of clear written communication. Write
your contribution. You should include: the problems caused by unclear written communication / the
positive effects of technology on written communication / why good grammar matters."

Model:

**The message you meant is not the message they read**
*A contribution from someone who has been misunderstood by email four times this month.*

Here is what I have learned: nobody has ever been offended by a message that was too clear.

**When it goes wrong**
Unclear writing has a signature. It is not a spelling mistake; it is ambiguity, and ambiguity is
expensive. An instruction that could be read two ways will be read the wrong way, and then somebody
does the wrong thing for a week. A tone that was meant to be brief reads as cold. A "fine" that was
meant as "fine" reads as the end of a friendship. Both of the passages you read for this topic
describe exactly this from different sides: one shows writing that fails because it is too formal,
the other because it is too fast. The failure looks different. The cost is the same.

**What technology fixed**
Genuinely a lot. Autocorrect catches the errors I would have made at fourteen. Voice notes let me
say a difficult thing with my actual voice in it. And the speed of a reply now means the small
misunderstanding gets cleared up in ninety seconds instead of festering until Monday. Technology did
not make us worse at writing. It made us write far more often, which is how anyone improves.

**Why grammar is not snobbery**
Grammar is not about being correct. It is about being impossible to misread. A comma in the wrong
place changes who is being addressed. "Let's eat, grandma" is a joke, but the same error in a
contract, a doctor's note or a message to somebody who is upset is not funny at all. Punctuation is
the difference between what you meant and what is on the page.

**My rule**
Read it once, out loud, before you send it. If you would not say it that way to their face, rewrite
it. If you would, send it.

Thanks to the site for the space — I read the comments here more than I should.

Marks: AO1 9 - AO4 9 - AO5 8 = 26/30
AO4 9: title, contributor standfirst, four subheadings, first-person contributor voice, closing
address to the platform.
AO1 9: all three bullets with multiple developed points each; texts used as a contrast.
AO5 8: accurate; colon, semicolon, dash used purposefully.

# 5. LETTER TO A FRIEND — November 2025 Q8

Question, verbatim:
"Write a letter to a friend about communication and manners in the 21st century. You should include:
positive aspects of modern communication and manners / problems with modern communication and
manners / advice on how to improve online behaviour and show good manners."

Model:

Dear Ayesha,

I am writing this by hand on purpose, which is either a nice touch or extremely annoying. You can
tell me which when you reply, probably in four words, at midnight.

You asked me last week whether I thought people were ruder than they used to be. I have been
thinking about it and I think the answer is no. I think we are the same people with a much faster
delivery system.

The good part first, because there is a good part. I can talk to you from a bus in a city you have
never been to, for free, and see your face while I do it. My grandmother writes to her sister in
another country every week and it takes two days; I send a photograph of my lunch and it takes a
second. Both of the texts you were given notice the same thing from opposite directions — one
celebrates how connected we are, the other mourns how rarely we properly talk. I think they are
both describing the same phone.

Now the problems, and they are small and constant. We say things in text we would never say out
loud, and then we are surprised when it lands badly. We answer in three seconds and call it being
efficient. We leave people on read and call it being busy. And there is a particular kind of
rudeness that is new: doing it in front of someone. Half a conversation with a person in the room,
half with a screen. Nobody has decided whether that is allowed yet.

So, my advice, and it is as much for me as for you. If it is emotional, send a voice note or call.
If it would upset you to receive it, do not send it at nine in the evening. Put the phone in
another room for one meal a day and see how strange the first ten minutes feel. And answer people.
Not instantly — just answer them.

That is all. Write back, by hand, badly.

Take care,
Zain

Marks: AO1 8 - AO4 10 - AO5 8 = 26/30
AO4 10: "Dear Ayesha,", shared specific context, contractions, anecdote, direct advice to the
reader, "Take care," plus name. Unmistakably a friend, not a column.
AO1 8: all three bullets; the third is the strongest.
AO5 8: controlled; fragments used deliberately; apostrophes and commas clean.

# The comparison, in one table

| | Talk | School article | Magazine article | Contribution | Letter |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| first line | "Good afternoon, everyone" | headline + standfirst | title + standfirst | title + contributor line | "Dear X," |
| voice | "I" speaking to "you" | editorial "we" | magazine "I/you" | contributor "I" | "I" to one person |
| divisions | spoken signposts | subheadings | subheadings | subheadings | paragraphs only |
| last line | thanks / call to action | a line that lands | a punchline | thanks to the site | "Take care, name" |

Nine or ten sentences separate a 16 from a 25. They are all form, and they cost nothing.
