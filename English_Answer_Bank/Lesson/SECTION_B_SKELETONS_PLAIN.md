# Section B — bare skeletons, in the candidate's own style

3 Nov 2026. No boxes, no tables. Each form is a fill-in list you can hold in your head.
Written after the candidate produced his own speech skeleton, which was 80% correct.

---

## The candidate's own speech skeleton, quoted
"Good morning/afternoon everyone. I hope you are all having a lovely day. So, I would like to talk
about x. X has been blah blah blah [x] [x] [x] So, if you ever decide to go treasure hunting,
remember to blah blah blah. That is all. Thank you for listening"

**It is right.** Greeting, announce, three body paragraphs, call to action, thanks. That is the
whole form. Two edits only:
1. "I hope you are all having a lovely day" = filler. Replace with a hook: "I'll keep this short."
   or a question: "How many of you have ever lost something important?"
2. Three body paragraphs need spoken signposts so the listener can hear the structure:
   "First," "Second," "The last thing, and the honest one,"

# 1. TALK

Good morning, everyone. [one short line to get attention].
I want to talk about ______.
First, ______.
Second, ______.
Third, and this is the part that matters, ______.
So if you remember one thing, remember ______.
That is all. Thank you for listening.

# 2. ARTICLE — SCHOOL OR COLLEGE WEBSITE

[HEADLINE: a short, blunt opinion]. 
[STANDFIRST: one line, in italics, that makes them read on.]

[Two sentences setting up the problem.]

**Why it matters**
[Four sentences.]

**Why we don't do it**
[Four sentences.]

**What it costs us**
[Four sentences.]

[One short line to finish. The shortest sentence on the page.]

---

# 3. ARTICLE — MAGAZINE FOR YOUNG ADULTS

Same as above, except:
- the first line is an OPINION, not a scene: "You are doing this wrong."
- the writer is one person with attitude: "I tried it and quit."
- the last line is a TWIST: short, slightly unexpected, reframes the piece.

---

# 4. WEBSITE CONTRIBUTION

[HEADLINE]. 
A contribution from someone who ______.

I have a theory about ______: ______.

**Who ______**
[Four sentences.]

**What's good**
[Four sentences.]

**What's bad**
[Four sentences.]

My rule is ______.
Thanks to the site for the space.

Three things make it a contribution, not an article: the "who I am" line, everything in "I", and
the last line spoken to the website.

---

# 5. LETTER TO A FRIEND

Dear ______,

[One line that sounds like you texting them.]

You said ______ last week, and I've been thinking about it.

First, ______.
Second, ______.
Third, and this is the part I actually care about, ______.

Just ______. That's all I'm asking.
Take care,
[Your name]

The candidate's own draft ("Dear AI, I hope you and your family are doing well... It's been a while
since we've last spoken") has the right register but is missing the one thing that proves it is a
letter to a FRIEND: a shared, specific memory. "You said last week that..." is that line.

---

# 6. LETTER TO A NEWSPAPER / EDITOR

Dear Editor,

I am writing about ______.

Firstly, ______.
Furthermore, ______.
Finally, ______.

I would ask ______.
Yours faithfully,
[Full name]

No contractions. "we" and "our", not "you". No jokes.

# 7. SECTION C — Q9

[Opening: your position, with the statement's key words in a sentence of your own. 3 sentences.]

[Reason one: claim, something you can see, one short sentence, what it adds up to.]

[Reason two: a different kind of reason, same shape.]

[The other side at its strongest, then answered, then used to sharpen your own claim.]

[Landing: how much of the statement survives, and what follows from it. 2 sentences.]

---

## The only three things that differ across all seven
1. The first line.
2. Whether the paragraphs have subheadings or spoken signposts or neither.
3. The last line.

Everything in the middle is the same job: three blocks, each with a point from Text One, a point
from Text Two, and your own comment.
