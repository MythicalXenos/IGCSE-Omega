## 2(g) — 9 marks

Regulation can reduce negative externalities by prohibiting harmful behaviour. Kenya’s single-use-bag ban helped create a cleaner environment and reduced visual pollution and the danger to livestock that ate discarded bags; these are benefits to third parties not necessarily reflected in market prices. A clear legal ban may change behaviour across many sellers and consumers, avoiding the need to monitor each bag through a price instrument. However, the evidence says the problem was not totally removed: enforcement may be difficult, consumers may switch to other harmful substitutes, or bags may still be obtained/used illegally. Regulation also imposes enforcement and compliance costs and may be less effective where alternatives are expensive or unavailable. Its success depends on monitoring, penalties and practical substitutes; combining the ban with education, waste collection or incentives may work better. Overall, the Kenyan case indicates regulation can substantially reduce pollution, but a ban alone is not guaranteed to eliminate it.

## 3(d) — 6 marks

Division of labour splits production into specialised tasks. At Mwanza, workers can focus on filling bottles, sealing caps or applying labels rather than each making a whole drink. Repetition lets them become faster and more skilled, raising output per worker and lowering unit labour cost. With more than 1,000 workers and over one million drinks bottled daily, even a small productivity gain can substantially reduce average costs and help Coca-Cola supply East African consumers. Specialised workers may also identify defects in their particular task, improving consistency. Specialised machinery can be operated efficiently by trained staff. This depends on coordination and steady demand: repetitive work may lower motivation, while a breakdown at one stage can interrupt the whole production line.

## 3(e) — 9 marks

Textile supply may be relatively price elastic because Bangladesh has many textile firms and factories often operate below capacity. When prices rise, existing firms may use spare machinery and workers to expand output relatively quickly, and competing producers may enter or switch production. This suggests a large quantity response. Agricultural supply may be less elastic in the short run: crops take time to grow, land and seasons constrain output, and the reported 40% employment in agriculture does not mean production can immediately increase when prices rise. Yet the comparison is not certain. Textile capacity can be limited by finance, imported inputs, skilled workers or demand, and below-capacity operation could reflect constraints rather than readily available spare capacity. Some crops can be harvested or planted quickly, and farmers may switch land between crops, making particular agricultural supply elastic over longer periods. Weather and harvest conditions can also make agricultural output difficult to adjust. Therefore textiles are plausibly more price elastic where spare capacity and flexible inputs are available, especially in the short run, but the result depends on time period, crop type, and the actual reasons factories operate below capacity.

MAS’s national chain can gain purchasing economies: buying large quantities of groceries from suppliers may secure bulk discounts, lowering input cost per item. It can pass some savings to customers as lower prices, matching its stated focus, or retain them as higher profit. ## 4(c) — 12 marks

Consumers may benefit if a private operator uses profit incentives to improve efficiency, service quality and investment. The evidence suggests rising population, flight demand and a stronger industrial base could attract investors; improved capacity or facilities might reduce delays and offer more routes or convenient services. If cost savings are passed on, fares and airport charges could fall. Competition between airlines or airports may reinforce pressure to improve. But Lagos Airport may be a natural/local monopoly, limiting consumer choice; a private owner could raise landing fees, parking or retail prices to increase profit. Private investment is not assured to reduce fares, and removing government subsidies could mean higher charges or less provision of safety/facilities unless regulated. Trade-union resistance may also signal transition costs or job disruption. Consumers’ outcome depends on regulation, competition, service obligations and whether efficiency gains outweigh profit-driven price increases. On balance, demand growth makes investment and service improvement plausible, but consumers are likely to gain only if the privatised airport is effectively regulated and cost savings are not captured solely as profit.
