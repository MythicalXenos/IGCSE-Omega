### (h) [3]

After privatisation, the new private owner may seek to reduce costs to raise profit. It could therefore make some Angola Telecom workers redundant or reduce staffing, leaving those workers unemployed or with less secure employment.

### (i) [6]

Moving from A to B increases capital-goods output from 350 million to 450 million units, while consumer-goods output falls from 160 million to 130 million. Resources have been transferred from producing consumer goods to investment goods, so households may have fewer consumer goods available now. However, producing more capital goods, such as machinery, can expand productive capacity in the future. This may shift the PPC outwards and allow the economy to produce more consumer goods and capital goods later, supporting economic growth. The trade-off is that this future benefit depends on the capital goods being useful and productive; the immediate opportunity cost is the 30 million fewer consumer goods.

## Question 2

### (a) [1]

**B — Luxury.** YED = 4.6% ÷ 2% = 2.3, which is positive and greater than one, so demand rises more than proportionately as income rises.

### (b) [1]

**C — Private benefits + external benefits.** Social benefits include benefits to the decision-maker and positive spillover benefits to third parties.

A trade union is an organisation of workers that represents them and negotiates with employers to protect their interests, such as seeking better pay or working conditions.

### (d) [2]

Total costs = $6,200 + $3,550 = $9,750.

Average cost = total costs ÷ output = $9,750 ÷ 3,000 = **$3.25 per unit**.

### (e) [2]

High start-up costs mean a new business needs a large amount of finance before it can begin producing. It may be unable to raise enough capital or afford the necessary premises and equipment, making it difficult to enter and compete in the market.

### (f) [3]

An oligopoly may give consumers less choice because a few large supermarkets dominate the UK market. If those firms make similar decisions or compete weakly, consumers may have fewer alternative stores or products and less pressure for firms to improve quality or keep prices low.

### (g) [9]

Division of labour means each Foxconn worker specialises in a particular stage, such as handling the iPhone screen, assembling components, checking the phone or packaging it. With 270,000 workers, specialisation can let employees repeat a task and become faster and more skilled. This can increase output per worker and reduce the labour cost per phone, helping Foxconn produce large numbers of Apple phones efficiently and meet demand.

However, a worker may find a narrow, repetitive task boring, reducing motivation and possibly increasing absenteeism or staff turnover. Also, the production stages depend on one another: a delay in component assembly or checking could hold up packaging and shipment. Foxconn’s large workforce and many stages may make coordination important. Overall, division of labour can benefit Foxconn if its productivity and lower unit costs outweigh the risks of low motivation and bottlenecks; the scale of its factory makes the potential efficiency gains substantial, but careful management is needed.

Draw a pie chart with **tertiary employment the largest share**, **primary employment a very small share**, and **secondary employment between the two**. For example, label approximate shares of **P 5%, S 20% and T 75%**; the sectors should total 100% and be labelled clearly.

### (d) [6]

Construction of the $296 million tower may create noise, dust and vibration for people working or living near Sydney’s business district. These are external costs if nearby residents and businesses bear them without being compensated by the developer. Construction traffic can also increase congestion and local air pollution, imposing time and health costs on other road users and local people. These harms reduce third parties’ welfare even though they are not directly involved in building the tower; the scale and duration of the costs depend on the construction work and measures used to limit disruption.

### (e) [9]

Employing migrant workers can benefit Dubai because they provide labour for construction, cleaning and care services. If local labour supply is insufficient for these jobs, migrant workers fill vacancies and allow projects and services to operate. Construction workers can help build infrastructure and housing, while care and cleaning workers support households and businesses. This can increase output and help Dubai’s large population and economy function.

There may also be disadvantages. If many workers compete for similar jobs, the supply of labour may rise and place downward pressure on wages, especially for lower-skilled work. A high reliance on migrant labour could also make some industries vulnerable if workers leave or migration rules change. In addition, workers may send some of their income abroad, so not all income generated is spent within Dubai.

The benefits are likely to be significant where migrant workers fill shortages and provide services needed by Dubai’s 2.5 million-plus residents. Whether the net benefit is large depends on the quality of work and living conditions, the extent of local labour competition, and whether the economy can continue to attract the workers it needs.

## Question 4

### (a) [2]

The inelastic product is **X**, with PED −0.62. Total revenue = price × quantity = $3,000 × 50 = **$150,000**.

### (b) [6]

Consumers may not maximise their benefit if they have imperfect information about the health effects of sugary drinks such as Coca-Cola and Fanta. They may focus on their immediate enjoyment and underestimate longer-term health costs, including obesity. Advertising can also influence preferences and encourage repeat purchases, even when consumers know that a sugar tax has raised the price by 0.50 zloty per litre. If demand is addictive or habitual, buyers may continue purchasing despite the higher price. The extract says purchases continue after the tax and advertising has increased, suggesting that price signals alone may not lead consumers to choose the option that maximises their long-term wellbeing.

### (c) [9]

Expanding could allow NT Massage to serve more customers in busier locations and reach tourists and business people as well as its established local clientele. Training employees may help the family maintain service quality across branches, while hotel advertising could make the business more visible. Mei expects the tourist and business market to support higher prices in the new shops; if customers value the service and are less price-sensitive, this could raise revenue and profit. Hong Kong’s 7.5 million residents also provide a large potential customer base.

However, expansion requires spending on premises, staff training and advertising. Similar businesses already compete in Hong Kong, so NT Massage may not attract enough customers to cover these costs. Tourists and business people may not return regularly, and customers may reject higher prices if close substitutes are available. A chain may also be harder for the family to manage, and rapid growth could weaken the personal service that long-standing local customers value. Keeping the business small limits financial risk and lets the family retain close control over quality and customer relationships.

On balance, the family should expand only if it can test demand and secure enough finance and trained staff without reducing the quality of its existing service. A gradual expansion into one suitable busy area, supported by hotel promotion, would be less risky than immediately creating a large chain. If the trial does not attract sufficient customers in a competitive market, remaining small is likely to protect the successful business.
