### (h) [3]
One barrier is economies of scale. Large established firms such as Sony or Warner may produce and promote music on a scale that lowers their average costs. ### (i) [6]
Land is needed because coffee plants grow on land, and the beans are harvested from the plants. Labour is required because workers pick the beans by hand when the fruit is ready; machinery cannot identify the ripe fruit in the way the workers can. This shows why labour remains important even though machines perform much of the later process.

Capital is used when machines sort the beans and transform them from their natural state into products such as beans for sale, ground coffee or instant coffee. If machinery can process large amounts more quickly or cheaply than manual methods, it can raise output per worker. Enterprise is also needed to organise and combine the land, workers and machinery, choose how to process and sell the coffee, and bring a product to consumers. Each factor contributes to a different stage, so production depends on their coordination.

### (h) [9]
The economic problem is how to use finite resources to meet infinite wants, including decisions about what, how and for whom to produce. In Japan, private businesses produce large quantities of entertainment electronics. If firms respond to consumer demand and seek profit, price signals can encourage them to make goods that consumers want. The pressure to control costs and attract customers may improve efficiency and help resources move toward products with strong demand.

However, private firms may not supply enough services that are difficult to sell profitably to individuals or where benefits extend beyond the paying consumer. The extract identifies health, education and street lighting as public-sector services in Japan. If provision depended only on customers’ ability to pay, some people could be excluded from education or healthcare, and street lighting might be underprovided because people can benefit without buying it directly. Public ownership can use taxation and government planning to provide such services according to public need rather than profitability. The extract’s description of Cuba as relying mostly on public-sector provision illustrates a different allocation choice.

Public ownership can still face limited budgets and may have weaker incentives to reduce costs or respond quickly to changing preferences. Private ownership may be more effective for a range of consumer goods, while public ownership can ensure access to essential or non-profitable services. Overall, neither ownership model alone is likely to solve every aspect of scarcity effectively. A mixed approach—using private firms where consumer choice and cost incentives work well, alongside public provision for important services that markets may undersupply—is likely to allocate resources more effectively across different needs.

### (d) [6]
The Turkish Government may raise the minimum wage to increase the incomes of the lowest-paid workers. The extract gives a rise from TRY 2,020 in 2018 to TRY 2,558 in 2019, an increase of TRY 538 per month. A higher wage can improve the living standards of workers who previously struggled to afford basic needs and may reduce income inequality between low-paid workers and higher earners.

A higher minimum wage may also increase workers’ motivation. If employees feel their work is rewarded more fairly, they may work harder or be more productive, benefiting firms as well as workers. In addition, if low-paid workers earn more, fewer may need government benefits, reducing the cost of supporting them. The government may therefore see annual increases as a way of raising incomes and shifting some responsibility for adequate pay from the state to employers.

### (e) [9]
Internal economies of scale are reductions in a firm’s average costs as its own scale of operation increases. Istanbul’s airport is planned to handle around 200 million passengers each year and will have six runways and four terminals. At that scale, it may gain bulk-buying economies when purchasing large quantities of seating or other equipment. It may also spread the cost of technical systems and airport infrastructure over a very large number of passengers. Some costs associated with management, marketing and facilities may likewise be shared across a high volume of flights and passengers.

There are also risks that the planned scale will not produce lower average costs in practice. The 200 million passenger capacity is not proof that this number of passengers will use the airport. If passenger numbers are much lower, expensive runways, terminals and equipment may be underused, so the cost per passenger could remain high. The airport’s $11.7 billion construction cost is substantial, and managing a site of 29.5 square miles may create communication and coordination problems. If it becomes too large to manage effectively, diseconomies of scale could raise average costs. The scale of the benefit also depends on whether equipment can be bought and installed in large batches; a staged build may limit some bulk-purchase savings.

Overall, the airport has considerable potential to gain internal economies of scale because its intended passenger volume and six-runway design allow fixed and technical costs to be spread widely. Whether it actually benefits depends on how fully that capacity is used and how effectively the huge operation is coordinated. The economies are plausible, but not guaranteed merely by the airport’s size.

### (b) [6]
Occupational mobility is the ability of workers to move between different types of work. Tony Simpson’s sales of paintings to small craft shops have fallen as many of those shops close. If it is easier for him to retrain as an art teacher, he can move from a job with declining opportunities into teaching, where his labour may be needed. This increases the supply of labour available to teaching and reduces the supply of labour remaining in the market for selling paintings.

The government scheme provides an income while Tony trains, reducing the financial risk of changing occupations. More workers may therefore be willing to retrain for jobs with vacancies rather than remain in shrinking sectors. Greater occupational mobility can help labour move toward industries that need workers and reduce unemployment caused by a mismatch between workers’ skills and available jobs.

### (c) [12]
Productivity is the amount of output produced with a given quantity of inputs. Restaurants may use tablets to replace waiters taking orders and machines to prepare drinks such as espresso shots and cappuccinos. If technology allows customers to place orders faster and machines serve more drinks per hour, a restaurant may serve more diners with the same or fewer staff. Its output per worker or per unit of labour and capital could rise. Lower labour costs per meal and a more consistent service may also make the restaurant more attractive, increasing sales and allowing its equipment to be used more fully.

However, technology may reduce productivity at first. Customers unfamiliar with ordering tablets may need staff assistance or may take longer to place an order, so the restaurant uses both the equipment and labour without serving more customers. Ordering errors can result in meals being prepared incorrectly and resources being wasted. Tablets and automated machines also have purchase, installation, maintenance and repair costs. If customers value personal service and dislike a technology-led experience, they may choose another restaurant, reducing output relative to the resources invested.

The effect therefore depends on the balance between additional output and the full cost of the technology. A restaurant with high labour costs, many routine orders and customers comfortable with self-service may gain more than one whose customers need extensive assistance. In the short run, installation and adjustment problems may lower productivity; over time, as customers and staff adapt and errors are resolved, productivity may improve. Overall, increased technology is likely to raise productivity when it reliably speeds service and replaces more labour cost than it adds in equipment and support costs, but the gain is not automatic and depends on the restaurant’s customers and implementation.
