## 1(i) — 6 marks

Demand for labour is derived from demand for the goods workers help produce. A 5% rise in demand for wooden furniture means furniture firms may increase output; they need more timber, so they may purchase more wood and employ additional woodcutters to harvest it. This shifts demand for woodcutters to the right and may raise their wages and employment. The size of the effect depends on how much extra wood is needed, productivity and whether firms can use machinery or existing workers instead; it also takes time to train or recruit workers.

## 2(g) — 9 marks

Trade unions can bargain collectively, increasing workers’ power to negotiate higher pay and safer conditions. The GMWU has more than 16,000 members and has represented mine workers since 1944; it has already negotiated a $500 monthly minimum wage. Better pay may motivate workers and raise productivity, while pressure and discussion at its 2023 conference may help secure health-and-safety improvements. However, the union is still negotiating to protect jobs after demand for mining products fell. Higher wages and safety measures raise firms’ costs; if sales and revenue are falling, employers may resist further concessions or reduce employment. The union cannot guarantee jobs, and workers may judge membership less worthwhile if their employment remains insecure. Its effectiveness therefore depends on employers’ ability to afford the changes and the union’s bargaining strength. The evidence shows it achieved a wage gain, but its impact on job security and safety is still unresolved.

## 3(d) — 6 marks

Amazon Prime’s withdrawal removed a relatively cheaper substitute for Tennis TV, while Sky charged a much higher price. Some tennis viewers may switch to Tennis TV, increasing demand for its subscriptions and shifting its demand curve right. This raises the equilibrium quantity and may allow Tennis TV to raise its price, consistent with its December 2023 increase. The size of the shift depends on how many viewers want Tennis TV’s coverage and whether they regard it as a close substitute for Sky; it is not every consumer who will switch.

## 3(e) — 9 marks

A subsidy can lower the cost or price of rail travel, encouraging passengers to switch from road transport to trains. If fewer cars use roads, congestion and related external costs such as pollution and lost time may fall, benefiting third parties. The Sri Lankan subsidy is intended to make train travel cheaper. But price reductions alone may not attract passengers if trains are overcrowded and tracks need upgrading; SLR’s Rs 331bn loss over ten years suggests service provision may require a substantial and continuing subsidy. The government faces an opportunity cost, and upgrading capacity may be needed before additional passengers can be served. Other policies such as road pricing or traffic restrictions may target congestion more directly. Subsidies can help if rail is a practical substitute and capacity is adequate, but they are less effective without improvements in reliability and comfort.

## 4(b) — 6 marks

An oligopoly is a market dominated by a few large firms. Uganda’s petrol market has five main companies and high start-up costs, which make entry difficult and protect the incumbents from new competitors. The firms are interdependent: each company’s choices can affect the others. They compete through branding and non-price methods rather than only price—for example, customer service at Vivo Energy and loyalty cards at Hass Petroleum—which may build customer loyalty and differentiate their petrol offers. These features are consistent with oligopoly, although the exact concentration and degree of competition depend on each firm’s market share.

## 4(c) — 12 marks

Expansion could give Řešení access to a larger client base: about 12% of Czech businesses are in Prague compared with 3.5% in Brno. Winning new accountancy clients could increase sales and profit, and higher prices in the capital may raise revenue per service. A second office also spreads the firm’s risk across two cities and might allow it to spread marketing or administration costs over more clients. However, its small team is already very busy, so it may need to recruit accountants and pay rent and set-up costs in Prague; travelling 200 km between offices could add coordination costs. Competing firms in Prague may make it difficult to attract clients, and the current Brno reputation may not transfer automatically. Expansion is most likely to benefit the firm if it can recruit qualified staff, maintain service quality and secure enough clients to cover the extra costs; otherwise the larger market and higher prices may not translate into profit.
