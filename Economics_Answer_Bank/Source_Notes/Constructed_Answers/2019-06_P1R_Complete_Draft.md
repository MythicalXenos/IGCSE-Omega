Customers may have **less choice**, because Robert is the only local specialist in handmade door signs. With no nearby rival offering similar signs, customers may have to accept his prices or travel farther to find another supplier.

### (i) [6]
Robert may prefer to remain in a niche market, where he makes individual signs and gifts to each customer’s requirements. Expanding into mass production could make it harder to preserve the personal service and custom-made quality that has earned positive reviews. Staying small lets him concentrate on the growing preference for locally sourced, individually made products.

Expansion could also require extra finance, premises or employees, increasing costs and the risks he faces. Because Robert works from a workshop in his own home and has been successful for nine years, he may value independence and the way he currently operates more than the possibility of higher profit. The market for specialist carvings may also be too small to justify the extra capacity. These factors give Robert reasons to keep the business manageable and focused on its existing customers.

## Question 2

### (a) [1]
**C — Maximise profits.** Profit maximisation is a common objective of private-sector firms.

### (b) [1]
**B — Roads.** Roads are likely to be provided by the public sector in Cyprus’s mixed economy.

### (c) [3]
On the pie chart, label **tertiary** as the largest employment sector, **secondary** as the second largest, and **primary** as the smallest. One illustrative split is **T 60%, S 30%, P 10%**; the required feature is their relative sizes, not those exact percentages.

### (d) [1]
A free rider is someone who **uses or benefits from a common resource without paying for it**.

### (e) [2]
Congestion can create air pollution that harms people who are not driving, such as pedestrians. Those third parties bear health costs without being part of the road-use transaction, so the pollution is an external cost.

### (f) [3]
The public sector may not have enough doctors available to give vaccinations to everyone who needs them. Paying private pharmacies to provide flu injections adds more convenient outlets and increases the capacity to vaccinate vulnerable groups, helping the government protect them.

### (g) [9]
Funding flu vaccinations can be justified because an injection protects vulnerable people, including older people, pregnant women and those with other health problems, from severe illness. Preventing infections may reduce the need for further treatment, saving the government some healthcare costs. Vaccination can also reduce the spread of flu to others. If fewer people become ill, more may be able to continue working and contribute to production. The programme therefore creates benefits for the recipients and positive spillovers for other people and the health service.

However, the annual cost is over **£100 million**, and this money could be used for other healthcare needs. A vaccination may not prevent every illness, so some people may still need treatment and the expected savings may not fully occur. It may also be difficult to identify every person most at risk, and the wider benefits and costs are difficult to measure precisely. Whether the spending is worthwhile depends on how many serious cases it prevents and the value of the healthcare resources saved compared with alternative uses of the money.

## Question 3

### (a) [1]
**D — Increasing technological advancements.** Better technology can raise the amount of output an economy is able to produce.

### (b) [1]
**D — Education.** Education is a want for which people may need to allocate scarce resources, rather than one of the basic necessities listed.

### (c) [2]
New total revenue = price × quantity = $0.85 × 95,000 = **$80,750 per month**.

### (d) [1]
**Inelastic.** Revenue rises from $75,000 before the change to $80,750 after the price increase, consistent with quantity demanded responding less than proportionately to the higher price.

### (e) [6]
A production possibility frontier shows the maximum combinations of goods that can be produced using available resources and technology. An outward shift from PPF1 to PPF2 means the economy can now produce more capital goods and more consumer goods. This could result from an increase in the quantity or quality of labour, such as a larger or better-trained workforce, or from investment that increases the capital available to firms. Improved technology can also raise productivity, enabling more output from the same resources. With greater productive capacity, the economy can expand output without having to sacrifice the same amount of the other good; this is economic growth.

### (f) [9]
Trade unions represent workers and can negotiate with employers for higher wages, improved working conditions or greater job security. SAFTU has nearly **700,000 members** and is South Africa’s second-largest trade-union federation, so coordinated bargaining may give its members more influence than if each worker negotiated alone. If employers agree to better pay or conditions, workers may gain higher incomes and improved welfare.

However, higher wages raise firms’ costs. If the wage is pushed above the market-clearing level and productivity does not increase, firms may demand fewer workers, reducing employment for some people even as those who remain earn more. A union’s influence may also be limited if its membership is small relative to the workforce, if legislation restricts its actions, or if firms can relocate or replace labour. The report notes that union influence has declined globally; the size of SAFTU’s membership alone does not establish how much leverage it has over every employer.

The overall effect depends on the strength of bargaining, how widely employers recognise the union, and whether higher wages are matched by productivity gains. If workers become more productive, firms may be able to pay more without cutting employment. If not, stronger bargaining may benefit members who retain jobs while reducing opportunities for other workers. A labour-market diagram could show a higher wage and lower quantity of labour demanded when a union successfully raises pay above equilibrium.

## Question 4

### (a) [2]
At a price of **$20**, the graph shows quantity demanded of **50** and quantity supplied of **20**.

Excess demand = 50 − 20 = **30 goods**.

### (b) [6]
The deal gives NBC Universal the exclusive US rights to televise the Olympic Games through 2032, making the broadcasts a unique product for its television, internet and mobile platforms. This can attract a large audience because viewers wanting to watch the Olympics have few direct alternatives. NBC can sell advertising to firms that want to reach those viewers; a larger audience makes its advertising space more valuable and can raise its revenue. The exclusivity also creates a barrier to entry for rival broadcasters wishing to show the same events. If the advertising revenue generated over the period is sufficient, it can help NBC cover the **$7.75 billion** rights cost and benefit from the deal.

### (c) [12]
Google’s **94.33%** share of the South American search-engine market gives firms access to a very large audience if they advertise there. Since consumers do not pay to use search engines, advertising is a way for firms to reach potential customers while users continue to use the service free. A high share may make Google advertising more likely to be seen and help firms gain customers and sales. Google’s large scale may also support investment in improving its search service; firms can benefit if those improvements make search advertising more effective.

There are risks for advertisers. Google’s dominant position may give it market power to charge high advertising prices, particularly when the alternatives together account for only **5.67%** of the market (Bing has 1.30% and other providers 4.37%). Firms may have little choice but to use Google to reach search-engine users, increasing their costs and dependence on one platform. With limited competition, Google may also have less pressure to improve the service or keep its prices low. If the cost of advertising exceeds the additional revenue from new customers, a firm’s profit may fall.

Overall, advertising on Google is likely to benefit firms that need broad access to search users and can convert the extra exposure into sales. Its 94.33% market share makes that reach valuable, but also creates a risk of high prices and dependence. The net benefit depends on the advert’s effectiveness, the firm’s target market and whether the resulting extra sales justify Google’s fee; a dominant share alone does not guarantee that every firm will profit.
