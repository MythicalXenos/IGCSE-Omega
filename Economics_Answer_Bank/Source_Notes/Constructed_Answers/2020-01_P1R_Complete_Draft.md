### (h) [9]
A fine can make over-harvesting more costly. Orient Seafood Production (OSP) was fined **$1.5 million** after failing to report that it harvested **113,400 kg** of sea cucumbers over two years. If the penalty is large enough, it reduces the expected profit from exceeding the permitted harvest. Fines can deter OSP from repeating the offence and warn other firms that over-harvesting can result in a substantial financial loss. This could protect sea cucumbers, which help keep the coral-reef ecosystem healthy, and reduce the external costs imposed on the ecosystem and other users.

However, a fine may be imposed only after harm has occurred. OSP had already harvested a large quantity over two years, so damage to the reefs may have taken place before the penalty. Monitoring harvests and enforcing the legal limit also takes government resources. The firm had made high profits from selling sea cucumbers, and demand in China makes sales attractive; if expected profits exceed the likely fine, the penalty may not deter the firm. Other measures, such as close monitoring, tighter quotas or suspending licences, could be needed as well.

Whether fines are effective therefore depends on the likelihood of detection and whether the penalty outweighs the expected gains from exceeding the harvest limit. The $1.5 million fine may discourage future offences, but by itself it cannot undo damage already caused and may be insufficient if over-harvesting remains highly profitable.

### (e) [9]
Cars may have a more price-elastic supply than organic strawberries because manufacturers can sometimes increase output by using spare factory capacity or adding a shift when prices rise. Car production is not directly dependent on the weather, whereas strawberry crops depend on growing conditions. Strawberries also take time to grow and are perishable, so farmers cannot quickly produce a large additional crop or store it for later sale. These factors can make strawberry supply less responsive to a price increase.

But car production is not automatically elastic. It requires expensive machinery, specialised components and time to expand factory capacity. High costs may make it unattractive to build cars in advance and store them. The **28,951 organic farms** in the Republic of Korea may collectively be able to adjust output, and strawberry supply may become more responsive over a longer growing period. In contrast, car supply could be more elastic in the long run if manufacturers have time to expand or reorganise production.

Cars are likely to have the more elastic supply when manufacturers have spare capacity and time to respond, while the perishability and growing cycle of organic strawberries restrict short-run adjustment. The comparison depends on the time period, factory capacity and growing conditions; the number of farms alone does not establish which product has the more elastic supply in every circumstance.

### (c) [12]
Division of labour means splitting production into tasks so that workers specialise. In Wong’s noodle factory, workers blend ingredients, knead dough, and cut noodles into different sizes. If each worker repeatedly performs one task, they may become faster and more skilled and spend less time switching between stages. The factory could produce noodles more quickly and increase output per worker. This may help Wong meet demand from the noodle-based food stalls that buy from the factory—around **70% of stalls in Jenjarum**—and increase output without a proportional rise in labour inputs.

However, specialisation may make work repetitive. Workers who continually knead or cut noodles could become bored, lose concentration and make errors, reducing output or quality. A delay at one stage can also hold up the whole production process and jeopardise supplies to the local stalls. Division of labour is not the only way to raise productivity: Wong could train workers to use noodle-making equipment or invest in more advanced machinery. Training and technology might improve output more than further task separation if machinery is the main constraint.

Overall, division of labour could be an effective way to raise productivity if the tasks are suitable for specialisation and the factory coordinates each stage. It is not necessarily the best method on its own: the result depends on the amount of routine work, worker motivation, and whether training or improved machinery would deliver a larger productivity gain. Wong may gain most from combining clear task specialisation with training or appropriate equipment.
