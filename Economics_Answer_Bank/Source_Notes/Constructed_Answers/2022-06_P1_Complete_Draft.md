### (e) [1]

### (f) [2]

### (g) [3]
Draw and label a new demand curve for apples to the **left** of the original demand curve (D1). Label the new intersection with supply at a **lower equilibrium price** and **lower equilibrium quantity**. Cheaper bananas make this substitute more attractive, reducing demand for apples. Label the shifted curve and both new equilibrium values on the axes. The ER specifically warns that omitted labels lose marks (PDF pp. 8–9).

### (h) [3]
A disadvantage is that the work may become monotonous for the Malta warehouse employees. If an employee spends the whole shift repeatedly sealing boxes for consumer orders, there is little variety in the task. This boredom may reduce motivation and productivity. The ER notes that defining division of labour is not enough: identify a disadvantage, put it in the employee/task context, and develop it (PDF pp. 10–12).

### (i) [6]
The German bottle-return regulation is likely to reduce negative externalities from discarded plastic. Consumers pay an extra €0.25 when buying a bottle but receive it back when they return it, so the financial incentive makes returning bottles more attractive. The reported 98.5% return rate suggests that very few bottles are left as litter; less plastic pollution reduces harm to the environment and to people affected by waste. The scheme also costs the government less, potentially leaving more public funds for other environmental measures. The effect depends on what happens to returned bottles and whether the remaining 1.5% and other plastic waste still create significant pollution, but the very high return rate indicates a substantial likely reduction. (Analysis, not a counterargument-led assessment; ER PDF pp. 13–14.)

## Question 2

### (a) [1]

### (b) [1]
**B — External economies of scale.** These are lower average costs that benefit firms across a growing industry, rather than only one individual firm.

### (c) [1]
Increased competition may reduce a firm’s profit, for example if it has to lower prices to retain customers.

### (d) [2]
Total costs = fixed costs + (variable cost per unit × output) = $75,000 + ($525 × 1,350) = **$783,750 per month**. Include the currency unit; the ER reports some candidates lost the final mark by omitting the dollar sign (PDF p. 16).

### (e) [1]
A takeover occurs when one firm buys another firm, gaining control of it.

### (f) [2]
Consumers may form a habit of buying a particular brand. Even if another product would give them greater benefit, brand loyalty can make them keep buying their familiar choice rather than compare alternatives.

### (g) [3]
If the price of wheat falls from $4.50 to $4.00, the quantity supplied falls from 150 to 100 units: this is a **movement down/left along the existing supply curve**, not a shift of the curve. At the lower price, producers are less willing to supply wheat because the potential revenue/profit per unit is lower. (Alternatively, a rise from $4.50 to $5.00 increases quantity supplied from 150 to 200 units.) The ER’s example uses the $4.50-to-$4.00 movement and stresses context plus development (PDF pp. 19–20).

### (h) [9]
An increase in skills and qualifications can increase the supply of teachers, but it will not always do so. Labour supply means the number of people willing and able to work in an occupation. More science, maths and modern-language graduates enlarge the pool with relevant qualifications. In England, a £9,000 bonus for graduates in these subjects can raise the financial reward from teaching and encourage some to enter the profession, increasing the number willing to teach.

However, qualifications alone do not determine occupational choice. Long working hours, bureaucracy and difficult student behaviour may make teaching unattractive, so some qualified graduates may choose other jobs. Science graduates in particular can use their qualifications in alternative employment; if those jobs offer better conditions or rewards, the additional qualified people will not become teachers. The bonus is also limited to certain subjects, so it does not incentivise every qualified graduate.

Therefore, more qualified people are likely to raise teacher supply when pay and working conditions make teaching competitive, but the increase is not automatic. The extent depends on how many graduates are both willing and able to teach, and whether the bonus offsets the non-pay disadvantages. Use the data to explain the mechanism, rather than merely repeat it. ER expects a developed two-sided argument and applied evidence for the higher levels (PDF pp. 21–24).

### (c) [3]
Draw and label a **leftward shift in labour supply** (SL to SL1). Raising the school-leaving age keeps more young people in education, reducing the number available to work at each wage rate. With labour demand unchanged, show and label a **higher equilibrium wage rate** and **lower equilibrium employment**. The ER stresses full labelling (PDF pp. 25–26).

### (d) [6]
A production possibility frontier shows the maximum combinations of capital and consumer goods an economy can produce using its available resources and technology. An outward movement from PPF1 to PPF2 means Zambia’s productive capacity increased, so it can produce more of both types of goods. This may result from economic growth through a larger or better-qualified labour force, more capital investment, improved infrastructure, or more efficient technology. For example, investment in machinery, transport or skills could raise output per worker in agriculture, copper mining and construction. With greater resources or productivity, Zambia can expand production without simply transferring the same fixed resources from one activity to another. The PPF depicts increased capacity; it does not by itself prove which specific cause occurred. (One-sided analysis; apply the sectors in the QP. ER PDF pp. 27–29.)

### (e) [9]
Brick production creates external costs where its smoke and dust impose costs on people who are not part of the transaction. The extract describes severe air pollution in Bangladesh and says children, older people, pregnant women, and people with respiratory or heart conditions may be especially affected. As demand for bricks rises with construction, output may also rise, increasing emissions. Those health effects are not necessarily included in brick producers’ private costs or the price paid by construction firms and households. If pollution worsens illness, affected people may suffer lower wellbeing or incur medical costs, and the government may need to spend more on healthcare. The scale—about 23 billion bricks a year—suggests the potential external impact could be substantial.

However, not every cost is external. More than one million people work in the industry and receive income, while construction users obtain benefits from the bricks; wages and the resources used in production are private costs, not spillover costs. Some people exposed to pollution may also be buyers or workers, so their position as third parties cannot simply be assumed. The extract identifies health risks but gives no measured illness rate, monetary valuation of damage, or comparison of pollution with other sources, so the exact size of external cost is uncertain.

Overall, there are strong grounds to conclude that a significant part of brick production’s pollution-related health damage is external, because it affects vulnerable residents who are not involved in producing or purchasing the bricks. The extent cannot be quantified from the evidence given, and the industry’s employment and private production costs should not be mislabelled as external costs. (Balanced, applied assessment; ER says high-level work weighs both sides and uses the evidence, PDF pp. 30–33.)

## Question 4

### (a) [2]
Total demand = 98 + 114 + 107 = 319 seats. Excess supply = 408 − 319 = **89 seats**. Show the working and unit.

### (b) [6]
The free-rider problem occurs when people can benefit from a good without paying for it. Street lighting is non-excludable: people can use illuminated streets whether or not they contribute to the cost. It is also non-rival, since one person benefiting from a lit street does not normally reduce the light available to others. If a private firm charged each pedestrian, people could avoid payment while still benefiting, so the firm might not collect enough revenue to provide lighting. As a result, private provision may be too low or absent. Government can fund street lighting through taxation, allowing the wider population to benefit from improved pedestrian safety, motorists’ visibility and the more inviting environment described in the question. (Develop the chain; ER cautions against listing disconnected points, PDF pp. 35–37.)

### (c) [12]
A monopoly is a market in which one firm dominates supply. KCOM is the only telecoms network provider serving Hull and nearby villages, so local customers may have little or no alternative provider. This weak competitive pressure can give KCOM market power: it may act as a price maker and charge more than providers elsewhere in England. Customers also face uneven access to fibre, low download limits in some places and restricted choice. Without rivals threatening to take customers, KCOM may have less incentive to improve coverage, quality or value for money; this can harm households and businesses that depend on reliable broadband.

A monopoly is not necessarily always bad, however. A large network provider may benefit from economies of scale: spreading the substantial fixed cost of infrastructure over many customers can lower average cost. Avoiding duplicate networks may also reduce wasted resources. If KCOM passes some cost savings to consumers or reinvests them, the firm could provide high-speed fibre (the extract cites speeds up to 250 Mb) and develop bundled phone, mobile and television services. A single network may also be more feasible in a smaller market where competing providers would each struggle to cover the full cost of infrastructure.

Whether consumers benefit depends on what KCOM does with any scale economies and how effective regulation or potential entry is. Lower costs do not guarantee lower prices or universal service if the firm retains the gains. The evidence of higher prices, incomplete fibre availability and low download limits points to real consumer disadvantages in some areas. Overall, monopoly is not inherently always harmful: scale and investment can create benefits, but in KCOM’s case the limited choice and reported price/service gaps suggest consumers are likely to lose unless cost savings are passed on and coverage and quality are adequately protected. (Balanced applied reasoning and a supported judgement are essential for the top evaluate level; ER PDF pp. 38–41.)
