## Constructed response

Privatisation is the transfer of a state-owned firm to private ownership. If Air India is sold, private owners may place greater emphasis on earning profit than the government did. Since the airline has been making losses and struggling to pay for fuel, stronger cost control and more commercially focused management could help it reduce losses and maintain reliable access to fuel. The government has already reduced almost half of the debt, which may leave the buyer with a less difficult starting position.

Private owners may also invest in Air India and use their experience to improve its operations. Air India already has a substantial domestic network and serves destinations in the US and Europe. Investment and improved management could help it compete more effectively and potentially expand service to more countries in Asia and Africa than its reduced network currently covers. If those routes attract enough passengers to cover their costs, the extra revenue could improve profitability.

However, private ownership does not guarantee that Air India will become profitable. It still faces competition from other airlines, and a private buyer may be unwilling to invest heavily if expected returns are low. Oil suppliers may also remain cautious because Air India previously struggled to pay for fuel. Although the government has reduced almost half of the debt, that does not establish that Air India’s remaining costs are manageable; without further state assistance, it may still struggle to pay them. A new owner focused on profit could also cut routes or capacity that are costly, rather than expand the network; that could limit Air India’s growth and revenue opportunities.

Overall, Air India could benefit in the longer term if the sale brings capable owners, fresh investment and a workable plan to control costs while rebuilding profitable routes. The debt reduction improves the prospects, but its past losses, fuel-payment difficulties and competition mean a sale alone is not enough to ensure success. The benefit is therefore plausible but conditional, rather than certain.

## Devil’s-advocate check

- The answer evaluates **Air India’s** likely commercial outcome, not generic welfare effects on consumers or workers.
- “Almost half” is tied to the QP’s statement about the government’s debt reduction; it is not presented as exact debt remaining or total debt cleared.
- Route restoration is conditional on sufficient passenger revenue covering route costs. The QP gives no route-level demand, cost, debt total or post-sale investment commitment, so none is invented.
- The counterargument does not assume that public ownership is inherently inefficient or that private owners will necessarily invest.
- The final judgement follows from the stated conditions and acknowledges uncertainty.
