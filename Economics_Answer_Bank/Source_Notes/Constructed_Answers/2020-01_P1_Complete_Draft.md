### (h) [9]
Remaining small could benefit Wonderful Wax because it can keep its candles handmade from soy wax and pure essential oils. The founder established the business to help the environment, and a small operation may make it easier for Elissa to maintain control over ingredients and production methods. A distinctive, environmentally friendly product can attract customers in a niche market, some of whom may be willing to pay more for handmade candles rather than mass-produced alternatives. Personal service and a clear brand identity may also help the firm retain these customers.

However, staying small may limit output. If demand for the candles grows, Wonderful Wax might be unable to supply all interested customers and could lose sales. The larger firm has offered production facilities, which could let the business make more candles and spread fixed costs over a larger output. Economies of scale could lower average costs, allowing the firm to earn greater profit, reduce prices or invest in further sustainable production. Selling more soy-wax candles could also advance Elissa’s environmental objective by giving consumers a wider alternative to paraffin candles.

Growth could nevertheless change the product or dilute the founder’s control, especially because the offer requires sharing profits. If larger-scale production reduces the handmade quality or changes the ingredients, Wonderful Wax could lose the niche identity that attracts its current customers. The decision therefore depends on whether the new facilities can expand capacity while preserving the sustainable materials and characteristics that distinguish the product. Remaining small protects control and identity; a carefully managed expansion is more beneficial if demand exceeds current capacity and the larger firm’s terms allow the candles to stay environmentally friendly.

### (d) [6]
Demand for labour is derived from demand for the output that workers help produce. As machines become able to perform tasks previously done by people, they act as substitutes for labour. For example, factories may use robots and shops may use self-checkout machines, reducing the number of workers needed for those tasks. If machines perform the work more cheaply or efficiently, firms have an incentive to substitute capital for labour, decreasing demand for workers in the jobs being automated.

The extract gives the example of Google’s automated assistant answering customer queries and telecom firms switching to calls made by the assistant. If call centres can handle more enquiries with automated systems, they may need fewer human call handlers, lowering demand for that type of labour. This effect may continue as the technology improves and more tasks can be automated. The change is task-specific: firms may still demand workers to install, maintain or manage the systems, but the overall demand for labour doing the replaced routine tasks is likely to fall.

### (e) [9]
Fireworks can impose negative externalities on people who are not choosing to attend or pay for the display. The extract says former military personnel may have traumatic memories triggered by the loud “bang”, “pop” and “whizz” sounds. Animals may also be frightened by the noise. These third-party costs can occur even when the event is privately organised and the affected people receive no direct benefit. Because fireworks are used at religious, historical, public and private events, these effects may occur at different times of year.

There can also be positive externalities. People outside a ticketed venue may see the colourful display without paying to enter. They receive a benefit from the visual spectacle even though they are not among the intended paying audience. Therefore the same display may create a positive spillover for some nearby residents while imposing noise-related costs on others.

Whether negative externalities are greater depends on the fireworks’ location, time, duration and sound level, and on how close or sensitive third parties are. A display near former military personnel, pets or wildlife late at night may cause substantial harm, while a short, well-managed event may offer many bystanders a visual benefit with limited disturbance. Overall, the evidence identifies serious costs that are not reflected in the private ticket price, but it does not show that every third party is worse off; the balance depends particularly on the scale and timing of the noise compared with the visual benefits.

### (c) [12]
Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price. It can help South Africa’s government predict what might happen to demand for taxable goods if VAT raises the prices consumers face. After the VAT rate increased from 14% to 15% in April 2018, consumer spending and business confidence fell. If demand for a product is elastic, a price rise may cause a proportionately larger fall in quantity demanded; this could limit the amount of revenue collected from that product and may reduce spending in affected markets. If demand is inelastic, quantity demanded may fall less, so the government may be better able to maintain sales of that product while collecting tax.

PED is useful because it highlights that a VAT change will not affect all goods in the same way. The extract notes that fruit, vegetables and bread are exempt. For taxable items, product-specific PED estimates could help forecast the likely change in purchases and compare the revenue effects of different tax rates. This is more informative than assuming every household good responds equally to a price change.

However, PED is only a guide. A past estimate may not predict the response to a future tax change because income, preferences, available substitutes and business confidence may also change. The fall in spending and confidence after the 2018 rise happened at the same time as the VAT change, but that timing alone does not establish that VAT caused the whole decline. The extent to which businesses pass the tax on to customers also affects the price change used in the forecast. In addition, estimates for a few goods cannot predict the overall effect on government revenue if many products have different elasticities and tax bases.

Overall, PED is useful as one input because it helps estimate how consumers may change purchases when VAT affects prices, and therefore informs both revenue and consumer-impact forecasts. It cannot by itself determine whether VAT should rise: the government would need reliable, product-specific estimates, account for exemptions and tax pass-through, and consider other causes of changes in spending and confidence. A decision should combine PED evidence with broader fiscal and economic information rather than rely on the 2018 experience alone.
