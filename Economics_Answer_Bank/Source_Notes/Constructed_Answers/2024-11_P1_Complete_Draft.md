Trade unions can raise wages by negotiating collectively: workers have greater bargaining power than as individuals, and the threat of strikes can pressure employers to agree. In Germany, strike action disrupted railways and airports and 2.5 million workers were covered by the deal, demonstrating potential leverage; workers ultimately received a 5.5% pay increase. Higher pay can also improve motivation and retention. ## 3(d) — 6 marks

A higher minimum wage can encourage more people to offer labour because the reward for working has risen from $6.25 to $8.50 per hour; some may enter the labour force or increase hours. In the diagram, quantity of labour supplied rises along the supply curve from the initial Q1 toward Q3 at the higher wage. But firms demand only Q2 at that wage: if the wage floor is above the market-clearing W1, higher labour costs may reduce employment, creating excess supply of labour between Q2 and Q3. Some workers may then be unable to find jobs, and the actual increase in labour supplied depends on how responsive workers are and how many jobs remain available. Thus the higher wage may increase quantity of labour offered, not guarantee an increase in employment.

## 3(e) — 9 marks

Higher labour quality can raise productivity because education, skills and knowledge help workers use equipment effectively, solve problems and produce more per hour. The evidence notes that education affects workplace ability; migrant workers may bring useful skills and information, allowing a country to fill shortages and transfer knowledge. Higher productivity can lower unit costs, raise output and improve international competitiveness. However, education quality and relevance matter: qualifications may not match jobs, and training takes time. Productivity also depends on capital, technology, management, infrastructure and worker motivation, so skilled workers without equipment or effective organisation may not produce much more. Migration can improve skills available to firms but may also create shortages in the workers’ origin country or require language and qualification adaptation. Overall, labour quality is important and can be a major source of productivity growth, but it is complementary to capital and management rather than sufficient by itself.

## 4(b) — 6 marks

A possible reason is imperfect information. Some tourists did not know that booking the guide directly on arrival cost $20 rather than $34 online. Without information about the lower price, consumers may choose the more expensive booking and their preferences are not fully reflected in market decisions; some may not take the tour or may have less money for other tourism services. This can lead to resources being allocated inefficiently because guides’ services are not matched to informed consumer demand, while consumers may pay more than necessary. The information failure could be reduced by clearer online and on-island price information. However, the $34 online price may include advance certainty or convenience, so the price difference alone does not prove inefficiency for every tourist.

## 4(c) — 12 marks

X may gain economies of scale if its much larger user base spreads fixed costs across more transactions. The 530 million users could let X spread the high fixed costs of developing its “everything app” and advanced technology over a large output, lowering average cost. Its publicity and brand reach may reduce average marketing cost, and combining messaging, payments, ridesharing and food delivery may create operational economies or cross-selling: one platform can serve multiple functions. If lower average costs improve service or permit competitive prices, X may attract still more users. However, research and development is a significant cost, and adding services may create coordination and integration costs. A 65% fall in advertising revenue weakens finance, while dismissing thousands of workers may undermine content moderation, product development or service quality; cutting staff does not automatically create productive economies. Large scale can also cause diseconomies such as slower decisions or management problems. The outcome depends on whether users actually adopt and pay for the new functions and whether their revenue grows faster than total costs. X has scope for technical and marketing economies from its large user base, but these are not assured; without successful monetisation and effective integration, its high R&D costs and falling advertising revenue may outweigh lower average costs.
