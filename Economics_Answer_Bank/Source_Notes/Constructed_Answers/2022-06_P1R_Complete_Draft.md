### (i) [6]
Unilever plans to replace much of its non-recyclable packaging with more expensive recyclable plastic. This raises the cost of packaging its products. If selling prices and other costs stay unchanged, higher costs reduce profit per unit and may lower total profits. Finding suppliers and changing production or packaging processes could also create transition costs, particularly while Unilever reduces its use by **100,000 tonnes** and replaces the remaining **600,000 tonnes**.

However, recyclable packaging may improve Unilever’s reputation with consumers who care about environmental damage. If this increases demand or allows the company to maintain sales, revenue could rise and partly offset the higher packaging costs. Reducing non-recyclable plastic may also reduce waste-related costs or future regulatory risks. The effect on profits therefore depends on whether the extra costs are outweighed by stronger sales, savings or avoided costs; the extract gives no price or sales data to establish the net effect.

### (g) [3]
A change in the price of cocoa beans causes a **movement along the demand curve**, not a shift in demand. For example, when price rises from **$2.32 to $2.42 per kg**, quantity demanded falls from **400 kg to 300 kg**. At the higher price, consumers are willing and able to buy fewer beans.

### (h) [9]
An oligopoly can benefit Tanzanian telecom consumers if competition between the large firms keeps prices low or encourages better services. Vodacom, Airtel and Tigo together held **86%** of subscriptions. They may use their scale to lower average costs and invest in network quality or new services. If they compete to attract subscribers, consumers could gain from better coverage, bundles or lower prices.

However, a few dominant firms may have considerable market power. They might avoid price competition or collude, leaving consumers with high prices and limited choice. Smart Telecoms stopped trading because it could not compete with larger providers; this reduces the number of alternatives. The merger of Tigo and Zantel may further concentrate the market, making it harder for smaller firms to survive or new providers to enter. High barriers to entry could let existing firms maintain prices or provide weaker service.

Overall, oligopoly may benefit consumers when the major networks compete vigorously through price and service quality. But the merger and Smart Telecoms’ exit are signs that competition may be weakening. Whether consumers benefit depends on how strongly the remaining firms compete and whether regulators prevent collusion or abuse of market power.

### (c) [3]

### (d) [6]
Vaccination can create an external benefit because people other than the person receiving the vaccine may benefit. If more children in England receive the MMR vaccine, fewer people are likely to catch or transmit measles, mumps or rubella. This lowers the risk of infection for people who are not vaccinated, including those too young or medically unable to receive it. As the NHS invited unvaccinated children to attend, higher vaccination rates could reduce the spread of disease across the community. A healthier population may also mean fewer absences from school or work and less pressure on health services.

### (e) [9]
Machines can substitute for some workers in shops. Technology that automatically sorts or dispatches online orders may reduce the need for shop staff, particularly as town-centre shops close and **at least 10%** have been vacant for five years. If online sales replace in-store purchases, demand for some retail workers may fall, reducing their employment opportunities and wages.

The effect is not necessarily a fall in all labour demand. Online sales exceeded **20% of retail sales in 2019**, creating more need for warehouse operatives, delivery workers, software developers and people who maintain the machines and online systems. Machines may complement some skilled workers rather than replace them. The change in demand also depends on whether online retail expands total sales or merely transfers purchases from shops, and on the types of work for which employees can retrain.

Overall, substitutes such as machines are likely to reduce demand for certain jobs, especially routine shop-floor tasks. They may increase demand for other occupations linked to online selling and technology. The effect on total labour demand therefore depends on the scale of online growth, how many jobs are displaced, and whether new roles are created and accessible to affected workers.

## Question 4

### (a) [2]
Total tickets demanded = 4,200 + 6,000 + 3,250 = **13,450**.

Excess demand = total quantity demanded − quantity supplied = 13,450 − 9,600 = **3,850 tickets**.

### (b) [6]
A large public sector allows the Cuban Government to provide or control important goods and services, including education, healthcare and infrastructure. This may help ensure that essential services are available even when private firms would not find them profitable to provide. Since more than **75% of the labour force** works for the state, public ownership can also support employment and allow the government to direct resources toward national priorities. The government may monitor production and address external costs more directly than a collection of private firms, and can provide public goods that are difficult to sell to individuals.

### (c) [12]
Combining Instagram, WhatsApp and Facebook Messenger could create economies of scale because the group could share software, technical infrastructure and specialist staff across platforms. With more than **2.6 billion users** altogether, a shared messaging system could spread development and maintenance costs over a very large user base, lowering average cost. Facebook might also need fewer separate systems and could coordinate updates more efficiently. If the services work together smoothly, this may make them more attractive to users and help Facebook compete with rival apps.

There are risks that could prevent cost reductions. Integrating three large services is technically difficult and may require substantial investment before savings occur. Employees are unhappy or do not understand the changes, and the original founders have left; this could reduce staff motivation, lead to further departures and increase recruitment costs. Poor communication or management across a larger organisation may create diseconomies of scale. If integration causes service problems or concerns about control, some of the 2.6 billion users may move to competitors, reducing the scale benefits.

Overall, integration could lower long-run average costs if the platforms successfully share software and infrastructure while maintaining reliable services. The benefits are not automatic: transition costs, staff resistance and user departures could outweigh savings. Whether economies of scale occur depends on the success and cost of integration and whether consumers continue using the combined services.
