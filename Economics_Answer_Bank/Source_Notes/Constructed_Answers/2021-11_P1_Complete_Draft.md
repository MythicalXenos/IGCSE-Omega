## Question 1 — firms, productivity and costs (20 marks)

### 1(a) — 1 mark; QP p.2, MS p.4
**MS anchor:** D is the only correct option: firms may complete charitable work.

**Constructed answer:** **D — They may complete charitable work.** A charitable aim can take priority over maximising profit.

### 1(b) — 1 mark; QP p.2, MS p.4
**MS anchor:** B, a normal good. The given YED is positive and below one.

**Constructed answer:** **B — A normal good.** YED = 0.7 is positive, so demand rises with income; because it is below 1, the response is proportionally less than the income change.

### 1(c) — 2 marks; QP p.2, MS p.4
**MS anchor:** Include both output produced and input/resources used.

**Constructed answer:** Productivity is the amount of output produced in relation to the resources or inputs used to produce it.

### 1(d) — 1 mark; QP p.3, MS p.4
**MS anchor:** Social costs equal private costs plus external costs.

**Constructed answer:** **Social cost = private cost + external cost.**

### 1(e) — 1 mark; QP p.3, MS p.5
**MS anchor:** A person, company or country that supplies goods and services.

**Constructed answer:** A producer is a person or organisation that supplies goods or services.

### 1(f) — 2 marks; QP p.3, MS p.5
**MS anchor:** $50,000 + ($250 × 750) = $237,500 per month; working can earn one mark.

**Constructed answer:** Variable costs = $250 × 750 = $187,500. Total costs = $50,000 + $187,500 = **$237,500 per month**.

### 1(g) — 3 marks; QP p.4, MS p.6
**MS anchor:** Higher raw-material costs reduce supply; award the diagram marks for a labelled leftward supply shift, higher equilibrium price and lower equilibrium quantity.

**Constructed diagram instructions:** Keep demand unchanged. Shift supply left/up from S to S1. Mark the new intersection with D at a higher price and lower quantity than the original equilibrium: P1 > Pe and Q1 < Qe. Label S1, P1 and Q1 clearly. Do not shift demand.

### 1(h) — 3 marks; QP p.4, MS p.6
**MS anchor:** Give a reason, develop it, and apply the 0.8%-per-day context. The MS accepts protecting consumers from financially harmful loan rates.

**Constructed answer:** The New Zealand Government may want to protect customers from firms charging extremely high interest on loans. A daily rate above 0.8% can make the amount owed grow rapidly, so borrowers may struggle to repay and face financial harm. The legal cap limits the maximum rate firms can charge to 0.8% per day.

### 1(i) — 6 marks; QP p.5, MS p.7; ER p.4
**MS anchor:** Explain technical economies of scale specifically: expansion lets a larger firm make more cost-effective use of machinery. Use Cutting Edge’s powerful saw and develop the cost/output chain. The ER warns against generic scale-economy points, extract-copying and counterarguments on an analyse item.

**Constructed answer:** Cutting Edge had turned down jobs because its saw could not cut larger trees. As the firm expanded and received more work, it could justify buying a more powerful saw. This is a technical economy of scale: a larger firm can use machinery that would not be cost-effective at a very small scale. The saw can now be used across more gardening jobs, so its purchase cost is spread over more work and the equipment is used more fully. Cutting Edge can accept jobs it previously rejected, increasing its output and potential revenue while lowering the average equipment cost per job. This explains how expansion made the technical investment worthwhile.

## Question 2 — resource allocation, firms and externalities (20 marks)

### 2(a) — 1 mark; QP p.6, MS p.8
**MS anchor:** B, point X, which lies inside the PPC.

**Constructed answer:** **B — X.** A point inside the PPC indicates resources are unemployed or underused.

### 2(b) — 1 mark; QP p.6, MS p.8
**MS anchor:** C, employee rights.

**Constructed answer:** **C — Employee rights.** A trade union represents and seeks to protect workers.

### 2(c) — 1 mark; QP p.6, MS p.8
**MS anchor:** Accept reasons including market size, a niche market, lack of finance or the entrepreneur’s aims.

**Constructed answer:** A firm may stay small because it operates in a niche market with too few customers to make expansion profitable.

### 2(d) — 2 marks; QP p.7, MS p.9
**MS anchor:** PES = 2.7 ÷ 4.9 = 0.55, to two decimal places.

**Constructed answer:** PES = percentage change in quantity supplied ÷ percentage change in price = 2.7 ÷ 4.9 = **0.55**.

### 2(e) — 1 mark; QP p.7, MS p.9
**MS anchor:** A dominant firm sets the market price.

**Constructed answer:** A price-maker is a dominant firm with enough market power to set or influence the price in its market.

### 2(f) — 2 marks; QP p.7, MS p.9
**MS anchor:** A computer can help produce goods or services; this makes it capital.

**Constructed answer:** A business can use a computer to produce or provide its goods and services. It is capital because it is a man-made asset used in production.

### 2(g) — 3 marks; QP p.8, MS p.10; ER p.5
**MS anchor:** Finance is an accepted factor; apply the answer to the larger premises/double rent and explain the funding constraint. The ER says that merely repeating the rent figure is not enough.

**Constructed answer:** Access to finance could affect whether Abi can expand. Moving to a larger café would require her to pay almost twice the current rent and may involve other set-up costs. If the café’s revenue or savings are insufficient and a lender will not provide a large enough loan, Abi may be unable to afford the new premises even though customers currently wait for tables.

### 2(h) — 9 marks; QP p.9, MS p.11; ER p.5
**MS anchor:** Explain how traffic creates a negative externality and how regulation may reduce it; apply the Sunday vehicle ban over 8 km and reported 65% air-quality improvement. Evaluate attribution, enforcement/cooperation, the rising vehicle count and other pollution sources. The ER emphasizes assessment rather than extract repetition; it does not require a conclusion.

**Constructed answer:** Traffic fumes impose external costs on people in Jakarta who are not choosing to drive, including poorer air quality and possible harm to health. Regulation can reduce this negative externality by limiting the activity that creates it. Jakarta’s Sunday-morning ban keeps vehicles off over 8 km of roads, so fewer vehicles emit fumes in that area at that time. The report that air quality in 2016 was 65% better than before the regulations were introduced is evidence that the policy period coincided with a substantial improvement.

However, that comparison alone does not prove the traffic ban caused all of the improvement; other policies or changes may also have affected air quality. The ban applies to a limited area and time, while more than 17 million vehicles are registered in the city and their number is increasing. If enforcement or public cooperation is weak, drivers may evade restrictions, and pollution from sources other than traffic may remain. Thus regulation appears to have helped, but its reach and the evidence given do not show that it has eliminated Jakarta’s pollution problem.

## Question 3 — markets and labour (20 marks)

### 3(a) — 1 mark; QP p.11, MS p.13
**MS anchor:** C, lack of information.

**Constructed answer:** **C — Lack of information.** Without adequate information, consumers or producers may make choices that do not allocate resources efficiently.

### 3(b) — 1 mark; QP p.11, MS p.13
**MS anchor:** D, sales assistant.

**Constructed answer:** **D — Sales assistant.** Selling a service to customers is a tertiary-sector activity.

### 3(c) — 3 marks; QP p.11, MS p.14
**MS anchor:** Show a minimum wage above equilibrium and clearly label the new quantity of labour demanded and supplied.

**Constructed diagram instructions:** Keep labour demand and supply curves fixed. Draw a horizontal minimum wage W1 above We. At W1, label Qd1 where the wage line meets the downward-sloping demand curve (left of Qe), and Qs1 where it meets the upward-sloping supply curve (right of Qe). This shows excess supply of labour (unemployment). Make the lines and labels unambiguous, as the ER specifically warns.

### 3(d) — 6 marks; QP p.12, MS p.15; ER p.6
**MS anchor:** Advertising can shift demand to the right; apply it to Pepsi’s worldwide advertising during a popular international cricket tournament and develop the mechanism. A diagram is optional. The ER says analyse needs developed applied points, not a counterargument.

**Constructed answer:** Pepsi was a main advertiser during a Cricket World Cup watched at stadiums and on television by supporters of teams including India, Bangladesh, England, Pakistan and Sri Lanka. The advertising could make more consumers aware of Pepsi and associate the brand with a popular international event. Some viewers may then choose Pepsi more often or develop greater brand loyalty. Therefore, at any given price, more consumers may be willing and able to buy Pepsi: the demand curve shifts right, rather than there merely being a movement along the original curve. A diagram may show D shifting to D1 and quantity demanded rising from Qe to Q1 at the original price.

### 3(e) — 9 marks; QP p.13, MS p.17; ER p.6
**MS anchor:** Explain how raising the retirement age can keep some people in work for longer, using Singapore’s age/life-expectancy data. Assess whether it is the most effective method against alternatives such as migration, female participation and skills/qualifications, applied to Singapore’s manufacturing and growing financial-services sectors. The ER notes that assess responses need balance, but no conclusion is required.

**Constructed answer:** Raising the retirement age can increase the supply of labour because some people who would otherwise retire remain available to work. Singapore’s retirement age is 62 and is planned to rise to 63 in 2022 and 65 by 2030. This could keep some 62–64-year-olds in employment for longer, increasing the number of people able and willing to supply labour. Life expectancy is now over 84, so some older people may remain capable of working beyond 62.

However, a higher retirement age may not be the most effective way to provide the types of labour Singapore needs. Manufacturing contributes almost 25% of GDP, and some roles may require physical capabilities that not every older worker can offer. The growing financial-services sector may require particular qualifications and skills; simply retaining workers for longer will not automatically fill those vacancies. Training and qualifications could increase the supply of appropriately skilled workers. Migration or higher female labour-force participation could also add workers, depending on policy and employer demand.

The policy may therefore help retain experienced workers, especially where health and job requirements permit, but the evidence does not establish that it is the single most effective method. Its impact depends on whether older workers’ skills match vacancies; skills development, migration and participation policies may be more effective for particular shortages.

## Question 4 — percentage change, market structure and privatisation (20 marks)

### 4(a) — 2 marks; QP p.15, MS p.19; ER p.6
**MS anchor:** (87,100 − 80,750) ÷ 80,750 × 100 = 7.86%. The ER stresses following precision instructions and using units where appropriate.

**Constructed answer:** Change in profit = $87,100 − $80,750 = $6,350. Percentage change = ($6,350 ÷ $80,750) × 100 = **7.86% increase**.

### 4(b) — 6 marks; QP p.16, MS p.19; ER p.6
**MS anchor:** Define oligopoly as a market dominated by a few large firms; apply the few large trekking firms, differentiated routes/languages and expensive permits. Explain how differentiation supports non-price competition and permit costs act as barriers to entry. The ER says each point needs development; there is no fixed number of points.

**Constructed answer:** An oligopoly is a market dominated by a few large firms. In Pokhara, the QP says there are a few large trekking firms, suggesting that a small number of providers hold much of the market. There are also smaller firms, some offering less popular routes or guides who speak different languages. This is product differentiation: firms compete through the route or service offered, not only through price, allowing smaller providers to attract particular tourists. Expensive permits make it harder for new firms to enter. This barrier protects existing providers from new competition and helps explain why a few large firms can continue to dominate the trekking market.
