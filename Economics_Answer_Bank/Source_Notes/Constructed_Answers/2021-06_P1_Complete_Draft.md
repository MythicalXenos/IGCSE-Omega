A patent prevents other firms from copying Microsoft’s designs for a period of time. This protects the firm’s intellectual property and can allow it to charge a higher price, helping it recover the research and development costs of creating its software designs.

### (h) [9]
Division of labour splits production into separate tasks, with each worker specialising in one task. In a fast-food restaurant, some employees prepare burgers, others add salad, and others prepare fries. Repeating one task can make workers faster and more skilled, and they spend less time switching between jobs. The restaurant may therefore serve more customers quickly, reduce labour cost per meal and increase profit.

However, specialisation does not always benefit the firm. Repeating a simple job such as frying burgers may become boring and reduce motivation. Workers may make more mistakes or leave, creating recruitment and training costs and lowering productivity. The different stages also depend on one another: if the worker preparing salad is slow, completed burgers and fries may wait and customers still experience delays.

Overall, division of labour is likely to help when tasks are suitable for specialisation and managers coordinate the stages well. It is not an automatic benefit: boredom, staff turnover or a bottleneck can offset the time and cost savings, so its success depends on the work and how the restaurant organises it.

### (d) [6]
Excess supply occurs when quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded. At a fare of **10,000 Kip**, the figure shows eight LOCA drivers willing to provide journeys but only four journeys demanded, leaving four units of excess supply. Drivers compete to attract passengers by lowering the fare. As the fare falls, more passengers are willing and able to use the app, while fewer drivers are willing to supply journeys. The excess therefore shrinks. At **8,000 Kip**, quantity supplied and demanded are both six journeys, so the market reaches equilibrium and the excess supply is removed.

### (e) [9]
Government funding makes education free to students at Norway’s public universities and can increase access for people who could not afford tuition. Education creates private benefits for the student, such as qualifications and access to skilled, better-paid work, and external benefits for society. A more educated workforce may be more productive, improve living standards and support economic growth. If more people gain the skills employers need, unemployment may fall; Norway’s low unemployment is consistent with a strong labour market, although it cannot be attributed to education funding alone. Lower unemployment may also reduce government spending on benefits and increase tax revenue.

There are costs and limitations. Public funding has an opportunity cost: money used for education cannot be spent on healthcare or other priorities. The benefits may take years to appear, and graduates may leave Norway, so the country may not receive all the social return on its spending. Education also does not guarantee that graduates find suitable jobs; outcomes depend on labour demand and whether training matches employers’ needs.

Overall, public funding can bring substantial long-term social benefits and make access less dependent on family income, particularly if graduates remain and find productive work. Whether funding *all* education is worthwhile depends on the quality and relevance of provision and whether its long-term benefits justify the opportunity cost compared with other public services.

### (b) [6]
The diagram shows a relatively steep supply curve, indicating **inelastic PES**: a rise in price causes a proportionately smaller increase in the quantity of bananas supplied. Farmers may be unable to respond quickly because banana plants take time to grow and harvests depend on land, climate and weather. Even if a higher price gives farmers an incentive to produce more, they cannot immediately bring forward a much larger crop. Bananas are also perishable, so firms cannot store large quantities for a long time and release them when prices rise. Consequently, quantity supplied increases, but only slightly in the short run.

### (c) [12]
A minimum wage is the lowest wage a firm is legally allowed to pay. In Spain it rose by about 22% in 2019, from €735.90 to €900 per month, after rises of about 4% in the previous year. Workers who keep their jobs receive higher incomes, which may improve living standards and reduce in-work poverty. More disposable income may increase household spending and firms’ sales. Higher pay may also motivate employees, improving productivity enough to offset some of the increase in firms’ wage costs; the rise in output and sales could then benefit both firms and workers. The government may receive more income-tax revenue as wages rise.

However, firms face higher costs, especially if many employees are paid near the minimum. If they cannot afford the wage rise, they may employ fewer workers, reduce hours or raise prices. Job losses would harm affected workers and could reduce household spending and tax revenue. Firms may be less able to raise prices if consumers can switch to alternatives. The effect therefore depends on firms’ demand, their ability to absorb or pass on costs, and whether productivity increases. A large increase may be harder to manage than the smaller rises in the preceding years; the chart shows the minimum wage rose from €633.30 in 2010 to €735.90 in 2018 before reaching €900 in 2019.

On balance, the increase is more likely to benefit the economy if higher incomes lift spending and productivity without causing substantial job losses. The risk of harm is greater for firms with many low-paid workers and little scope to raise prices or improve productivity. The 22% increase is large relative to the previous annual rises, so its net effect depends on how firms and employment adjust over time.
