A merger may create economies of scale, lowering average production or research costs. If pharmaceutical firms pass some of these savings to patients through lower medicine prices, consumers benefit; a larger firm may also have more resources to develop new treatments. ## 1(i) — 6 marks

Collusion occurs when competing firms coordinate decisions rather than compete independently. If the six accommodation firms coordinate rents or restrict supply across more than one million properties, tenants may face higher prices and fewer affordable choices, worsening the affordability problem described. With less competitive pressure, firms may have less incentive to improve service or offer favourable rental terms. Consumers can be harmed even if the firms do not formally merge. The extent depends on whether the alleged coordination is proven and how much choice renters have in each local market.

## 2(g) — 9 marks

If 75 KWD is above the market-clearing wage for some Kuwaiti firms, a minimum wage raises their labour costs. Firms may respond by reducing the quantity of labour demanded, substituting machinery for workers or hiring fewer people; the diagram would show movement up the labour-demand curve from Q1 to a smaller Q2. However, firms already paying at least 75 KWD may face no immediate cost change, so their demand may remain unchanged. Higher wages could also improve motivation or reduce turnover, partly offsetting costs if productivity rises. The overall effect depends on how many workers previously earned less than the minimum and how responsive firms’ labour demand is to wage costs; it is therefore likely to reduce demand in affected firms but need not do so across the whole economy.

## 3(d) — 6 marks

Favourable weather after heatwaves and droughts can improve olive yields, shifting the supply of olives and olive oil to the right. More output is available at each price; Greece’s expected harvest rises from 130,000 to 230,000 tonnes, with Spain and Tunisia also forecasting increases. If demand is unchanged, the greater market supply lowers the equilibrium price and increases quantity traded. This is consistent with prices falling from €10,000 per tonne in summer 2024 to an expected €4,000–€5,000 in 2025. The change depends on actual harvests across producing countries and whether demand changes at the same time.

## 3(e) — 9 marks

Offering a discounted boxing day can support Fitnation’s charitable aim by making coaching and facilities accessible to city residents who otherwise could not afford them. The lower price may attract new customers, increase use of otherwise idle capacity and build goodwill or publicity, potentially bringing future members and revenue. But the facilities are already very busy on the discounted day. Full-price members paying $25–$50 per month may lose access to equipment or classes, feel they receive poor value and cancel; lost memberships and lower revenue could outweigh the extra discounted sales. The benefit depends on whether the discounted users are genuinely additional customers and whether Fitnation can manage capacity, perhaps by limiting bookings or adding sessions.

## 4(a) — 2 marks

At 430 consumer goods, the PPC shows 950 capital goods. At 510 consumer goods, it shows 800 capital goods. Opportunity cost = 950 − 800 = **150 units of capital goods**.

## 4(b) — 6 marks

If public transport in Bulgaria is an inferior good, its YED is negative: as incomes rise, demand for it may fall because consumers switch to private cars, especially given unreliable trains and late, slower buses. Operators could lose passengers and revenue, making it harder to cover costs. They can use YED estimates to anticipate the change and improve reliability, safety or journey times to make services more attractive. However, public transport may remain a necessity or normal good for commuters and people without cars, so demand may not fall for every group. The effect depends on the actual YED and the availability and cost of car travel.

## 4(c) — 12 marks

The AU$107m investment could improve transport links along the 72 km Swan River, with five electric ferries carrying over 130 passengers each and new stops linking to buses and the University of Western Australia. Easier access can widen job and education opportunities, reduce travel time and support local businesses; local firms building ferries may gain contracts and employ workers. If passengers switch from cars, congestion and pollution could fall. But public funds have an opportunity cost: the money could have supported health, education or other transport. Construction takes time, and safety risks for small-boat users may require extra spending; added parking could create congestion rather than reduce it. The net benefit depends on passenger uptake, useful connections and whether long-term social benefits exceed the cost and safety/parking problems. Investment is likely to benefit the economy if the network is well used and safely integrated with other transport, but the benefit is not guaranteed.
