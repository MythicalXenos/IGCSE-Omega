### (g) [9]

Division of labour allows each car-factory worker to specialise in a task such as welding, painting, inspection or testing. Repetition can make workers faster and more skilled, reducing the time and cost per vehicle. Cars may then be produced more efficiently and sold at a lower price or in greater numbers, benefiting the manufacturer.

However, repetitive work can become boring and reduce motivation. Lower morale may raise staff turnover and recruitment costs or reduce productivity. Production stages are also interdependent: a delay or defect in painting, for example, can hold up inspection and delivery. This may offset the efficiency gains and leave customers waiting.

The manufacturer is therefore not guaranteed to benefit. Gains are more likely when tasks are well coordinated and workers remain motivated; the result depends on whether higher productivity and lower unit costs outweigh the risks of monotony, staff turnover and bottlenecks.

### (e) [9]

Higher healthcare spending can improve access to treatment and help people remain healthy. Healthier workers may be absent less often and be more productive, enabling Indonesian firms to produce goods and services more reliably. This may support output and economic growth, which has already been helped by overseas demand for Indonesian products. Better health can also create positive spillover benefits for families and the wider community.

However, government spending has an opportunity cost: funds used for healthcare cannot be spent on education, infrastructure or other priorities. Benefits may take time to appear, and treatment does not guarantee that people will return to work or that the gains will remain in Indonesia. The improvement in growth may also reflect export demand rather than healthcare spending alone.

The increase is likely to benefit Indonesia if it funds effective services that improve health and workforce participation. The scale of the net benefit depends on how well the money is allocated and whether these longer-term gains exceed the cost of alternative public spending.

### (b) [6]

Air Asia operates in several markets, including flights, hotels, holiday packages, airport transfers and travel insurance. If demand or revenue falls in one area—for example, fewer people buy travel insurance—the firm may still earn revenue from its other services. This diversification can make total income less dependent on any one market.

Its operations across more than 150 destinations in 25 countries also spread exposure across locations. A downturn or disruption in one country may be partly offset by continued travel or sales elsewhere. Revenue from a range of products and destinations can help Air Asia manage uncertainty and keep the wider business operating. The reduction in risk depends on whether the markets are sufficiently different; a widespread shock to international travel could affect several of them at once.

### (c) [12]

The increase from €620 to €700 raises the earnings of workers previously paid the old minimum wage. With food, energy and clothing bills rising, the extra income may help low-paid households afford essentials and improve their standard of living. Workers may also feel more motivated, potentially improving productivity. Their additional spending can increase demand and revenue for firms, while higher incomes may raise tax receipts.

But firms employing affected workers face higher wage costs. If productivity does not rise enough to offset them, businesses may reduce hiring or employment, or raise prices. Workers who lose jobs could be worse off, and higher prices may reduce the real gain for other consumers. The impact may be limited because only 16% were paid the minimum wage, the lowest share in five years; many firms may already pay above €700. Effects also depend on firms’ ability to absorb the cost and the demand for their products.

The increase is likely to benefit many workers who keep their jobs, especially given the pressure on household bills, but it cannot be said to benefit everyone. The overall result depends on employment and price responses: the smaller share paid the minimum wage may limit firms’ cost increases, while workers who lose work or consumers facing price rises may not gain.
