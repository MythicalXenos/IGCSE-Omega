## Question 1

### (a) [1]
**D — Working conditions.** Trade unions represent workers and seek to improve their pay, employment conditions and treatment at work.

### (b) [1]
**C — An increase in demand is shown by a shift to the right.** A change in price causes movement along the demand curve; a non-price change in demand shifts the curve.

### (c) [2]
Economies of scale occur when a firm’s average costs fall as it expands its scale of production.

### (d) [1]
One main feature of oligopoly is that a few large firms dominate the market.

### (e) [1]
Excess supply occurs when quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded at the current price.

### (f) [2]
PES = percentage change in quantity supplied ÷ percentage change in price = 4.3% ÷ 3.7% = **1.16** (to two decimal places). Show the working.

### (g) [3]
Draw and label a **rightward shift in supply** for electric cars (S to S1). The new equilibrium has a **lower price** and a **higher quantity**. A subsidy lowers firms’ production costs or raises the return from supplying electric cars, so more are supplied at each price. Label the new curve and the new price and quantity on the axes. The ER notes that missing labels cost marks (ER PDF p. 3).

### (h) [3]
Privatisation could benefit Brazilian consumers if the new private owner has stronger incentives to reduce costs and improve the food company’s products. Lower production costs could allow lower prices, making food more affordable; alternatively, better quality could attract and retain customers. This benefit is not automatic: it depends on whether efficiencies are passed on to consumers and whether competition or regulation limits the firm’s market power. (Develop one consumer advantage in context.)

### (i) [6]
Market failure occurs when resources are allocated inefficiently. At the Tallinn Song Festival Grounds, only a few firms sell food and drink to as many as 65,000 visitors, so customers have limited choice inside the venue. Security checks prevent them bringing refreshments from outside, while concerts last long enough that visitors may need to eat or drink. With little competition and no outside alternative, sellers can charge high prices; slow service and long queues also reduce the value customers receive. As a result, food and drink are not allocated at an efficient price and quantity, and consumer welfare is lower than it could be in a more competitive market. (Applied one-sided analysis; ER says focus on market failure and avoid an irrelevant counterargument, ER PDF p. 4.)

## Question 2

### (a) [1]
**C — 250 units per worker per month.** Labour productivity = 33,750 units ÷ 135 workers = 250 units per worker.

### (b) [1]
**D — Increasing technological advancements.** Better technology can increase productivity and productive capacity, contributing to positive economic growth.

### (c) [2]
Opportunity cost is the value of the **next best alternative forgone** when a choice is made.

### (d) [2]
Fixed costs = rent + advertising = Tk20,250 + Tk5,125 = **Tk25,375**. Raw materials and labour paid in direct proportion to output vary with production, so they are not included. Show the calculation and unit.

### (e) [2]
An entrepreneur takes the risk of setting up or running a business. They may also organise the other factors of production to make goods or services; this risk-taking and coordination is why enterprise is treated as a factor of production.

### (f) [3]
Firms may benefit from retaining experienced workers for longer. If workers cannot access their private pension until at least age 57, some may continue working for two additional years. The firm can keep their skills and experience, avoiding some recruitment and training costs when replacing them.

### (g) [9]
A tourist tax could reduce overcrowding in Venice by increasing the cost of visiting. The city receives about 24 million tourists each year, and large visitor numbers can create negative externalities such as litter, congestion and pollution for residents and other visitors. A compulsory charge of €3–€10 per day raises the private cost of each trip. Some tourists may then choose a different destination or visit at a quieter time, reducing the number of people in Venice. The higher €10 peak-time rate gives a stronger incentive to avoid the busiest periods, which may reduce the external costs most when congestion is worst. Tax revenue could also help fund waste collection or other measures to reduce visitor-related problems.

However, Venice is highly popular, so many visitors may consider the tax small compared with the overall cost of an international trip and pay it anyway. In particular, the €3 off-peak charge may not deter many tourists. Even if visitor numbers fall, the reduction may be too small to eliminate overcrowding, and collecting payments could itself cause queues at entry points. The effectiveness also depends on how elastic demand for visits is and whether tourists can easily reschedule. If the revenue is used for mitigation, the tax may still help even when it does not greatly reduce visitor numbers.

Overall, the tax is likely to have some effect, particularly at peak times, but is unlikely on its own to solve overcrowding given Venice’s popularity and the modest charge relative to total trip costs. Its success depends on visitors responding to the price signal and the city using revenue to address remaining spillover costs; combining the tax with visitor-flow management may be more effective. (Balanced, data-applied assessment; the ER says assessment must be balanced and developed, ER PDF p. 5.)

## Question 3

### (a) [1]
**B — To sell a niche product.** A specialised product may serve a small group of customers without requiring the firm to become large.

### (b) [1]
**D — Decrease quantity of labour demanded and increase the wage rate.** A minimum wage above equilibrium raises the wage paid while firms demand fewer workers at that higher wage.

### (c) [3]
On the U-shaped LRAC curve, label the downward-sloping section **economies of scale**, where average costs fall as output expands. Label the upward-sloping section **diseconomies of scale**, where average costs rise at larger output. Mark the lowest point of the curve as the firm’s **most efficient point** (minimum average cost).

### (d) [6]
An external benefit is a positive spillover from consumption or production that benefits third parties. Students receive the direct benefit of Japan’s free primary and secondary education, but others may benefit too. A well-educated population can later provide skilled workers, including people doing socially useful jobs such as teaching or healthcare. These workers may raise productivity and contribute more output and tax revenue, which can support services and living standards for people who did not receive that education themselves. Because primary and secondary schooling is free and widely provided in Japan, these spillover gains can extend across society and even benefit future generations. (Develop and apply one-sided analysis; the ER stresses analysis of productivity/services in Japan, ER PDF p. 5.)

### (e) [9]
Increasing prices and charging for late arrivals or cancellations could improve Simona’s financial position. Her low prices and flexibility have contributed to long waits, lost customers and difficulty repaying a bank loan. A moderate price increase would raise revenue per appointment; if demand does not fall substantially, this could improve cash flow and help meet loan repayments. A fee for late cancellations could discourage missed appointments and compensate the business for time that might otherwise have been sold to another customer. Better scheduling and fewer no-shows could also make appointment slots available sooner, addressing the weeks-long wait that is driving customers away.

However, customers may choose Simona partly because her prices are low and she is flexible. Raising prices could reduce demand, especially if customers can switch to other hairdressers. A strict fee may also damage goodwill among clients who value her customer care, or deter customers whose late arrival is outside their control. If she loses more clients, revenue and loan repayment ability could worsen rather than improve.

Overall, a carefully explained, modest price increase and a fair, clearly communicated cancellation policy could benefit Simona if they preserve enough loyal customers while reducing wasted appointment time. They should be accompanied by improved appointment management; simply charging more without addressing long waits risks losing further custom. The result depends on customer price sensitivity, the availability of alternatives and how fairly the fee is applied. (Balanced, applied assessment; the ER requires balance and developed context-specific points, ER PDF p. 6.)

## Question 4

### (a) [2]
Percentage change in profit = ((2022 profit − 2020 profit) ÷ 2020 profit) × 100 = (($22,950 − $20,250) ÷ $20,250) × 100 = ($2,700 ÷ $20,250) × 100 = **13.33% increase**. Show the working and round to two decimal places.

### (b) [6]
PED may be relatively inelastic at Marina Café because its location and surroundings make the experience distinctive. Customers can enjoy sea and mountain views while having their drinks, which may make the café less substitutable for people who value the setting. Although the same brand of coffee is available elsewhere in Budva for €0.80 rather than €2.00, those cafés may not offer the same views or tourist experience. Customers who choose Marina Café for that experience may therefore continue buying coffee even if its price rises. Also, a cup of coffee may be a small share of a tourist’s total spending, so a higher price may not greatly reduce quantity demanded. The café could consequently charge a €1.20 premium and still retain some customers. (Develop context-specific reasons; ER PDF p. 6.)

### (c) [12]
Division of labour means dividing production into stages so each worker specialises in one task. It may increase productivity at Raphael’s churros-pastry factory because workers can focus on a repeated stage rather than switching between mixing, heating, cooling, adding eggs and shaping the dough. Repetition can make each worker faster and more skilful, reducing time lost between tasks. The process may also be easier to organise and machinery can be assigned to a particular stage. If the factory can make more pastry with its 23 employees, it may meet demand from restaurants and street vendors across Madrid more reliably and increase output per worker.

However, repetitive work can become monotonous. Workers who lose motivation or concentration may make mistakes, wasting ingredients or producing inconsistent pastry. Because stages depend on one another, a delay or error at one point—such as adding eggs after cooling—could hold up later shaping and disrupt deliveries to busy restaurants and street vendors. If Raphael’s small family business cannot provide enough staff cover, specialisation may also make it vulnerable when one worker is absent.

Other methods could raise productivity too. Training may improve workers’ skills and reduce errors; investment in better mixing, heating or baking equipment could increase output per hour. These alternatives may require capital and may not be affordable, while training alone may not remove the time lost switching between stages. Raphael could combine specialisation with task rotation to limit monotony and with targeted equipment or training at bottlenecks.

Overall, division of labour is likely to be a useful way to raise productivity if the pastry stages are sufficiently repeatable and can be coordinated. But it is not automatically the best method on its own: the size and finances of the 23-worker factory, the complexity of each stage, staff motivation and the cost of machinery or training all matter. A combined approach—specialisation with rotation and selective training/equipment—would likely be more reliable than relying on repetitive task division alone. (Balanced argument and supported judgement required for “evaluate”; ER highlights application, balance and a conclusion, ER PDF p. 6.)
