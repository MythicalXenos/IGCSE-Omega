## 1(i) — 6 marks

Street lighting is a public good: it is non-excludable and largely non-rival, so people may benefit without paying. Private firms may therefore be unable to charge each user and may provide too little because of the free-rider problem, creating market failure. Government provision can address this gap. The Brazilian Government spent about $100m replacing 389,000 lights, which could reduce darkness-related accidents and crime and benefit 4.5 million people, including third parties who are not direct buyers. This improves safety and quality of life where the private market may not provide sufficient lighting.

## 2(g) — 9 marks

Higher productivity means the same output can be produced with fewer workers, or more output with the same number. In UK car plants, robots can replace some workers, lowering labour costs and reducing firms’ demand for labour. However, productivity gains can lower unit costs, improve competitiveness and support lower prices or higher profits. Since demand for cars has continued to rise, firms may expand output and need more workers to build, maintain and programme the robots and meet that demand. Increased productivity therefore will not always reduce labour demand: it depends on whether output expands enough to offset the labour saved per car, and on whether technology substitutes for or complements workers.

## 3(d) — 6 marks

A business may choose objectives other than profit maximisation, such as caring for customers or making education accessible. Gillian charges only enough to cover costs on three days each week, enabling lower-income students to afford maths tuition. This can improve access to education and build a positive reputation, encouraging families to book her lessons. Since her time periods are already fully booked months ahead, this customer-focused objective may also support future full-price demand and allow the business to cover costs without maximising profit on every lesson.

## 3(e) — 9 marks

Oligopoly can benefit consumers through economies of scale: Bangladesh’s four leading mobile finance firms hold 95% of the market and may spread technology, security and transaction costs over millions of users. Lower average costs could support lower fees or investment in new services. The firms already serve over 50 million users, including people without traditional bank access, making deposits, withdrawals and transfers more convenient; growth and innovation may further improve service and quality of life for low-income users. However, concentration can also give dominant firms market power, reducing competitive pressure to lower fees or improve quality. Large firms may spend heavily on advertising to protect market share, and those costs may be passed to consumers. The benefits depend on whether scale savings and innovation reach users and whether regulation and rivalry prevent exploitation.

## 4(a) — 2 marks

At $30, quantity demanded = 40 and quantity supplied = 30. Excess demand = 40 − 30 = **10 units**.

## 4(b) — 6 marks

Tourism creates external costs when people not directly involved in the tourist transaction are harmed. Large numbers of visitors can overcrowd public areas and buses or trains, making daily travel and parks less comfortable for local residents. Tourists may also leave litter or cause pollution, imposing clean-up, health and environmental costs on residents and government. Higher tourist demand can push up local prices, so residents pay more for goods and services. These costs fall on third parties and may not be included in the prices tourists pay, so the market can produce more tourism than is socially optimal.

## 4(c) — 12 marks

Mars may gain purchasing economies by buying ingredients in large quantities for more than 30 confectionery brands, negotiating lower input prices and reducing average costs. It may gain marketing economies by promoting its brands across 180 countries and spreading advertising costs over high sales. Technical economies are also possible if specialist machinery and research are used across a large output. The Hotel Chocolat takeover could give Mars access to an established UK brand and help it enter new markets, increasing the scale over which these costs are spread. However, expanding the portfolio may raise coordination and management costs, and Hotel Chocolat’s premium image could be weakened if its product or brand is standardised. Large scale alone does not guarantee lower average costs; benefits depend on integrating the takeover and maintaining demand. Mars is likely to benefit from some economies of scale given its existing sales and global reach, but the extent depends on whether additional cost savings exceed integration and diseconomy costs.
