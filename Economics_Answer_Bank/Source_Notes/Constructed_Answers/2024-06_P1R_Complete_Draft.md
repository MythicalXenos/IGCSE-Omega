### (g) [9]

Employees may gain higher incomes when the legal wage floor rises to €1,934.40 a month. This can improve living standards, especially for low-paid workers, and may increase their spending in local businesses. Higher wages could also motivate staff, reduce turnover and help firms recruit, potentially improving productivity.

However, firms face higher wage costs. In a weak economy after the health crisis, businesses may have difficulty passing these costs on to customers. Some may reduce recruitment or hours, raise prices, or substitute machinery for workers; employment opportunities for low-skilled workers could fall. If inflation is high, the real purchasing-power gain from a nominal wage increase may also be limited.

The benefits depend on how many workers were previously paid below the new minimum, firms’ ability to absorb or offset the extra cost, and the effect on employment. Workers who keep their jobs are likely to benefit, but the overall impact on employees and firms is mixed when demand is weak.

### (d) [6]

Privatisation could give TAP Air access to private investors’ finance. Investment could be used to improve aircraft, routes or service quality, making the airline more attractive to passengers and potentially increasing revenue. A private owner may also impose stronger cost controls and performance targets; if these improve efficiency, TAP Air could reduce waste and become more competitive.

There is no guarantee that private ownership will achieve these outcomes. Investors may focus on short-term returns and cut services or jobs, while restructuring may involve substantial costs. If the airline retains market power on some routes, it may not pass efficiency gains on to passengers. The benefits depend on the investors’ plans, competition and whether investment raises productivity rather than simply reducing costs.

### (e) [9]

If demand for Prime is price inelastic, a higher price causes a proportionately smaller fall in quantity demanded, so retailers’ total revenue may rise. The reported popularity among teenagers and limited availability could make some buyers less responsive to price, allowing retailers to charge more and increase revenue per unit.

But the outcome depends on elasticity. If consumers can switch to other drinks or regard the high prices as excessive, quantity demanded may fall proportionately more than price rises, reducing total revenue. The large gap between the recommended £1.99 price and some Amazon listings may also reflect scarcity rather than a sustainable market price; availability and consumer reactions may change.

Therefore, a price rise can increase total revenue when demand is inelastic, but it is not always likely to do so. Retailers need to consider substitutes, brand loyalty and how far the higher price reduces sales.

### (b) [6]

External economies of scale arise when firms’ average costs fall because of benefits from growth in the wider industry. The railway improvements may make it cheaper and faster for Indian construction firms to transport cement, iron ore and steel. More reliable delivery can reduce delays and inventory costs, allowing firms to complete projects with fewer resources per unit of output.

A larger construction industry may also support specialist suppliers, skilled workers and better infrastructure, from which multiple firms benefit. These shared advantages can lower average costs and improve productivity beyond any one company. However, firms may not all be located near the routes, and congestion, project costs or shortages of skilled labour could limit the savings. The impact depends on how widely firms can access the improved network and related industry services.

### (c) [12]

Land reclamation could increase the area available for cultivation, easing Egypt’s land constraint and allowing more wheat to be grown. Guidance on fertiliser and irrigation may help farmers use inputs more effectively, raise yields per hectare and improve output from existing land. Higher productivity could increase domestic wheat supply.

However, reclaimed land may be costly to prepare and may not be as fertile as established farmland. Farmers using traditional methods may lack the skills, finance or equipment needed to follow the advice or adopt improved techniques. If irrigation is unreliable or fertiliser is unaffordable, potential yield gains may not occur; expanding cultivated area alone does not ensure higher output per unit of input.

Overall, the programmes could raise productivity if they are accessible, properly funded and supported by training and suitable infrastructure. Their effect is likely to be limited for farmers unable to finance or implement new methods, so outcomes will vary across farms and regions.
