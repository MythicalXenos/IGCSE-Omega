### (d) [6]
An inward shift of the PPF means the economy can produce fewer combinations of both capital and consumer goods than before. One reason could be the depletion of natural resources used in production. For example, if an economy runs out of important resources such as oil or coal, firms have fewer inputs available and productive capacity falls. This can reduce the maximum output of both types of goods, moving the PPF inward.

A loss of skilled workers could also reduce productive capacity. If workers emigrate to take better opportunities abroad, firms have less skilled labour available. With fewer workers able to operate equipment or produce goods efficiently, output of both capital and consumer goods may decline. Natural disasters, conflict or deterioration of machinery could also destroy or reduce available resources and contribute to an inward shift.

The merger could create economies of scale because Warner Bros. Discovery would have **59 million streaming subscribers** over whom it could spread fixed costs such as technology, programming and administration. This may lower average cost per subscriber. The two firms’ technologies and content libraries—including television channels, studios, DC Comics and CNN—could be combined, avoiding duplication and allowing a larger range of programmes to be marketed together. Specialist managers could coordinate the expanded portfolio, while a company valued at **$150 billion** may have easier access to finance on favourable terms.

However, the merger may also create diseconomies of scale. Combining large organisations can make communication and decision-making more difficult, and integrating different platforms or systems may be costly and take time. If employees oppose changes or leave, recruitment and training costs may rise. Overall, the scale of the combined subscriber base and assets makes lower long-run average costs plausible, especially if the firms share technology, marketing and administration effectively. The merger will not automatically produce economies of scale: the benefits depend on successful integration, subscriber retention and whether savings exceed restructuring and coordination costs.

### (c) [12]
Demand for labour is derived from demand for the goods and services workers help produce. When demand for an event rises, firms may need more workers to serve customers. For example, music festivals and football matches attract spectators, so stadiums employ extra security staff. When households buy more groceries before holidays and celebrations, supermarkets may employ more staff to stock shelves and serve customers. If demand for these products or events falls, the demand for the workers who provide them may also fall.

Derived demand is not the only influence on employment. Firms may meet extra demand by increasing productivity or using substitutes such as machines instead of hiring more staff. The extract gives examples of restaurant robots and automated online customer service, while machinery is also used in manufacturing. If these technologies can handle additional work at lower cost, demand for labour may rise less than demand for the firm’s output, or may decline in particular occupations. However, demand for labour can increase in other occupations needed to develop, build and maintain the machines.

Overall, derived demand can be a major influence on labour demand, especially for event security and supermarket staff where higher customer demand directly increases the need for workers. Its effect is smaller where firms can use machines or raise productivity to meet additional demand. The extent therefore varies across industries and depends on how easily labour can be substituted and how much additional output requires extra workers.
