### (h) [9]
A monopoly such as PKP may benefit consumers through economies of scale. Operating more than 18,510 km of railway track and carrying both passengers and freight allows fixed infrastructure costs to be spread across many journeys. Lower average costs could allow PKP to charge lower fares or invest in a more reliable, higher-quality service. A single operator may also avoid wasteful duplication of railway tracks and coordinate routes across Poland. Its stated aim of meeting consumers’ interests gives some reason to expect service provision to matter.

However, PKP’s dominance may leave passengers and freight customers with few alternatives. If routes are unavailable or service quality is poor, customers may have little choice but to accept it. With limited competitive pressure, PKP could act as a price maker, charge higher fares, or have less incentive to reduce costs and innovate. Its stated aims do not prove that prices are low or that all consumers’ needs are met.

Overall, monopoly can benefit consumers when scale economies are passed on through lower fares, broad coverage and investment. But this is not automatic: the outcome depends on PKP’s objectives, the degree of effective regulation and whether customers have realistic alternatives such as road travel. The evidence indicates a very large network and a consumer-oriented stated aim, but does not give fare or service-quality comparisons; therefore the potential benefits are plausible, not established for every Polish passenger. (Balanced and evidence-applied assessment; ER identifies a 9/9 response and warns against copying rather than using the extract, PDF pp. 17–20.)

### (d) [6]
People may ignore the harms of heavy mobile-phone use because the immediate benefits are salient while some costs occur later. A phone offers many functions and a constant flow of information, so checking it can provide instant entertainment or communication that is difficult to resist. If using the phone for many tasks has become a habit, people may reach for it automatically and give less weight to the future risk of sleep loss or anxiety. Seeing friends and family using phones extensively can also normalise the behaviour, making a person less likely to change it even when thumb pain or other harms arise. These habits and social influences can weaken rational decision-making, so users may continue a pattern that reduces their overall wellbeing. (Applied analysis, not a list of separate reasons; ER PDF pp. 22–24.)

### (e) [9]
The secondary sector converts raw materials into manufactured goods, so it can be important to an economy. Manufacturing directly produces items people and firms need and provides employment and incomes. The extract also notes that manufacturing generates tax revenue; governments can use this revenue to fund tertiary services such as healthcare and education. In addition, many service industries depend on manufactured inputs: transport needs vehicles, retailers need goods to sell, and hospitals and schools use equipment. A productive secondary sector can therefore support output, jobs and activity in other sectors.

However, the secondary sector is not the only source of growth or welfare. The tertiary sector provides essential services such as healthcare, education, banking and transport, and may account for a larger share of output and employment in a developed economy. Primary industries supply raw materials that manufacturing needs, so secondary production depends on agriculture, mining or other extraction. A country with limited resources or a comparative advantage in services may gain more from developing those sectors than from expanding manufacturing. Manufacturing can also have costs, including pollution or high capital requirements, and its contribution depends on productivity and demand for its goods.

Overall, manufacturing can play a substantial role because it creates goods, incomes and tax receipts and supplies inputs to services. But its importance is relational rather than exclusive: the economy relies on complementary primary and tertiary activity, and the relative contribution of each sector depends on the country’s structure and capabilities. (Balanced applied assessment; the ER’s illustrated response scored 8/9, with top marks held back by insufficient balance, PDF pp. 25–28.)

## Question 4

### (a) [2]
Total ticket demand = 22,200 + 21,000 + 20,250 = 63,450. Excess demand = 63,450 − 60,000 = **3,450 tickets**. Show the calculation. (ER PDF p. 29.)

### (b) [6]
The free-rider problem occurs when people benefit from a good without paying for it. National defence is non-excludable: people in Zambia cannot readily be excluded from protection if they have not contributed. It is also non-rival, because one person benefiting from defence does not reduce the protection available to others. Individuals therefore have an incentive to free ride rather than pay a private provider. Since it would be difficult for a firm to charge everyone who benefits, private provision could be unprofitable and too little defence might be supplied. The Zambian Government funds the army and air force collectively, using public revenue, so the population can benefit from national defence. (Analyse the causal chain rather than listing characteristics; ER PDF pp. 30–31.)

### (c) [12]
Government regulation may reduce air pollution by changing the incentives facing vehicle owners. In the proposed 800 km² Greater Manchester Clean Air Zone, owners of non-compliant lorries, buses, taxis and vans would pay £7.50–£60 for each day they drive in the area. Some may switch to cleaner vehicles, change routes or reduce driving, lowering emissions and the external health costs imposed on residents. The extract associates polluted air with more than 1,000 deaths a year, so a reduction in emissions could produce substantial health benefits. The charges may also raise public revenue that could support monitoring or environmental improvements.

The regulation may be less effective if firms cannot afford cleaner vehicles or have no practical alternative to driving in the zone. The century-old ice-cream business estimates an additional £29,000 yearly cost for its vans and says it cannot replace them. It may absorb the charge and become less profitable, pass it to customers through higher prices, or reduce activity and jobs; some firms may close. If firms continue driving and pay the charge, the tax may raise revenue without reducing emissions as much as intended. The effects also depend on enforcement, vehicle owners’ responsiveness to the charge, and whether public transport or other cleaner options are available.

Overall, a Clean Air Zone can be effective where the charge is high enough to change behaviour and cleaner alternatives are realistically accessible. The large area and serious health externality make intervention defensible, but the evidence of substantial costs for small businesses shows that a charge alone can impose hardship without ensuring a rapid vehicle switch. Its effectiveness would be stronger if paired with affordable transition support and reliable emissions monitoring. (Balanced, applied evaluation with a supported judgement. The ER shows a 10/12 candidate response whose evaluation was not balanced enough for top Level 3; it specifically calls for a conclusion/judgement, PDF pp. 32–35.)
