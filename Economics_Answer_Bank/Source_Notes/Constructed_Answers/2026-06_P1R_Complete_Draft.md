### (g) [9]

Lowering the school-leaving age from 16 to 14 could increase labour supply because some 14- and 15-year-olds who are able and willing to work could enter employment or a paid apprenticeship earlier. The proposal could be especially attractive to young people who prefer practical training to full-time education, and an apprenticeship may develop skills that raise their productivity. This could increase the number of people available to Scottish employers.

However, leaving school earlier may reduce young people’s qualifications and skills, making them less productive or limiting the jobs they can do. Some may not be ready or willing to work, so the increase in effective labour supply could be smaller than the number of extra potential workers suggests. Raising the retirement age from 66 to 67 in 2028 may also keep some older workers in employment and increase labour supply without reducing young people’s education.

Lowering the school-leaving age could be an effective way to expand labour supply if there are enough suitable apprenticeships and protections for young workers. But it is not necessarily the most likely or best method: its success depends on participation and training quality, while encouraging later retirement may add experienced workers with less risk of reducing qualifications. The relative importance of labour shortages, worker skills and available jobs will determine which approach contributes more.

### (d) [6]

The PPC shows the maximum combinations of rice and sugar cane that can be produced with the economy’s resources and technology. Moving from A to B raises rice output from R1 to R2 but reduces sugar-cane output from S1 to S2. If resources are fully employed on the PPC, more land, labour or other resources must be transferred from sugar-cane production to rice production. The decrease in sugar cane is therefore the opportunity cost of producing more rice. Without an outward shift in the PPC, the economy cannot increase both outputs at once using the same resources.

### (e) [9]

Technology can raise shop efficiency by allowing more output or customer service to be provided with fewer resources. Amazon Go’s cameras and sensors record products that shoppers take, so customers can leave without waiting at a checkout. Faster shopping can improve convenience and allow a shop to serve more customers in a given time. In-store notifications may also help customers find products more quickly. If technology substitutes for some checkout labour, the shop may reduce staffing costs.

However, installing and maintaining AI cameras and sensors can be expensive. Technical failures could lead to incorrect charges or disrupt sales, while some customers may be uncomfortable with being monitored. Technology may also reduce employment for checkout workers. Other methods—such as better staff training, store layout or improved stock management—could raise efficiency at lower cost or without relying on complex systems.

Technology is likely to improve efficiency where the savings in time and labour exceed installation, maintenance and error costs, and where customers accept the system. But it is not automatically the best method for every shop: the outcome depends on the shop’s scale, costs, customer preferences and whether less costly management improvements could achieve similar gains.

## Question 4

### (a) [2]

At $50, quantity supplied is 50 units and quantity demanded is 20 units. Excess supply = 50 − 20 = **30 units**.

### (b) [6]

The sunglasses market may be described as a monopoly because the Luxottica Group is the dominant producer and controls more than 80% of eyewear production, directly or indirectly. Its ownership of major brands such as Oakley, Ray-Ban and Vogue means that apparently different brands may belong to the same group rather than competing independently. This can give Luxottica considerable market power to influence price and product choice. The high technology and distribution costs described as barriers to entry make it difficult for new firms to challenge the group, helping it retain its dominant position. Strictly, the extent to which it is a monopoly depends on how the market is defined and the degree of competition from other producers, but the high share and entry barriers support describing it as monopoly-like.

### (c) [9]

A subsidy for bus and tram travel lowers the cost to passengers. Cluj-Napoca’s Green Friday offer makes public transport free on Fridays, encouraging some residents to switch from private cars. If fewer people drive, traffic congestion and pollution may fall. Since pollution imposes external costs on people who did not choose to travel by car, reducing car use can move the market closer to the socially efficient level. The scheme may be particularly relevant in a city of about 335,000 people with many students, who may be more willing to use public transport.

However, the subsidy has a cost to the government or transport provider, and the benefit depends on how many people change their behaviour. If most free-ride users would have taken the bus anyway, the subsidy may have little effect on car use while still reducing fare revenue. It applies only on Fridays, so traffic and pollution on other days may remain. More frequent or reliable services, congestion charges, parking controls or emissions regulations may be needed alongside a subsidy.

The subsidy can be effective when it attracts people away from private transport and public transport has enough capacity to carry them. Its success should be judged by the actual reduction in car journeys and pollution relative to its cost, rather than by free journeys alone. It is a useful part of a wider policy, but by itself may not deal fully with the external costs of private transport.
