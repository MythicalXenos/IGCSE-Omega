### (h) [9]
Automated Parking Systems (APS) could reduce the shortage because they stack cars vertically and use land more intensively than a conventional car park. If a city can fit more vehicles into a limited area, the number of parking spaces supplied rises. This shifts the supply of parking to the right; with demand unchanged, the excess demand at the previous price should shrink, and the market-clearing price may fall. The evidence that APS parks take up less space and cost less to build suggests that the technology could make additional capacity more practical in crowded city centres.

However, the shortage may not disappear. If the number of vehicles entering city centres grows faster than APS capacity, demand can continue to rise and remain above supply. There may also be a substantial initial shortage, so a modest increase in spaces would reduce but not eliminate it. Building APS requires time and investment, meaning the supply response may be delayed; moreover, the technology only helps drivers who can and will use these facilities. If motorists distrust automated systems, or if access, location or operating capacity is unsuitable, the effective increase in supply will be smaller than the theoretical number of spaces.

Overall, APS can reduce the shortage where land is scarce because it increases the number of cars that can be accommodated per unit of land and may be cheaper to build than traditional multi-storey provision. The extent depends on how rapidly APS capacity is installed relative to growth in car use, and on whether the facilities are accessible and accepted. It is likely to ease rather than automatically remove a city-wide shortage.

### (d) [6]
Canadian firms were looking for people with specialist skills for positions such as computer engineers and web designers, but the supply of suitably qualified workers may have been too small. If firms cannot recruit the workers they need, vacancies can remain unfilled and projects may be delayed or output constrained. The extract says a government programme, the Global Talent Stream, gives firms a faster route to hire highly skilled foreign workers, suggesting that some firms could not find enough suitable labour locally.

The extract also says high costs and long time commitments often stop Canadians from training. These barriers reduce the number of domestic workers who acquire the specialist qualifications employers require, so the supply of skilled labour grows slowly even when firms have vacancies. A firm might instead recruit from abroad through the programme, but it must pay a processing fee for each new employee. That adds to recruitment costs; although firms welcomed the faster route, the fee and time needed to recruit can still make filling specialist positions more difficult.

### (e) [9]
Education and training can increase productivity by improving workers’ knowledge and skills. School, college or workplace training relevant to Canadian firms can raise output per worker and reduce mistakes or wasted materials. The evidence says Canada’s labour productivity growth was lower than that of other leading economies for decades, but improved markedly from 2011 and became the third highest among 16 leading economies. Improving human capital could help explain or sustain such progress and may strengthen international competitiveness by lowering unit costs or improving product quality.

Training can also make employees more adaptable to new machinery and methods. If workers understand how to use technology effectively, investment in equipment may generate a larger productivity gain. Training may improve job satisfaction and motivation as well; if workers then produce more per hour and are less likely to leave, firms can avoid repeated recruitment and induction costs.

Nevertheless, education and training may not be the best method in every case. It can be expensive for a firm or government, and firms risk paying to train employees who later move to another employer. The benefits may take years to emerge, particularly when education is provided to young people before they enter work. Some productivity gains may be achieved faster through investment in machinery, automation or better management, especially in industries where routine tasks can be mechanised. Conversely, in services or specialist work, skilled labour may be more important than additional machinery. Training also raises productivity only if its quality is adequate and matches the skills employers actually need.

On balance, education and training are likely to be most effective when Canadian firms face a genuine skills gap and the training is closely matched to their technology and work. It is not universally the “best” route: the size and timing of the productivity gain, the cost and the nature of the industry matter. A combination of suitable training with capital investment and effective work organisation may produce a stronger result than relying on training alone.

## Question 4

### (a) [2]
At a price of $50, quantity supplied = 50 and quantity demanded = 20.

Excess supply = quantity supplied − quantity demanded = **50 − 20 = 30 goods**.

### (b) [6]
Collusion occurs when firms cooperate rather than compete independently. Online sellers of electrical equipment could agree to set prices for products such as laptops and games consoles above the level that would prevail under competition. Consumers may be able to search many sellers online, but if those sellers coordinate their prices, comparing deals will not reveal a genuinely lower competitive offer. Buyers would then pay more, reducing their real purchasing power and potentially preventing some from buying.

Colluding sellers might also restrict the quantity or availability of particular products. If consumers see fewer offers or believe an item may soon be unavailable, they may feel pressure to purchase quickly and have less opportunity to wait for a better deal. Higher profits from collusion could also make it harder for new sellers to enter, weakening competition over time. Thus, online access to a wide range of listings does not necessarily protect consumers if the sellers coordinate their conduct.

### (c) [12]
An oligopoly is a market dominated by a small number of large firms. In the Netherlands supermarket market, Albert Heijn, Super Unie and Jumbo together account for 83.5% of the market, so decisions by one of these businesses may have a substantial effect on the others. Their large scale can create economies of scale: for example, they may buy stock in bulk and spread distribution and advertising costs across many stores. Lower average costs can increase profit or give these firms resources to invest in price offers, loyalty schemes and store improvements, reinforcing their position.

The firms may therefore compete actively to attract customers, but not only through price. Supermarkets can differentiate through product range, quality, own-brand goods, opening hours, convenience, advertising and loyalty rewards. Because the main firms are interdependent, a large price cut by one may prompt rivals to respond, potentially reducing profit for all. They may prefer non-price competition to avoid a costly price war. Their market power and brand recognition can also create barriers to entry, making it difficult for a new supermarket to reach the scale needed to compete nationally.

However, a dominant position does not guarantee lasting success. If one supermarket invests in a successful advertising campaign or improves convenience, customers may switch from rivals and reduce their market shares. This encourages innovation and continued attention to consumer preferences. Smaller firms can also survive by specialising or serving a niche that the largest chains do not serve well; the “Others” category in the figure shows that the market is not made up solely of the three largest businesses. Changes in shopping habits, including online grocery shopping, could also shift competitive advantage toward firms that provide effective digital ordering and delivery. If dominant firms fail to adapt, they may lose customers despite their current scale.

Overall, competing in this oligopoly is likely to influence the large supermarkets to protect their market positions through economies of scale, branding and sustained non-price competition, while remaining alert to each other’s decisions. Market concentration gives the leading firms advantages and can make entry difficult, but it does not remove rivalry or guarantee dominance. How strongly a firm benefits depends on whether its cost advantages and response to changing consumer preferences outweigh competitors’ innovations and the opportunities available to smaller or online-focused firms.
