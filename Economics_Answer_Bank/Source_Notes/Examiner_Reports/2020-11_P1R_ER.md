## Item-specific observations

### Question 1
- **1(c), total revenue:** Many candidates correctly referred to money received and sales of output. A two-mark “what is meant by” definition needs both components; examples do not replace a definition.
- **1(d), economic-growth cause:** Generally well answered. “State” needs one concise cause.
- **1(e), external benefit:** Generally good understanding; define the term rather than giving an example.
- **1(f), tour-guide excess supply:** Full marks were available for total excess supply of **1,725**, or for correctly calculating the separate monthly excesses (800, 550 and 375). Show working.
- **1(g), birth-rate demand diagram:** Label the demand shift and both new equilibrium values; do not shift both curves.
- **1(h), Connect Airways takeover:** Candidates offered customer benefits such as improved quality or cheaper prices, but some did not apply the point to the context. Develop one benefit and make its connection to passengers explicit.
- **1(i), adverse weather and supply:** Some answers stopped at an unexplained inward shift. Develop the effect of weather on supply and the resulting market outcome; do not add a counterargument to an “analyse” item.

### Question 2
- **2(c), fixed costs:** Some candidates selected the right costs but omitted the required currency unit. Use **Rs/Rupees**.
- **2(d), demand:** Definitions often omitted either “willing” or “able.” Give the full definition and refer to a given price (and/or time).
- **2(e), negative-externality policy:** Taxation was a suitable policy, but many did not develop how it addresses the externality. A “describe” answer needs development.
- **2(f), irrigation and productivity:** Understanding of irrigation was often weak. Explain how extra water enables more output from the resources used, in the Scottish-farming context.
- **2(g), Lanterns’ aims:** Responses sometimes described the data rather than assessing whether aims should change. Use a developed, balanced argument applied to the café. A conclusion is not required for “assess.”

### Question 3
- **3(c), labour-supply diagram:** A rightward supply shift was expected. Some candidates shifted both curves or the wrong curve; label the new wage and employment equilibrium clearly.
- **3(d), collusion:** Understanding was reasonable, but development and thorough analysis were common weaknesses. “Analyse” is one-sided; develop the consumer effects in context.
- **3(e), Cambodian roads:** Some answers gave a reasonable two-sided case, but many lacked enough knowledge or development. Balance the benefits and costs of public investment; no conclusion is required for “assess.”

### Question 4
- **4(a), PPF opportunity cost:** The correct cost was **30 units of consumer goods**. Show the working and units.
- **4(b), diseconomies of scale:** Stronger answers explained how growth creates inefficiency; some merely described the term. Develop the link from the firm’s size/management issues to higher costs.
- **4(c), YED and Amazon Prime Video:** A balanced discussion needed to use the supplied Japanese market information. Descriptive or generic YED statements alone were weaker; “evaluate” requires a supported judgement.

## General examiner guidance

The report says candidates sometimes copied extract material without analysing it. Use source evidence as context for a causal explanation. “Analyse” needs developed one-sided reasoning; “assess” needs a developed balance but does not require a conclusion; “evaluate” requires a supported conclusion. Show workings, units and clear diagram labels. These observations apply to the local November 2020 01R report only; its placeholder publication code is not invented here.
