## Overview (PDF p. 3; summary p. 51)

Pearson found that stronger responses connected economic knowledge to the
specific evidence provided; generic explanations and superficial use of the
extract limited performance. Candidates should follow command words, integrate
rather than copy extract evidence, label diagrams fully, observe requested
rounding and units, and show calculation workings. The report reiterates the
stable distinctions: analyse is one-sided; assess is balanced without a required
judgement; evaluate is balanced and requires a supported judgement. Definitions
are not a useful opening unless the question asks for one.

## Question 1

- **1(c), productivity (PDF pp. 4–5):** two marks required two parts: the amount
  of output and its relationship to input. Examples did not earn marks. The
  illustrated full-mark response referred to total output in a given time.
- **1(d), state a formula (pp. 6–7):** one accurate, concise response was enough
  for the one available mark; lengthy answers were unnecessary.
- **1(e), define producer (pp. 8–9):** a correct definition earned the mark; no
  further explanation was needed.
- **1(f), total cost (pp. 10–11):** the correct result was €147,500. Candidates
  were advised to show workings and use the correct currency unit.
- **1(g), supply-and-demand diagram for cars (pp. 12–13):** show and label the
  leftward supply shift and the new, higher equilibrium price and lower
  equilibrium quantity. Missing curve or equilibrium labels cost marks.
- **1(h), CMA intervention in Microsoft/Activision (pp. 14–15):** the three
  marks rewarded a relevant reason, development, and application to context.
  The report’s example credits preventing a monopoly, linking Microsoft’s
  40–60% gaming-market share to protecting other firms, and limiting consumer
  exploitation. An explain response needs a developed, contextual effect—not a
  definition of the key term.
- **1(i), labour demand (pp. 16–18):** analyse requires a one-sided argument;
  no evaluation marks are available. The full-mark example develops the
  derived-demand link from increased demand for furniture to increased demand
  for woodcutters. A counterargument is unnecessary.

## Question 2

- **2(c), PES calculation (pp. 19–20):** some candidates missed the requested
  two decimal places and lost a mark. Show workings.
- **2(d), invention (pp. 21–22):** a two-mark “what is meant by” response needed
  the commercial exploitation of a new invention. Examples alone did not earn
  marks.
- **2(e), describe (pp. 23–24):** one reason/feature earns the first mark; the
  second is for development. The report’s example identifies labour and its
  role in fixing cars.
- **2(f), privatisation (pp. 25–26):** only one effect/reason/way is credited;
  marks reward context and development. Defining privatisation was unnecessary.
- **2(g), 9-mark assess (pp. 27–30):** stronger answers used both sides, developed
  chains of reasoning and integrated evidence. Copying the extract is not
  application. The illustrated Level 3 answer earned 9/9; assess requires
  balanced analysis, but no conclusion or judgement.

## Question 3

- **3(c), labour-market diagram (pp. 31–32):** label the new wage rate, quantity
  of labour demanded and quantity of labour supplied. Full, accurate labels are
  required for all marks.
- **3(d), analyse Tennis TV’s demand curve (pp. 33–36):** candidates needed to
  address the demand curve named in the question and apply evidence. Analyse is
  one-sided; develop applied chains rather than listing points. The report’s
  example earned 6/6.
- **3(e), assess subsidies and externalities (pp. 37–39):** top responses gave a
  balanced assessment and used evidence from the data. Assess requires a
  developed two-sided argument; a conclusion/judgement is not required. The
  illustrated response earned 9/9.

## Question 4

- **4(a), percentage change in profit (pp. 40–41):** 1.18% was the correct
  two-mark result. Show the full calculation; workings can earn a method mark
  where the final answer or rounding is wrong. Observe units and decimal-place
  instructions.
- **4(b), oligopoly in Uganda’s petrol market (pp. 42–45):** some candidates
  copied extract material instead of applying it to explain why the market was
  an oligopoly. The Level 3 example earned 6/6 through clear knowledge,
  appropriate application, selectivity and developed analysis.
- **4(c), 12-mark evaluate (pp. 46–50):** top-level responses integrated evidence
  into a balanced argument; merely restating it limited marks. The illustrated
  answer earned 12/12 and included strong applied analysis, a balanced counter-
  argument and a supported judgement. Evaluate requires the judgement in the
  conclusion.

## Practical takeaways

1. Convert evidence into a causal link; do not quote or paraphrase it without
   explaining why it matters to the question.
2. Match the command word: one-sided developed analysis for **analyse**;
   developed balance for **assess**; developed balance plus a supported final
   judgement for **evaluate**.
3. For short responses, answer the asked task directly: definitions only when
   requested, one developed point where the tariff calls for one, and no
   unnecessary padding.
4. For quantitative work, show method, use correct units, and follow precision
   instructions. For diagrams, label every required curve and equilibrium
   value.
